### Automating Inequality (Virginia Eubanks, 2018)

In Allegheny County, Pennsylvania, there is a room where a telephone rings: someone reports that a child may be in danger. As the hotline worker listens, a number slowly surfaces on the screen — from 1 to 20. This is the "risk score" produced by the Allegheny Family Screening Tool, an algorithm that has ground through more than a hundred variables from state databases: whether the family applied for benefits, whether anyone was treated for addiction under Medicaid, whether the father was under the supervision of the probation service. The higher the number, the more insistently the system advises opening a case. Virginia Eubanks, who came there as a researcher, noticed a simple and terrible thing: nearly all the variables that feed the algorithm are traces of poverty. A rich family treats addiction in a private clinic and leaves no digital trace. A poor one leaves a trace. And the system reads poverty as a danger to the child.

From this observation grew the book "Automating Inequality: How High-Tech Tools Profile, Police, and Punish the Poor" — and its central image, the **digital poorhouse**. Eubanks draws a historical line: in the nineteenth century, poorhouses were built for the poor — institutions that herded people in so as to help and punish them at once, to feed and shame them, and to separate the "deserving" poor from the "undeserving." The poorhouse was never about the efficiency of handing out bread; it was about moral sorting and containment. Eubanks argues that automated welfare systems are the same poorhouse, only built out of databases, scoring models, and matching algorithms. It is invisible and has no address, but it does the same work: it monitors, ranks, cuts off aid — and does all of this under the banner of objectivity.

The book is built on three detailed cases, and its strength is that this is not abstraction but reportage with names and lives. **Indiana**: in 2006 the state signed a $1.16 billion contract to automate the processing of benefit applications. Human caseworkers who knew their districts were replaced by call centers and a system that treated any hitch — a lost document, a missed call — as a "failure to cooperate" and cut off aid. Over three years, more than a million applications were denied. Eubanks tells of Omega Young, a woman whose Medicaid was cut off while she was fighting cancer; she died, and the day after her death she won her appeal: benefits reinstated.

**Los Angeles**: the coordinated entry system for homeless services, which gathers the most intimate data — about drugs, sex, mental health, arrests — in order to "match" a person with housing. But there is almost no housing, and in practice the system turns into a registry of the vulnerable, to which the police have access.

**Allegheny** — that very child-welfare algorithm, the most humane in intent and therefore the most insidious: it was designed by people of good will, passed an ethics review, and all the same encodes class inequality into the very metric of risk.

For the AI-safety canon, the concept shifts the focus: while the industry argued about a future superintelligence, Eubanks showed harm already being done by a technology tested on the powerless. The rich are not subjected to algorithmic screening of their fitness as parents. New systems are broken in where people can neither refuse, nor sue, nor be heard — on benefit recipients, the homeless, the poor. This overturns the naive picture of "first for the best, then for everyone": in fact it is first for the most vulnerable, in a regime where failure costs the developer nothing.

**How it came about.** Eubanks is a political scientist, a professor at the University at Albany, but above all a longtime activist who worked with women from working-class neighborhoods on questions of technology and rights. The book's spur was a personal episode she describes in the introduction: her partner was beaten up, and soon afterward the insurance company suspended payment on their claims, which had apparently been flagged by an automated algorithm for detecting "suspicious" patterns. Eubanks, a middle-class person, suddenly felt firsthand the logic under which the poor live constantly. "Automating Inequality" was published by St. Martin's Press in January 2018, the same year as Safiya Noble's "Algorithms of Oppression," riding the wave raised by Cathy O'Neil's "Weapons of Math Destruction" (2016). The book won the Lillian Smith Book Award and the McGannon Center Book Prize and quickly became one of the load-bearing texts of the critical-humanities wing of the conversation about algorithms.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The objections run along three lines. The first is methodological: three in-depth case studies give a vivid picture, but this is not a representative sample; one can object that from vivid accounts of failure one cannot conclude that automation harms *on average*. The second concerns the Allegheny algorithm: its creators, above all Rhema Vaithianathan and Emily Putnam-Hornstein, publicly answered Eubanks that the discretion of human caseworkers is also biased through and through — racially and by class — and that a transparent, verifiable algorithm can at least be audited, whereas a person's bias is hidden in their head. This is a strong counterargument: the comparison should be with the real alternative, not the ideal.

The third line is that Eubanks in places verges on technological determinism in reverse: she shows so convincingly how the tool reproduces oppression that she almost sets aside the question of whether these systems simply mirrored a political decision already taken — to starve the poor. The problem, her opponents say, is not in the code but in the budget and in politics, and the risk is that criticism of the algorithm will become a convenient lightning rod, diverting attention from money and power. Eubanks herself, however, largely asserts this too — that technology merely lends old cruelty the look of inevitability.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Eubanks is no Luddite, and she isn't demanding that we throw out our computers. Her practical contribution is an "oath of non-harm" for the designers of such systems and two simple test questions: does the tool increase the self-determination and capabilities of the poor? and — would it pass if it were aimed not at the poor but at everyone? If a child-screening system cannot be imagined as applied to middle-class families, then it is not about children's safety but about the surveillance of poverty. Hence several ideas still alive in the field today: a right to explanation and to contest an automated decision; disparate-impact auditing before, not after, deployment; the participation of affected communities in the design; and a sober recognition that "removing the human from the loop" for the sake of savings almost always strikes those with the fewest levers.

For the reader of the "Bestiary," this concept is an antidote to a narrow technical view of alignment: it reminds us that the question "whose values does the system serve and on whom is it broken in" is asked not in the future and not in the laboratory, but already — in the line for benefits. The digital poorhouse is not a metaphor for a coming dystopian AI; it is worth understanding as reconnaissance-in-force: where the poor are cut off today, tomorrow the tested mechanisms of surveillance and sorting will spread to everyone.
