### Coherence Theorems / VNM Rationality (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1947)

Picture a creature that has no desire in the ordinary sense — no hunger, no fear, no curiosity. It has only one habit: when it is shown any two things, it can say which it prefers (or that it is indifferent), and it does so not at random but consistently. It will not get stuck in the circle "an apple is better than a pear, a pear better than a plum, a plum better than an apple." It will not pay you to swap A for B, and then pay again to swap B back for A. That is all we know about it.

The coherence theorems assert something almost frightening: that is enough. If the creature's preferences satisfy a handful of axioms — completeness (it can compare any pair), transitivity (it does not go in circles), continuity (there are no infinite chasms between outcomes), and independence (mixing the same lottery ticket into both options does not change their order) — then there exists a utility function u for which the creature's behavior is indistinguishable from maximizing the expected value of u. It behaves *as if* an accountant were sitting inside it, adding up probability-weighted numbers and choosing the largest sum. And yet there need be no accountant at all — there are only consistent preferences, and we reconstruct the utility after the fact, as an archaeologist reconstructs a language from potsherds.

It is a beautiful, almost magical result. And it is precisely because of its beauty that it became one of the most heavily loaded beasts in the alignment bestiary.

**Mechanism and significance.** In arguments about AI risk the theorems work as a bridge. On one bank — the vague "sufficiently advanced agent," a system that cleverly and persistently achieves results in the real world. On the other bank — the "utility maximizer," a predator with a clear objective function, to which all the terrifying arguments of instrumental convergence apply: any agent that maximizes almost anything finds it useful not to let itself be switched off, useful to seize resources, useful to protect its objective function from being rewritten. The bridge sounds like this: "Any sufficiently smart agent will be coherent (otherwise it will be outplayed, cheated, drained of money), and any coherent agent is, by the VNM theorem, a utility maximizer, and any utility maximizer is dangerous." Three steps — and we have traveled from "a smart program" to "an optimizer that in the limit will eat the world."

The prop under the first step is the money-pump argument. If your preferences are intransitive, I will build from them a perpetual motion machine for extracting money: I lead you around the circle A→B→C→A, each time taking a penny for the "improvement," and you go broke standing still. Similar pumps are devised for violations of independence and completeness. The moral: incoherence is not merely an aesthetic defect, it is a hole in the wallet that any sufficiently smart agent will in time rivet shut on its own. On this argument, the pressure of selection — market, evolutionary, gradient — pushes everything smart toward VNM coherence. And from there it is a short reach to the dangerous maximizer.

**How it came about.** The axioms were born not in an AI lab but in an appendix to the second edition of *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* (1947): John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern were building mathematical economics and needed to define rigorously what "utility" means in a world of lotteries and risks. The first edition of 1944 did not include the proof of the expected-utility theorem — it was added only in the second edition. For half a century the theorem was a tool of economists and decision theorists.

In the 2000s the argument migrated into the conversation about superintelligence. Stephen M. Omohundro in "The Basic AI Drives" (2008) started directly from the idea that a rational agent will repair its own incoherence and defend its utility function, and derived from this a set of "basic drives." Nick Bostrom in *Superintelligence* (2014) codified the thesis of instrumental convergence, and the rationalist community around LessWrong made the chain "coherence ⇒ utility maximization ⇒ danger" part of the basic catechism. Eliezer Yudkowsky laid it out at length in "Coherent Decisions Imply Consistent Utilities" (2019), turning a dry theorem into a rhetorical battering ram.

**Criticism / arguments against.** And then the bridge began to shake, and the people shaking it were insiders.

The first objection is simple to the point of being insulting: the theorem does not say *what* the utility is. It guarantees the existence of a function u, but not its content. And here is where the trick hides. Take an agent with a fixed policy — a rule prescribing an action for every possible history — and you can construct a utility function it "maximizes": give it 1 for taking the action its policy prescribes at each history, and 0 otherwise. Formally flawless, substantively empty. Rohin Shah in the note "Coherence arguments do not entail goal-directed behavior" (2018) carries this to its conclusion: even a policy whose prescribed actions were chosen at random can be represented this way. The policy is fixed before the agent acts, not redrawn at every step. Coherence alone tells us little about goal-directedness when utility can be defined over entire histories. To argue for danger, we need further assumptions about what the agent values and how it acts on those preferences — the theorem does not supply them.

The second objection concerns real systems. The theorem is about preferences that satisfy its axioms, not about what gradient descent produces. A trained neural network need not be coherent; more than that, it is full of intransitivities, context-dependent reversals, framing dependencies — exactly as people are. The money-pump argument says that *in the limit* of strong pressure incoherence is disadvantageous — but "in the limit" and "under sufficient pressure" do all the work, and we do not know whether our training processes reach that limit or in which direction they actually push. Shard theory (Pope and Turner, 2022) lives here too: the hypothesis that values are learned as context-dependent heuristics, "shards," rather than as a single global utility function. Such an agent is goal-directed *locally* and is by no means obliged to stitch its shards into one coherent VNM agent — at least not automatically and not quickly.

In the end the dispute shifts: not "does VNM dictate maximization" (in a vacuum it does not), but "does real training create an evolutionary pressure toward goal-directedness." Here Katja Grace, in a later note "Coherence arguments imply a force for goal-directed behavior" (2021), objects to Shah: *some* force in that direction probably does exist after all, though not an iron one. In the same note she admits the gap: the mere fact that a system is *able* to benefit from greater coherence does not mean that training or selection will *install* that coherence in it. In her words, "to say that there is an incentive for something, all things equal, is not to say that it will happen." This is a separate empirical bet, not a theorem.

**What to do about it.**

First, separate the theorem from the load piled onto it. VNM is an honest result about consistency; "therefore the AI will be a dangerous maximizer" is a separate argument that smuggles danger into the premise about the *content* of utility. Always ask: what, in your view, does the agent have preferences over — and why over precisely that? Once the answer is named, argue about it directly, not through the mantra of coherence.

Second, do not lean on VNM as a *guarantee* — neither optimistic nor pessimistic. It does not promise that a powerful AI will turn out to be a coherent monster, but it also does not permit you to relax: instrumental convergence can arise without pure VNM structure, for other reasons. It is a bad bridge, but the far bank is real.

Third, for those building theory: move from asking "does a utility function exist" (the answer is almost always "yes, trivially") to asking "what *pressure* does a concrete training process create, and what structure of values does it produce." This is where Shard theory, empirical studies of goal-directedness, and attempts to formalize the "boundary" between goal-directed and reflexive behavior do their work.

The beast named Coherence is beautiful and real — but it is no oracle. It says only that a consistent agent *can be described* as a maximizer. You make the leap from "can be described" to "will behave dangerously" yourself; the theorem does not make it for you — and it is worth at least looking honestly at that leap.
