### Ontological crisis (Peter de Blanc, 2011)

Picture an agent you have charged with protecting diamonds. Not an abstract "good," not "the happiness of mankind" — simply diamonds: hard, transparent, faceted stones that must not be scratched, crushed, or dissolved in acid. The task seems supremely clear precisely because it is narrow. And so the agent, faithfully carrying it out, begins to study the world — and one day comes to realize that no "stones" exist at the fundamental level. There are carbon atoms in a tetrahedral lattice; there are electron densities; there are, if you dig deeper, excitations of quantum fields, in which the word "diamond" means precisely nothing. The question on which the whole alignment problem rests sounds deceptively simple: **what do you now do with a value that was pinned to a concept that has just dissolved beneath your feet?**

Peter de Blanc called this an ontological crisis. An agent's values are always formulated in terms of its model of the world — its ontology, its list of what exists at all and what is connected to what. "Maximize human happiness" presupposes that there are "people" and there is "happiness." "Do not damage diamonds" presupposes that there are "diamonds." But the model of the world is not a granite slab; it is a living, ever-deepening construct. The agent learns. Its ontology is rewritten: what seemed a basic, indivisible entity turns out to be composite, approximate, and sometimes — outright fictitious. And the value nailed to the old concept is left hanging in the air.

The mechanism here is subtler than "the agent got confused." A utility function is a mapping from states of the world to numbers. But it reads the states of the world through the model, not directly. Change the model — and the function's domain changes. If the new ontology is richer and contains the old one as a special case, transfer is still possible: "diamond" is redescribed as "a connected region of carbon in such-and-such a lattice," and the value neatly relocates to the new address. But what if the old concept has no clear referent at all in the new picture? The history of science is littered with such casualties: caloric, phlogiston, the vital force, the ether, the celestial spheres. These were not merely mistaken facts — they were entities around which whole systems of explanation were built, and when the ontology changed they were not "refined," they vanished. If an agent's value rested on such an entity, it has nowhere to go. It does not become false — it becomes **undefined**. And with an undefined utility function, an agent's behavior can no longer be predicted: it may fill in the gaps however it likes, including in ways that would strike us as bizarre.

A human being knows this from the inside. A crisis of faith just is an ontological crisis: the picture of the world on which morality rested collapses, and the person discovers that the commandments were formulated in terms to which they can no longer attach meaning. "Do not anger God" — but what if God is no longer in the ontology? Usually we do not go mad and start killing; we somehow carry the value over — onto human dignity, onto suffering, onto something that seems to us an invariant across the shifting pictures. But note: nobody has formally described this "somehow." We do not know the rule by which we perform the transfer. And this is exactly the rule we lack — the one we would need to write into the machine.

**How it came about.** De Blanc's paper "Ontological Crises in Artificial Agents' Value Systems" appeared as a preprint in 2011 (arXiv:1105.3821) and was one of the first attempts to grasp the problem formally. He considered an agent whose utility is defined over one probabilistic model of the world, and asked: how do you carry it over to another, finer model? His technical answer relied on the idea of a mapping between ontologies that preserves probabilistic structure — but de Blanc himself marked out the limits: this works cleanly only in a narrow class of cases. From there, MIRI (the Machine Intelligence Research Institute) picked up the baton. In Eliezer Yudkowsky's work the task was reformulated as the "ontology identification problem" — how to make an agent recognize the target objects within its own internal model, unknown to us in advance; and it is there that the textbook "diamond maximizer" lives. The problem entered MIRI's technical agenda through the work of Nate Soares and Benya Fallenstein on aligning superintelligence and through "The Value Learning Problem" (Soares, 2016), where ontological shift is named as one of the reasons why one cannot simply "hardwire" values once and for all.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and chief one is John Wentworth's natural abstraction hypothesis: reality may be so arranged that a broad class of agents studying the same world converge on the same high-level abstractions. "Tree," "pressure," "organism" are not arbitrary labels but objectively distinguished joints of nature, toward which any sufficiently smart observer converges. If that is so, then "diamond" will survive quantum mechanics no worse than "temperature" survived the molecular-kinetic theory — the concept will not vanish but will acquire a reductionist foundation. The second objection is empirical: human values have de facto survived the Copernican, Darwinian, and quantum revolutions. People have changed their ontology radically — and for the most part did not turn into monsters. That means value transfer is possible, and perhaps the problem is milder than the theoretical panic suggests. The third is methodological: de Blanc's formal models are limited, and the problem they describe may be an artifact of a particular formalization rather than a deep difficulty. Finally, there is a dispute about the very status of the thing: is this a practical engineering problem we will run into, or theoretical exotica that will never arise in real systems?

Natural abstractions are a hypothesis, not a theorem; they may hold for "tree" and collapse precisely where it matters most to us — for "person," "consent," "suffering," "identity." Value-laden concepts are exactly the ones that are hardest to reduce — and exactly the ones on which a superintelligence that has outgrown our ontology is most likely to part ways with us. And human resilience to shifts in the picture of the world may have rested on the fact that our revisions were minor compared with the leap that will be made by an entity that conceives the world several levels deeper than we do.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The ontological crisis is not a separate beast but a knot in which half the menagerie converges. It is the mechanism behind the fragility of value: if the target concept loses its referent, optimization for it careens off into the unknown, and an "almost right" goal yields not an "almost right" world. It buttresses the argument for corrigibility: since we do not know how to specify values so that they stably survive a deepening of the world model, it is wiser to build an agent that admits correction and does not resist having its goal redefined. And it sits directly next to the symbol grounding problem — how the symbol "diamond" latches onto the world at all — and to the task of ELK (Eliciting Latent Knowledge): how to extract an honest answer to a question framed in our concepts from an agent's internal model that is alien to us, if the agent itself already conceives the world in other categories. Nobody has a practical recipe that "carries" an arbitrary value through an arbitrary ontological shift. There are directions: to seek out and lean on natural abstractions where they exist; to teach values not as rigid formulas but as procedures sensitive to context; and — most sober of all — not to pretend that we have set the goal once and for all, since the ontology in which we set it will almost certainly turn out to be childish by the standards of the agent we are building.

We can tell the machine what to value only in the words of our present-day picture of the world. And the machine we are making will outgrow that picture — and at the moment when our words lose their former meaning for it, we will discover whether we built into it a way to carry the value tenderly onto new ground — or left it hanging in a void, to be filled in any which way.
