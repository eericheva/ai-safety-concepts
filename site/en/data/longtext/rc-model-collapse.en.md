### Ouroboros (Shumailov et al., 2023)

The serpent biting its own tail — and hidden in this image is a cruel pun. Because model collapse begins precisely with the tails: not the metaphorical tail of a reptile, but the tails of a distribution — rare words, unusual faces, strange sentences, small communities, exotic events. Train a generative model. Have it generate text. Train the next model on that text. On its outputs — a third. And so on, generation after generation. After a few iterations, this is what happens: first the outskirts quietly vanish from the data, then the half-tones, and in the limit there remains a smooth, self-assured, endlessly self-repeating "average," which by now bears almost no relation to the reality that gave birth to the first model. The serpent has finished its tail and set about the trunk.

Shumailov and co-authors showed this with a toy but mercilessly vivid example. Take a language model, ask it to continue a text, collect the continuations, fine-tune a new version on them — and launch the loop. Already by the ninth generation the model, asked about the architecture of English church towers, was answering with a lengthy treatise about… multicolored jackrabbits with black, white, blue, red, and yellow tails. A stream coherent in form, wholly degenerate in meaning. The same with pictures, with Gaussian mixtures, with any model fed its own reflection: the distribution shrinks, the variance collapses, diversity leaks away.

**The mechanism.** Why is this inevitable, if nothing is done? The authors break the error down into three sources, of which two matter most. The first error is statistical: any sample is finite. When a model generates, say, a million examples, a rare event with a probability of one in ten million simply will not land in the sample. The next generation trains on data where this event is absent entirely — and assigns it a probability of zero. The tail is cut off forever; there is nowhere to resurrect it from. The second error is functional, from limited expressiveness and imperfect approximation: a model is not a perfect mirror, it has its own systematic skews, its own favorite turns of phrase. It distorts, ever so slightly, what it models. At each iteration these distortions do not average out but accumulate and amplify. It is exactly the game of telephone. Each reteller adds their own microscopic shift, and by the twentieth person the phrase "in the forest a fir tree was born" turns into unrecognizable noise. The model stops modeling the true distribution of the data and begins to model, ever more precisely, its own artifacts — a delusion raised to a higher power with every generation.

**Significance.** This would be an amusing laboratory curiosity, were it not for one circumstance: the internet — the main source of training data — is filling up with machine text before our very eyes. Articles, reviews, comments, forum answers, stock images — ever more synthetics, and ever harder to distinguish from human work. Which means the next generation of large models will scrape the same internet and inevitably gorge on the outputs of the previous generation. The loop that is built deliberately in the lab closes by itself out in the world — simply because the models write into the same place from which they later draw. Hence the authors' alarming conclusion and a beautiful economic paradox: the value of data collected before the age of generative AI — "clean," verifiably human corpora — rises sharply. Like steel smelted before the first nuclear tests of 1945 (it is indeed sought out for sensitive detectors, because all postwar steel carries a radioactive background) — so antediluvian, pre-AI text becomes a strategic resource. Whoever managed to download Reddit as it was in 2015 is sitting pretty.

Two different beasts are easily confused here. There is **mode collapse** — when a single model, for example after fine-tuning with reinforcement learning from human feedback, loses the diversity of its answers, always tells the same joke and draws the same face. This is an effect on *one* model, a property of its training or post-training. Model collapse is about something else: about the recursive feedback loop in the *data* itself, between *generations* of models. The first is cured by adjusting the sampling temperature and tweaking the training; the second is a disease of the corpus, not of a single network. The folk name captures the essence more precisely than any term: **Habsburg AI**, by analogy with the royal dynasty that married within itself for centuries and in the end degenerated into the famous chin and infertility. The incest of data yields the same result as the incest of genes.

**How it came about.** The preprint "The Curse of Recursion: Training on Generated Data Makes Models Forget" came out in May 2023 — on the very crest of the post-ChatGPT wave, when the question "aren't we poisoning our own well?" hung in the air. The assembled team was impressive, and its composition was no accident: Ilia Shumailov and Zakhar Shumaylov, Yiren Zhao, Nicolas Papernot, Yarin Gal, and — Ross Anderson, a legend of computer security from Cambridge, author of *Security Engineering*, a man with an instinct for systemic catastrophe. The term "model collapse" caught on instantly; slightly earlier, in February 2023, the researcher Jathan Sadowski had introduced the label "Habsburg AI," and it stuck fast. And in July 2024 the work appeared in Nature under the punchy headline "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data" — a rare honor for a subject barely more than a year old, and a sign that the topic had struck a nerve.

**Criticism and arguments against.** The headline in Nature sounds like a verdict, but the verdict turned out premature. The strongest objection was formulated by Gerstgrasser, Schaeffer, and colleagues in a work with the telling title "Is Model Collapse Inevitable?" Their argument is simple and devastating: at each step, the authors of the original experiment *replaced* real data with synthetic — the old generation was thrown out, only the new was fed in. But in real life no one does that. Data *accumulates*: the human corpus does not go anywhere, synthetics are *added* to it. And if the real data is not thrown out but accumulated together with the artificial, collapse does not set in — the error stops growing like an avalanche and levels off at an upper bound. The curse of recursion, it turns out, is lifted by simple bookkeeping: do not replace, but add.

The second objection is practical. Synthetic data is everywhere and works splendidly: it is used to train distilled models, to cover rare domains where real examples are scarce, and whole families of strong models are trained largely on generated data. So the problem is not synthetic data as such but an uncontrolled closed loop with no human and no filter. Curated synthetic data — with bad samples rejected, quality checks and human feedback — is not poison but medicine. A third objection, subtler, came from Dohmatob and others in "A Tale of Tails": collapse is not a binary catastrophe but a shift in the scaling laws; admixing synthetics changes the return-on-size curve, and the consequences depend on the proportion, the quality, and exactly how the sources are mixed. The picture of the world shifted: not "we are doomed" but "there are regimes in which we are doomed, and regimes in which we are not."

Many demonstrations of collapse are on small models and narrow tasks, with full replacement of the data, without any curation. Such demonstrations prove that a dangerous regime exists; they do not predict the fate of the whole industry.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** First — guard the tails: rare events, minority languages, marginal topics vanish first and most quietly, so these are what must be watched, like a canary in a coal mine. Second — do not close the loop blindly: accumulate human data alongside the synthetic, not instead of it, and keep a human in the loop along with rigorous quality control. Third — learn to tell the machine from the human (watermarks, data provenance), because without this it is impossible either to curate the corpus or to assess how deeply the well is already poisoned. From this grew a whole research topic on autophagy — "self-consuming" generative models, whose "disorder" (Model Autophagy Disorder, MAD) was described by Alemohammad and colleagues: a system feeding on itself without an inflow of fresh data goes mad in the literal technical sense.

The ouroboros is an ancient symbol of eternal return and self-sufficiency. Model collapse inverts its meaning: a loop closed on itself is not self-sufficient but self-destructive. Diversity, rarity, strangeness, the living human tail of the distribution — that is what truly feeds the system. Cut off the inflow of the real — and the magnificent machine will begin, with appetite, to finish itself off, generation after generation, until there remains a smooth, self-satisfied emptiness.
