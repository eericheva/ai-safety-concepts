### Artifacts Have Politics (Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", 1980)

On the south shore of Long Island, on the road to Jones Beach, there still stand overpasses beneath which a tall person feels uncomfortable and a scheduled bus simply cannot pass. The clearance is about three meters, in places less. A car passes; a city bus does not. The legend, retold in 1980 by the philosopher of technology Langdon Winner, has it that this was by design. Robert Moses, the all-powerful builder of New York's parks and parkways, a man who redrew the city without once being elected to any office, allegedly ordered the bridges built low on purpose. The automobile in the 1920s and '30s means the middle class, white and well-off. The bus means the poor, the Black, the immigrant. Lower the overpass by half a meter — and you need neither "whites only" signs, nor police at the entrance, nor a racist law that can be challenged in court. The discrimination is already cast in concrete. It will work silently, around the clock, and will outlive its author by centuries.

Here is Winner's central gesture: he takes an object we consider neutral infrastructure — just a bridge, just a road — and shows that a political decision is sewn into its geometry. Not beside it, not "in the way it is used," but inside, in the very dimensions. The artifact is not indifferent to who will live, and how. It has already voted.

From this observation Winner spins the thesis that became one of the foundations of STS — science and technology studies. Technical devices, he says, are political in two different ways. The first is the "settling" way: a particular thing, or its arrangement, settles a public dispute in someone's favor. Moses's bridges; or the pneumatic molding machines that Cyrus McCormick Jr. installed at his reaper works in the 1880s — machines that produced parts that were worse and more expensive than the parts skilled workers made by hand, but which allowed him to fire qualified union members and break the union itself. Technology here is a weapon in a labor conflict, disguised as progress. The second way is harsher: there are technologies that Winner calls inherently political. They are not merely convenient for a certain order — they require it. The atomic bomb is the exemplary case. Fissile materials cannot be entrusted to the free play of the market and the town meeting; by their very physics they drag behind them guard details, secrecy, hierarchy, paramilitary discipline, a closed caste of those with clearance. As long as there is atomic weaponry on Earth, Winner says, an authoritarian, undemocratic structure will inevitably grow around it — regardless of who is in power. In choosing such a technology, a society also chooses a form of power, often without being aware of it.

Design is the continuation of politics by other means. The designer, the engineer, the architect of a system are not "just solving a technical problem" — they are determining who will find things convenient and who will not; who gets access and who cannot fit beneath the overpass. And the less noticeable this distribution is, the longer it remains effective.

**How it came about.** The essay appeared in 1980 in the journal *Daedalus*, and then became the central chapter of Winner's book "The Whale and the Reactor" (1986). By the end of the 1970s two extremes dominated the philosophy of technology. On one side, technological determinism: technology develops by its own logic and drags society along behind it (close to Jacques Ellul's "autonomous technology"). On the other, naive instrumentalism: a hammer is neutral, all that matters is whether you use it to drive a nail or to bash in your neighbor's head, "it's about the person, not the thing." Winner lays out a third path. Against the instrumentalists he says: no, the arrangement of a thing is not neutral, values and relations of power are already built into it. Against the determinists, he argues that this is not impersonal fate but the result of concrete human decisions, which can be traced, contested, and made otherwise. He drew on the biography of Moses — journalist Robert Caro's giant work "The Power Broker" (1974), from which he took the story of the bridges — and on research into the mechanical tomato harvester developed at the University of California, Davis: the machine required fields of a certain size and tomatoes of a certain variety, and in the end washed the small farmers out of the industry, concentrating the land in the hands of big growers. No one passed a law mandating the ruin of small farms — the harvester "passed" it.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The loudest blow fell on the load-bearing structure — on the bridge story itself. In 1999 the sociologist Bernward Joerges published in *Social Studies of Science* an article with a mirror-image title, "Do Politics Have Artefacts?" He went to sort out the facts and found the legend fragile. Some of the overpasses on the parkways simply conformed to the road standards of the time: commercial transport, buses included, was already forbidden on park roads — regardless of height. Poor New Yorkers reached Jones Beach quite readily — by the Long Island Rail Road, and later by buses on other routes. Moses was a racist and an elitist — that is true, documented in Caro — but to ascribe the magical power of social segregation specifically to the bridge dimensions is to take a beautiful tale for a proven mechanism. Joerges showed that "Moses's bridges" function in scholarship as an urban legend: every author retells the story without checking, because they illustrate the desired point too conveniently. An artifact, per Joerges, "says" nothing by itself — meaning is given to it by the people who interpret it and cite it; the politics is not in the concrete but in our stories about the concrete. In the same issue of the journal, Steve Woolgar and Geoff Cooper answered with the essay "Do Artefacts Have Ambivalence?": yes, the story is factually shaky — but the debunking is not the last word either, and the fact that the case stays this stubbornly ambiguous is itself the lesson for STS.

A more general line of criticism (close to Bruno Latour's actor-network theory) holds that Winner divides the world too cleanly into "politics" and "artifacts" and asks which of them is primary — whereas in fact they are woven into a single fabric, and people and things together form a network in which influence travels in all directions. Finally, the thesis of "inherently political" technologies is charged with determinism through the back door: are we not ascribing to things the inevitability that we ourselves in fact create through our institutions?

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For the "Bestiary," Winner is the ancestor of a whole brood of younger beasts. "Values in design," algorithmic bias, value-sensitive design, value alignment — all of these are direct descendants of the 1980 idea, only Moses's bridge has been replaced by the training set and the loss function. When a face-recognition model is worse at recognizing faces with dark skin, when a scoring algorithm rejects you based on your postal code, when a recommender feed structurally rewards rage — these are the same "low overpasses," only digital: a political decision frozen in the architecture and working silently. The lesson of the Winner–Joerges dispute is twofold, and both halves matter equally. The first: stop treating the technical as "neutral." Ask about any system — who gets to pass comfortably beneath it, and who does not fit the clearance, and whether this was chosen or simply "turned out that way." The second, sobering, from Joerges: don't fall for the beautiful exposé. The convenient story about malice cast into a thing too often turns out to be a legend; the politics of things demands not rhetoric but investigation — who exactly, which decision, with what consequences, and whether it could have been otherwise. For anyone designing AI, both halves add up to a single discipline: to treat the architecture, the data, and the objective function as a political act — and to answer for it as scrupulously as for code that either passes review or does not.
