### Interpretability: how to look inside the black box

Almost the whole previous conversation ran up against the same wall: we judge an AI by how it behaves, but we do not see what is "inside" it. A modern neural network is a black box: we know all its weights down to the last number, yet from the billions of parameters there is no way to read off what the model "thinks" or why it gave that particular answer. Interpretability, or more precisely mechanistic interpretability, is an attempt to crack open the box: to reverse-engineer the model's internal makeup and understand how exactly it computes, not just what it outputs.

A feature is a direction in activation space encoding some meaningful property (say, "text in French" or "a mention of the Golden Gate Bridge"). A circuit is a small subgraph of neurons and connections that jointly implements a specific computation. The trouble is that a model has far more features than neurons: they are stored in superposition, and a single neuron turns out to be polysemantic — responding to several unrelated things at once, which makes the network harder to read. A landmark finding in this field concerns induction heads: attention heads that implement the simple rule "saw [A][B], and now [A] again → predict [B]" and, as Olsson and co-authors suggest (Anthropic, 2022), largely account for models' capacity for in-context learning. To combat polysemanticity, sparse autoencoders (SAEs) were devised: they decompose activations into thousands or millions of more "pure," monosemantic features, and Anthropic's Towards Monosemanticity and Scaling Monosemanticity work is built on them (hence "Golden Gate Claude," a model that could be made to talk obsessively about the bridge by amplifying a single feature).

Among the methods in use are activation patching and path patching (we swap activations and watch what changes), ablations, probing and the logit lens.

If we learn to read what is inside a model, we will be able to check claims about it rather than take them on faith: to catch deception, hidden goals, and dangerous capabilities before they show up in behavior. This is, in essence, one answer to the King Lear problem (Holden Karnofsky's term) and deceptive alignment (Hubinger et al., 2019) that does not rely on behavior: instead of trusting how an AI behaves on tests, we get to look inside its "head." Evan Hubinger's "understanding-based safety evaluations" (2023) allow interpretability as one route to understanding, alongside other training rationales. The goal is to release a model not because it passed behavioral tests, but because we understand how it is built and can see that there are no dangerous mechanisms in it.

Progress so far is encouraging but modest: circuits in small models have been dissected in fair detail, and SAEs already pull meaningful features out of large networks. But full "mind-reading" is a long way off: we read fragments, not the whole mind at once, and we do not yet know how to reliably scale interpretability to a model as a whole. Interpretability has pitfalls of its own too — "interpretability illusions": a beautiful story about what a feature or circuit "does" may turn out to be partial or simply wrong if it is not checked causally (with activation patching, for instance). Critics argue over whether interpretability will mature in time, before truly powerful systems appear, or whether it would be more practical, for now, to rely on behavioral evaluations instead. If we ever want to truly trust an AI, rather than merely hope it is trustworthy, we will have to open the black box. Opening it is the technical hope on which almost all of our conversation tacitly rests.
