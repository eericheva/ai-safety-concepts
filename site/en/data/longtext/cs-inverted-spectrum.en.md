### The Inverted Spectrum (John Locke, 1689; Ned Block and Sydney Shoemaker, 20th century)

Imagine we are standing at the same rosebush. You say "red" — and I say "red." We both set it apart from the green leaves, both call the sky blue, both step on the gas the instant the light turns green. Not a single divergence — not in words, not in reactions, not in how fast we find a ripe tomato in a basket. And yet: what flares up inside you at the sight of a rose is exactly what flares up in me at the sight of foliage. Your "red experience" is my "green." We have lived a life side by side and never once noticed it, because there was nothing to notice it with.

Its force lies in the asymmetry between what can be checked and what is at issue. What can be checked is behavior and function: how you sort, name, and compare things, and what you react to. On this side we are indistinguishable down to the role of the very last neuron. And what is at issue is something else — the very quality of experience, the "what it is like" to see red from within (philosophers call such qualities qualia). Here is the thesis of the inverted spectrum: if such a systematic swap of qualities can be imagined without a single difference in behavior or functional organization, then the quality of experience is not reducible to the functional role. The function is the same, the qualia different. And if so, a theory that says "to be in a mental state is to play such-and-such a causal role among inputs, outputs, and other states" — that is, functionalism — misses something important. It describes the skeleton on which experience walks, but not its color.

This is the twin of another objection — that of "absent qualia": there one imagines a system functionally identical to a human but with no inner light at all (hello, philosophical zombie). The inverted spectrum strikes more softly and more insidiously: not "there is no light" but "the light is different." The zombie lacks qualia entirely; inversion leaves them in place but untethers them from function. The second is harder to write off as a confusion, because the qualities are there — merely reshuffled.

**How it came about.**

The first to draw the map was John Locke — an empiricist who, one would think, would be the last to let such a ghost into the house. In *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689), Book II, chapter XXXII, he allows for the possibility: what if one and the same flower — a violet — gives rise in one person's mind to the idea that another gets from a marigold, and vice versa? Locke at once reassures himself: this cannot be checked, since the names and the distinctions will coincide, and so for practical purposes it does not matter. But the genie is already out: the very fact that two people's experiences can conceivably differ in quality while their use of words coincides completely is the seed of the whole later discussion. Locke noticed a crack and neatly papered it over with the wallpaper of practical indistinguishability.

For three centuries the crack waited. It was pried open when functionalism became the dominant philosophy of mind in the 1960s–70s and counterexamples were needed. Ned Block and Jerry Fodor, in "What Psychological States Are Not" (1972), brought inversion back as a weapon against the identification of the mental with the functional. Sydney Shoemaker turned it into a truly separate, carefully built argument in "The Inverted Spectrum" (1982). Curiously, Shoemaker was in fact trying to save functionalism: he argued that interpersonal inversion is conceivable, but that relations of qualitative similarity and difference can be characterized by their effects on a person's beliefs and behavior, even if particular qualia are not fixed by functional role alone. And Block later gave the screw one more turn — with the thought experiment "Inverted Earth" (1990), where the colors of the world are inverted and the person wears color-inverting lenses, so that representation and experience diverge over time, in a single person.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first and most physically grounded blow was struck by C. L. Hardin in the book *Color for Philosophers: Unweaving the Rainbow* (1988). His argument: color space is asymmetric. We see four pure, "unmixed" hues (red, green, yellow, blue) and a host of mixed ones; yellow is subjectively lighter than blue; warm and cool are distributed unevenly. You cannot just rotate the color wheel so that every color maps perfectly onto its counterpart. Under any rearrangement, a mismatch will pop out somewhere — for instance, the inverted observer will start finding "yellow" darker than "blue" and will give themselves away when distinguishing subtle differences in lightness. It follows that a smooth, behaviorally undetectable inversion is not a harmless given but requires nature to play along with a great deal of luck. For many colors there is no such luck.

Supporters of the thought experiment counterattack: the asymmetries are contingent features of humans and our cones, and the thought experiment can be run on creatures with a symmetric color space, or one can invert only that region where symmetry exists. The argument is conceived as conceptual — about what is conceivable — and facts about a particular anatomy need not defeat it. The dispute therefore retreats into the question of what "conceivability" proves at all.

The second front is verifiability and its specter, verificationism. If inversion is by definition undetectable by any means — not by behavior, not by physiology, not by self-report — then is it not a difference without a difference, mere wordplay? The strict empiricist will say: a hypothesis that changes no possible observation asserts nothing. The defender of qualia will reply: this very undetectability is the heart of the matter — it shows that the reality of experience is wider than the reality of function, not that it does not exist.

The third, the most serious by present-day standards, is representationalism. Michael Tye (*Ten Problems of Consciousness*, 1995; *Consciousness, Color, and Content*, 2000) and those close to him hold that qualia are not an inner paint over and above content but the representational content of experience itself: to see red is to represent a surface as possessing the property of redness. Then two systems that equally represent a tomato as red experience one and the same thing — by definition. Inversion under identity of representation becomes either impossible or (as in "Inverted Earth") breaks down into neatly distinguishable cases where what changes is *what* is represented, not the disembodied "how." The answer is elegant exactly to the degree that we are prepared to believe that in experience there is no remainder beyond what it represents.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**

This beast enters the alignment bestiary not as a metaphor but as a direct question. We speak more and more of the "inner states" of an AI — activations, "representations," inner "goals" that we hunt for with interpretability methods. The inverted spectrum asks whether, even if such states exist and we learn to read their causal role completely, the role guarantees the quality. We will be able to say what function a vector plays in the network — what it separates, what it leads to, how it affects the output — without necessarily settling what it is like from within, if there is a "what it is like" there at all.

And for machines the problem is nastier than for humans. With another human, I have the crutch of analogy: they are built like me, from the same meat, shaped by the same evolution — and I fill in the light for them by default. For an AI the crutch does not hold: the substrate is alien, the origin story alien, and the behavioral similarity is one we engineered ourselves — we trained the model to sound human. So with an AI, behavioral similarity can be a product of training — exactly the variable that the inverted spectrum declares blind to quality. If there can be an inversion of quality under identity of function, then a system perfectly imitating the words of pain does not thereby establish what its experience is like. Inversion alone does not settle whether it has experience at all.

The practical conclusion is not to give up but to hold two irreducible tasks apart. The first is functional and can be worked on: what the model represents, what it strives for, how it will behave out of distribution; this is what interpretability and evaluations actually address. For the second — about quality and moral status — the inversion argument, if sound, would limit what behavioral and functional evidence alone can establish about experiential quality. Hence a sober caution against two errors: passing off fluency as the presence of experience (the risk of false attribution), and declaring the question closed on the grounds that "it's just numbers" — the inverted spectrum undermines precisely the "just function" move. And the neighboring family of arguments — "the Chinese Nation" (a nation as a single functional mind), Mary's room (knowing all the physics of color without experiencing red), the zombie — marks one and the same breach: between what we can measure from outside and what it is like from within. The inverted spectrum is the quietest creature in this row. It does not shout that there is no light. It silently asks whether the light is the right color — and departs, leaving no way to check.
