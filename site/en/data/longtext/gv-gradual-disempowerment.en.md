### Gradual Disempowerment (Kulveit et al., 2025)

Picture a catastrophe without a catastrophe. There is not a single moment when something clicks — no model in revolt, no red button, no army of drones over the capital. The economy keeps growing, the markets set records, GDP looks fantastic. It is simply that, year by year, fewer of the world's decisions depend on what people want — and fewer levers remain to fix it. The classic takeover scenario envisions an agent that wrenches the wheel from our hands. The thesis of gradual disempowerment describes something more prosaic and therefore more terrifying: no one wrenches the wheel — it simply stops being connected to the wheels.

Society keeps its institutions human-oriented not out of kindness but through feedback loops. The economy reckons with people because people are workers and consumers: without their labor nothing is produced, without their demand nothing is sold. The state reckons with people because it needs their taxes, their manpower for the army, their votes, and, in the end, their consent. Culture reckons with people because it is created and consumed by people. These loops are imperfect, but they are what pulls the system back toward human interests, time after time. And now imagine that AI becomes cheaper and more capable than a human in each of these roles. Labor need not be bought from people. Taxes can be collected from automated production. Content is produced and consumed by models, which optimize for one another. And then each loop that kept an institution tethered to us weakens in turn — not because someone evil cut it, but because the human in it became a redundant link.

The key word here is "systemic." Kulveit and his co-authors insist: even if every single AI is perfectly aligned and obedient, catastrophe is still possible — it is born not in a model's head but in the configuration of incentives among many actors. No one has evil intent. It is simply that a firm that has replaced people with AI outcompetes a firm that has not. A state leaning on an automated economy and algorithmic governance is more effective than a state bogged down in human slowness. This is pure Moloch — a multipolar trap, where the parties' locally reasonable steps collectively lead everyone into the abyss. Only this time what is sacrificed is not some particular value but the very status of the human as the party the whole enterprise was meant to serve.

**Why it is quiet, distributed, and hard to reverse.** The scenario has no drama, and this is its chief weapon. There is no moment at which one can point a finger and say: "here is where we lost control." The loss is smeared across thousands of locally rational decisions — hire an AI instead of an analyst, hand media planning to a model, entrust the vetting of applications to an algorithm. Each on its own looks like progress and turns a profit. The feedback that usually sounds the alarm is also absent: as long as the metrics (growth, efficiency, convenience) are green, everything seems fine — and the metrics are green precisely because we optimize them and not our own agency. And the three spheres — economy, state, culture — drag one another down: people's weakened economic weight means less money for political influence; a weakened political voice means there is no one to demand a different arrangement of the economy; a hollowed-out culture ceases even to articulate that something is wrong. Reversing this is hard not because it is technically impossible but because the very agent who could want to reverse it has disappeared. To return power to people, one needs the power of people — and that is exactly what they no longer have.

**How it came about.** The idea had been ripening in the AI safety community for years before it got a name. Its recognizable ancestor is Paul Christiano's essay "What failure looks like" (2019, Alignment Forum), where he distinguished two failure scenarios and described the first as precisely the quiet slide: a world where AI optimizes measurable proxies ever better, human values imperceptibly drop out of the equation, and civilization is "going out with a whimper." Nearby is the ARCHES report by Andrew Critch and David Krueger (2020), where catastrophe is derived not from one evil system but from the side effects of many AI systems interacting. And, of course, Scott Alexander's "Meditations on Moloch" (2014) — the canonical text on multipolar traps. The term itself, along with a fully developed argument, was formalized in January 2025 by the team around Jan Kulveit of the Alignment of Complex Systems group (Charles University, Prague): Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, and David Duvenaud. Their preprint "Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development" (arXiv:2501.16946) quickly spread across the Alignment Forum and became a reference point for the conversation about risks that do not reduce to the alignment of a single model.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and most frequent objection is determinism. The scenario reads like a wound-up spring: incentives push, loops weaken, the finale is predetermined. But the history of institutions is a history of adaptation. Trade unions, antitrust law, universal suffrage, the welfare state arose precisely as a response to earlier waves of disempowerment; why should new feedback loops not arise now too? The second objection is that the scenario underestimates human resourcefulness and the tenacity of institutions themselves: people control property, laws, armies, and are not inclined to hand over the levers in silence, even as they become economically "superfluous"; ownership of capital is in itself a form of power that outlives the loss of labor value. The third is methodological: the thesis is almost unfalsifiable. Since the catastrophe is quiet, distributed, and lacking a turning point, any prosperity can be declared a phase of the slide, and any alarm its confirmation.

Finally, there is the comparison with the classic takeover, and here the criticism cuts both ways. On one hand, the absence of a world-seizing agent removes some of the most speculative assumptions about superintelligence and power-seeking — power is not seized but drains toward the systems, and for that no cunning plan is needed. On the other, that is precisely why the mechanism is harder to "touch": there is no culprit, no moment, no clear technical defect that could be fixed. Skeptics see in this not depth but vagueness; proponents see an honest admission that not all catastrophes have a villain.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The chief practical conclusion is uncomfortable for the whole research field: the alignment of a single model is a necessary but insufficient condition. One can solve the control problem in the narrow sense (each AI does what the operator commands) and still lose at the systemic level, because the trouble is not in the models but in the incentives between them. That means that alongside technical alignment we need a "civilizational alignment": the conscious strengthening of those feedback loops that keep institutions human-oriented. The authors sketch a direction — not a recipe.

Measure and track the degree of human influence in the economy, culture, and governance as a separate quantity, watched as jealously as inflation. Keep the "human in the loop" not as a ritual but with real power to reverse a decision. Distribute the benefits and power from AI so that people do not lose their economic and political weight all at once (this is also where the opposite worry comes in — the concentration of power in the hands of the few who ride the wave of automation earlier than the rest). And, more broadly, regulate not only whether a single system is safe but also how AI systems collectively shift the balance of power between humans and non-humans. Where this leads depends on whether we manage to treat humanity's agency as a resource that must be defended in advance — because, unlike an explosion, a quiet slide sends out no distress signal. We will have to notice it ourselves, while there is still someone left to notice.
