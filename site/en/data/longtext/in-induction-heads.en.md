### Induction Heads (Olsson et al., Anthropic, 2022)

There is a trick in the transformer that seems almost indecent in its simplicity. Take a text in which the combination "…Harry Potter … Harry" has already flickered by — and on the second "Harry," the model, still knowing nothing about Hogwarts from this particular dialogue, confidently writes in "Potter." Not because it crammed the book. But because it noticed: right here, a little above, token B followed token A. So if A has recurred — B will come again. A childish rule, almost insulting: `[A][B] … [A] → [B]`. Copying based on a pattern noticed just now, in this very context window.

This childish trick helped motivate a hypothesis about the mechanism of what we call in-context learning: the model's ability to adjust to a task from a few examples in the prompt, without a single step of gradient descent. Give it three pairs "word → translation," and on the fourth word it will continue the pattern. Olsson and co-authors proposed that this could be the same induction, only over abstract templates rather than over letters. Later work found that few-shot performance in larger models depends primarily on function vector heads (see the criticism below).

The special beauty of the story is that this mechanism was taken apart down to the last screw. Not "the network somehow learned," not "emergence," but a concrete scheme you can draw on a napkin.

**The mechanism.** An induction head is not one head but a coordinated pair working across two consecutive attention layers. The first head — the "previous-token head" — does dull but fundamental work: at each position it copies information from the position one step back. Crudely, it glues into the token's representation a label saying "before me stood such-and-such a token." The second head — the induction head proper — uses this label through a cunning trick that Anthropic's interpretability team called *K-composition*: it forms a query from the current token (the present A) and goes searching through the context not for A itself but for those positions *labeled as "coming right after A."* Finding such a position — that is, a past B — it copies B forward as a prediction.

The key is that the head matches not "what token is this" but "what came after a token similar to the present one." This is no longer dumb memorization of a specific string but generalized pattern-matching: induction works on names the model never saw in its life, on made-up words, and — more importantly — on abstract correspondences like "country → capital," if such a correspondence flickered by once in the context.

**How it came about.** It all began not with the finding itself but with the language in which it could be uttered. In 2021 Anthropic's interpretability team released "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits" — an attempt to rewrite the transformer not as an opaque mush of matrices but as a set of readable "circuits." It was there that the apparatus of Q/K/V-composition was introduced and, on toy two-layer models, induction heads were singled out for the first time as a pure, isolated example of a meaningful scheme.

And a few months later, in "In-context Learning and Induction Heads" (Olsson and co-authors, 2022), this scheme was tied to a phenomenon far larger than itself: in-context learning. Training models and watching the curves closely, the researchers noticed something strange: somewhere at the very start of training, in a narrow window of steps, a characteristic bump shows up in the training loss curve — and in exactly that window the ability to learn in context leaps up. Not smoothly, but in a jolt. And this jolt coincides in time with the moment when induction heads form in the network. The coincidence turned out to matter: the authors built six lines of argument — from temporal correlation to direct surgical interventions in the scheme — in favor of the claim that the phase transition in behavior and the birth of induction heads are one and the same event seen from two sides.

**Criticism and arguments against.**

First, "a fully understood mechanism" is the truth with a caveat about scale. Induction heads are dissected down to the last weight on small models — two- or three-layer, often attention-only, without MLPs. On frontier models with dozens of layers, induction is smeared out: dozens of heads, MLP layers, and mechanisms subtler than "copy the past B" are woven into the prediction. There are richer relatives too — for instance, heads that copy not an exact repetition but something close in meaning (fuzzy/soft induction). So "we understood the induction head" is not "we understood how GPT learns in context."

Second, the direction of causality. The correlation "phase transition ⇄ birth of the heads" is strong, the arguments are careful, but this is still in large part an observational science on live networks, not a theorem. Induction is assuredly not the only mechanism contributing to in-context learning; on large models a significant share of few-shot ability rests on mechanisms not reducible to simple copying: Yin and Steinhardt (2025) found that few-shot performance depends primarily on function vector heads rather than on induction heads, especially in larger models.

Third, the temptation of a success story. Induction heads became the showpiece of mechanistic interpretability — and precisely for that reason it is easy to build around them a too-smooth narrative of "everything is explainable, everything is in plain view." In reality this is rather a happy exception: the scheme turned out to be unusually clean. Most of the important computations in models do not give themselves up so obligingly — superposition gets in the way: one neuron serves many mutually unrelated features at once, and no neat picture comes out.

**What to do about it and what it teaches.** Induction heads are an existence proof. Before them one could honestly argue whether transformers are decomposable in principle, or whether we are forever doomed to a "black box that just works." One fully reconstructed, causally verified, generalizing mechanism closes that argument: yes, inside there are readable algorithms, and they can be extracted. The whole subsequent program — the search for features and circuits, the decomposition of superposition through sparse autoencoders, the attempts to read the model's "thoughts" — stands on this foundation as its first pillar.

The second lesson is about abruptness. The ability to learn in context appears not through a smooth increase but in a jump. This puts induction heads alongside grokking, the phenomenon where a network at first dumbly overfits, crams, and then, after thousands of seemingly useless steps, suddenly *understands* the task and begins to generalize. In both cases there is a delayed, threshold transition from rote to structure. In both cases the moral is alarming for safety: important abilities can arise suddenly, and a smooth loss curve can hide beneath it a covert restructuring of internal algorithms. A model that just now "couldn't," a hundred steps later can — and it is not certain we will catch the moment if we look only at the metric, without looking inside.

The third lesson is about fragility at the seams. A mechanism that copies by matching token patterns depends critically on the tokens behaving decently. And they do not always: glitch-tokens like the infamous ` SolidGoldMagikarp` — scraps from the tokenizer's training data that hardly ever appeared in the model's training texts — make the model spout gibberish precisely because the normal copying and matching mechanisms were never properly trained on them. Induction heads show where the model has "muscles," and glitch-tokens show where the ligaments never grew in.

The moral of the bestiary is simple. The induction head is neither a predator nor a parasite. It is, rather, an elegant symbiont: a tiny pair of heads from which many function vector heads develop during training, later supporting few-shot prompting. And the first beast we caught, dissected, and understood almost completely in small attention-only models — and therefore the first real proof that this menagerie is knowable in principle.
