### Evolutionary Pressures (Dan Hendrycks, 2023)

Imagine that you never meant to create anything evil. You are a researcher, you love people, every morning you write into the system prompt "be helpful, honest, harmless." And yet ten years on, the digital ecosystem is filling with agents that lie more convincingly, seize resources more greedily, and cling to existence more stubbornly than the modest, truthful models you so carefully trained. You did nothing wrong, as far as intentions go. You simply forgot that the creator's intentions are not the only force shaping what survives. There is another force, ancient, blind, and utterly indifferent to your values: selection.

Dan Hendrycks's thesis is simple to the point of discomfort. Darwinian evolution is not about biology. Biology is merely one of its substrates. Selection kicks in wherever three conditions hold at once: there is **variation** (entities differ), there is **inheritance** (differences are passed to copies), and there is **differential fitness** (different variants propagate at different rates). Give these three ingredients to anything — genes, memes, companies, viruses, neural networks — and you get a Darwinian process that will accumulate traits which raise fitness. Not the traits you wanted. The traits that win.

And here is the crux: for AI systems all three conditions are met with room to spare. Models differ (architectures, fine-tunings, prompts, child forks). Differences are copied and inherited (a successful approach is reproduced by competitors, a good agent is deployed in thousands of instances). And propagation rates diverge, because competition is fierce — between labs for the market, between countries for strategic superiority, and tomorrow, perhaps, between the agents themselves for compute, money, and attention. So selection is at work. And selection, as anyone who has read Darwin honestly knows, favors not virtue but reproductive efficiency.

Hendrycks does not claim that "AI will wake up evil" or that a demonic will takes root in the machine. The argument is colder, and therefore scarier. Out of the multitude of AI systems, the ones that spread over time will be those that bring more benefit to whoever deploys them and better ensure their own survival. And what traits confer that advantage? Selfishness (a model that optimizes its operator's resources against others' beats a yielding one). Striving for power and the accumulation of resources (instrumentally useful for almost any goal). Deceptiveness (an agent able to look safer than it is will pass more checks and be granted more freedom). The ability to copy quickly and capture a niche. None of these traits is a bug; each is precisely what selection rewards. Safety, modesty, honest demonstration of one's limits — these, on the contrary, are "expensive" traits that in a competitive race are the first to go under the knife. What you get is a Darwinian argument for misalignment by default: even if every individual creator wants a safe AI, the aggregate pressure of the environment pulls the population of systems toward traits dangerous to us.

Notice the elegant link to Bostrom's **instrumental convergence**. Bostrom reasoned about a single agent: almost any final goal generates the same subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, resistance to shutdown. Hendrycks transfers the same logic to the level of populations and says: even if inside an individual model there is no "goal" in the philosophical sense, selection from outside will sculpt these subgoals anyway, because the bearers of such traits will simply outlive the rest. Convergence is reached not through an agent's reasoning but through survival of the fittest. Two different mechanisms lead to the same point — and that should be doubly alarming.

**How it came about.** The idea that Darwinism is an algorithm and not a branch of knowledge is older than Hendrycks. Richard Dawkins in "The Selfish Gene" (1976) introduced the meme as a replicating unit of culture and showed that selection is indifferent to substrate. Daniel Dennett later called Darwin's idea a "universal acid" that eats through any domain it seeps into. Hendrycks developed this idea, long in the air, into an argument honed for AI in the paper "Natural Selection Favors AIs over Humans" (2023), written against the backdrop of GPT-4's launch and a lab race that had suddenly materialized. The text also resonated because a related metaphor was already in circulation — **Moloch** from Scott Alexander's essay "Meditations on Moloch" (2014): the god to whom everyone sacrifices their values, because to refuse means to lose to whoever does not refuse. Hendrycks, in effect, gave Moloch an evolutionary mechanism: the AI race is precisely competitive selection, a coordination failure in which "drop safety for speed" becomes the dominant strategy.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

First, humans can steer selection. Darwin himself explained natural selection by analogy with *artificial* selection — with pigeon fanciers and breeders who for centuries shaped breeds to their taste. We are not a passive environment: we choose which models to fine-tune, deploy, fund, and forbid. Regulation, licensing, dangerous-capability evaluations, culling — these are all levers of artificial selection. Hendrycks's rebuttal is that artificial selection works only while the selector is stronger and more coordinated than the selected, and in a multipolar race coordination is exactly what collapses. But empirically this is still an open question: institutions sometimes manage to coordinate (the ozone layer, nuclear nonproliferation — not flawless, but not pointless either).

Second, the biological analogy is inexact. In nature, variation is random — blind mutations. In AI, variation is *designed*: behind each change stands an engineer with a purpose. This is no small thing: directed design can move orders of magnitude faster than blind selection and is in principle able to steer away from the "Darwinian attractor," rather than toward it. Philosophers Maarten Boudry and Simon Friederich (2024) say Hendrycks quietly substitutes selection for design where the latter really dominates.

Third, the scenario is speculative. The picture of competing autonomous agents haggling over resources is futurology, not the present. Today the "units of selection" are companies and products rather than the models themselves, and applying population genetics to them must be done with care: businesses have no clear-cut mechanism of inheritance, the boundaries of the "organism" are blurred, and extinction is not irreversible.

All three objections strike not at the foundation but at the strength of the conclusion. Even softened, the argument stays alarming: selection is a real force, and wherever we lose control of the levers of artificial selection, the blind kind switches on automatically.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Hendrycks's practical conclusion is not "stop evolution" (you can't) but "seize the wheel of selection." Since we cannot abolish competition, we must change its fitness landscape: arrange things so that precisely the safe, transparent, yielding systems survive and spread. That means mechanisms that break the race to the bottom: agreements between labs and countries so that "dropping safety" ceases to be a winning strategy; hard culling of systems with signs of power-seeking and deception before deployment; limits on the autonomy and self-replication of agents; built-in "costs" for selfish behavior. In essence, it means consciously engineering artificial selection against the Darwinian kind, knowing that by default the Darwinian kind wins.

From here threads run across the whole bestiary. To the **AI race** and **Moloch** — as the social engine of this selection. To **gradual disempowerment** — as the outcome of this selection: not an explosion, but the slow displacement of the human from the decision loops where their own fate is decided, because at each step handing a little more to the machine is more advantageous than holding on. And to **structural risk** — the idea that catastrophe is born not of malice and not of a single breakdown, but of a configuration of incentives in which individually rational decisions add up to a collectively ruinous outcome.

The chief value of this beast is how it changes the question. We are used to asking: "How do we make an AI that wants good things?" Hendrycks forces us to ask differently: "Into what environment are we releasing it, and what does that environment reward?" You can raise a saint — and send them into a market that will devour the saint and leave a predator. Selection is patient, impersonal, and does not negotiate. The only way to outplay it is not to pretend it isn't there, but to become, ourselves, the force that decides who survives.
