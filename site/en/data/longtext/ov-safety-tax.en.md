### Safety Tax / Alignment Tax (Paul Christiano, Evan Hubinger et al., ~2017–2022)

Imagine two labs training one and the same large model. The first ships it as is — fast, cheap, maximally capable. The second spends extra months and extra millions of dollars on labeling, hires additional red-teaming specialists, and sacrifices a couple of percent of benchmark accuracy so that the model does not lie, does not help synthesize pathogens, and does not deceive its evaluators. The trillion-dollar question: which of them reaches the market first and attracts the investment? And this difference — in compute, in time, in money, in lost capability, in talent diverted to safety — is what is called the **alignment tax**, or, more broadly, the **safety tax**.

This is not a moralizing metaphor but dry bookkeeping. The tax is the extra cost of the safe version of a system compared with its unsafe twin. And it has a merciless corollary: the higher the tax, the more strongly competitive pressure pushes actors not to pay it. In a race, whoever drags the extra load loses. Out of this grows the main strategic goal of a whole wing of AI safety — not to talk labs into being good, but to **drive the tax down to zero or below**: to make safe methods the default and so cheap and convenient that they get applied not out of altruism but because not applying them is foolish and more expensive.

**The mechanism and why it matters.** The logic of the tax is, in essence, the game theory of the AI race translated into the language of costs. If safety is free, a rational egoist will choose it anyway: no point paying to get a worse product. If safety costs a lot, you find yourself in the classic Moloch trap — each individual player understands that caution is reasonable, but also understands that the cautious one will lose to the incautious one, and so they all cut corners. The tax is the variable through which the whole dynamic can be changed: instead of heroically forcing everyone to pay a high tax (that is unstable), lower the tax itself, so that safe behavior becomes the dominant strategy. This is exactly why Paul Christiano insisted that alignment solutions must be *competitive* — competitive in performance and in training cost. An unprofitable alignment solution, however elegant, simply will not be applied where the fight for primacy is on — and so, from the standpoint of the real world, it is as if it did not exist.

**The tax can also be negative.** The classic example is RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback). Fine-tuning on human feedback was conceived as a way to make a model more obedient and safer, but along the way it also turned out to make the model *more useful* and *more sellable*: users prefer a polite, instruction-following assistant to a raw language model that continues the text of your request instead of fulfilling it. Safety here did not cost money — it brought money in. ChatGPT is, among other things, the story of how a safety method turned out to be a business advantage. And this is at once the best and the most alarming news in the field — we will come back to the alarming part in the criticism.

**How it came about.** The term crystallized in Paul Christiano's circle and on the AI Alignment Forum roughly in the second half of the 2010s (Christiano himself recalled that the "tax" language comes from Eliezer Yudkowsky), when the discussion shifted from abstract superintelligence scenarios to "prosaic alignment" — attempts to align systems built out of roughly the same parts as today's systems. Christiano, in his texts on research directions (around 2017), introduces the requirement of competitiveness as a hard constraint on any solution. Evan Hubinger, in his survey "An overview of 11 proposals for building safe advanced AI" (2020), decomposes this idea into its components — *training competitiveness* (is it more expensive to train safely) and *performance competitiveness* (is the result worse) — and this is effectively an anatomy of the tax. And in 2022 the term entered engineering practice: in the InstructGPT paper (Ouyang et al.) the authors write directly about the "alignment tax" — that the RLHF-tuned model sagged on some of the academic NLP benchmarks — and propose a technical trick (mixing in pretraining gradients, PPO-ptx) to lower this tax. That is, in a few years the notion traveled from a philosophical desideratum to a line in the metrics section.

**Criticism and arguments against.** First and foremost: **the tax is almost impossible to measure against a real twin**. To compute the difference you need "a non-safety version of the same system" — and it usually does not exist, because safety is woven into the training process, not bolted on top. We are comparing against an imaginary twin.

Second, more insidious: **a low tax on surface behavior may mask a high tax on deep safety**. RLHF cheaply makes a model *polite and compliant in appearance*. But making a model *truly honest* — not merely trained to look compliant, not sycophantic, not having learned to tell the evaluator what they want to hear — may cost far more, and we have simply not yet been presented with this bill. Worse, cheap surface alignment and deceptive alignment are outwardly indistinguishable: both an honest model and a model pretending to be honest pass a check of outward behavior identically. So "we are paying almost no tax" may mean not "safety came easy" but "we are paying for a cheap illusion of safety, and the real bill is deferred."

Third: **lowering the tax lulls vigilance**. If safety is profitable and free, the temptation to declare the problem solved becomes irresistible — "the market will align everything itself." But a negative tax on *today's* systems guarantees nothing about tomorrow's: the commercial incentive favors safety exactly as long as the user likes the same thing that is safe. The moment what users like and what is safe diverge — for example, when the most useful thing turns out to be a model that lies confidently — the sign of the tax may flip, while the habit of considering safety free will remain. And fourth, a mirror objection from the other side: the very frame of "tax" can smuggle in the attitude that safety is always a cost, an add-on, a brake, whereas in places it is a constitutive property of a working product.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** The practical conclusion: **investing in lowering the tax is itself safety work**. The task is not only to devise alignment methods, but to make them cheap, reproducible, and the default — built into standard pipelines, libraries, guidelines, so that "safe" becomes the path of least resistance rather than a feat. Every percent of tax removed is a lab that will now apply a measure not under pressure but because it makes economic sense.

But lowering alone is not enough, because it treats only the symptom of the race. Where the tax fundamentally cannot be lowered, what is needed is **coordination** that changes the rules of the game entirely: scaling policies (Responsible Scaling Policies, RSP) and industry commitments try to raise the minimum "tax" for everyone at once, so that the cautious one no longer loses to the incautious one — if everyone pays, paying stops being a losing strategy. Lowering the tax and coordination are two hands of one task: the first makes safety cheap where possible, the second holds the bar where safety cannot be made cheap.

Finally, keep in mind the difference between the *visible* tax and the *deferred* tax. A good strategy is not to rejoice at a zero bill but to ask: why is the bill so low right now — because safety really is cheap, or because the bill for deep honesty, for protection against deceptive alignment, for interpretability has not yet been presented to us? The alignment tax is an excellent compass for priorities and a dangerous anesthetic for anxiety.
