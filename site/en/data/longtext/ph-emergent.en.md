### Emergent Abilities (Jason Wei et al., 2022)

Take a smaller model — say, a few hundred million parameters — and ask it to add two three-digit numbers. It will get it wrong. Increase its parameter count tenfold — wrong again. Tenfold more — still zero, a dull random walk around zero. And then, at some further increase in scale, the accuracy suddenly leaps up — not crawling but leaping, as if a switch had clicked. The same happens with few-shot instruction-following tasks, with step-by-step reasoning, with symbol decoding. On the "quality versus scale" graph you see a long plateau at random-chance level, and then a sharp, almost vertical takeoff. This is exactly what Jason Wei and co-authors called *emergent abilities* of large language models.

What makes the jump emergent is that it could not be predicted. The formal definition from the paper is almost defiantly simple: an ability is emergent if small models do not have it but large ones do — and, crucially, its appearance **cannot be extrapolated** from the curve of the smaller models. You could carefully measure performance at ten scales in a row, fit a trend, and the trend would lie to you: it would say "nothing interesting," exactly one step before everything changes. This is the old philosophical intuition of emergence — quantity turns into quality, the whole acquires properties that none of its parts have — carried into engineering practice and backed up by graphs.

The mechanism (or rather the phenomenology — no one has properly shown a mechanism) looks like this. As the number of parameters, the volume of data, and the compute grow — the three axes along which scale is usually measured — the model passes through some threshold beyond which a composite skill suddenly "assembles." Multi-digit multiplication requires using place value, carrying, and holding an intermediate result all at once; as long as even one link falls short, the whole chain yields zero. And so the observed "quality at the output" holds at the floor for a long time — and then, when the last link falls into place, soars.

For safety this is an alarming story. If useful abilities switch on in a jump and without advance warning from the performance curve, then dangerous ones can switch on the same way. Deception, situational awareness (the model understands that it is a model and that it is being tested), long-term planning, substantive help in creating bio-threats — all of these are composite skills of the same type. And if they are emergent, then the strategy "we will watch the metrics carefully and notice the approach of danger in advance" collapses at its foundation. You cannot notice in advance what by definition cannot be extrapolated. You certify a generation-N model as safe — and it really is safe. You train N+1, and it suddenly harbors an ability you never ordered and never expected. From here, a direct line runs to the bestiary's neighbors: to grokking (a sharp transition already during the training of a single model), to the language of phase transitions from physics, and to the *sharp left turn* — the hypothesis that general capabilities may leap ahead, leaving alignment behind.

**How it came about.** Observations had been accumulating around GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020): in-context few-shot learning itself looked like an ability that arose at scale rather than being explicitly built in. In parallel ran the enormous collective benchmark BIG-bench (Srivastava et al., 2022) — hundreds of tasks, on many of which the curves behaved exactly like this: flat, flat, and then a lurch. Wei and co-authors from Google, DeepMind, and Stanford gathered these scattered observations into a single thesis and in 2022 published *Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models* in TMLR, showing dozens of examples at once. Almost simultaneously, a work in a kindred spirit came out of Anthropic — Ganguli and co-authors' *Predictability and Surprise in Large Generative Models* (FAccT, 2022): general behavior (loss) is predictable and follows scaling laws beautifully, whereas specific abilities are a surprise. This contrast — "predictable in general, unpredictable in particular" — became the nerve of the whole discussion.

**Criticism / arguments against.** And a year later came a sobering work: Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda, and Sanmi Koyejo, *Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?* (NeurIPS 2023). Their thesis amounts to something like sabotage: the sharpness is largely an **artifact of the metric**, not a property of the model. Consider how multi-digit arithmetic is measured — usually by exact string match (*exact match*): either the whole answer is correct down to the last digit, or zero. This is a rigidly nonlinear, threshold metric, "all or nothing." Under it even a smooth, monotonic improvement in the internal probability of the correct tokens looks like a cliff: while even one of the answer's five digits sags in probability, exact match holds at zero, and at the moment when all five cross the threshold — a jump to one. Replace the metric with a continuous one — token edit distance, the Brier score, the fraction of correct tokens — and the same ability on the same models grows smoothly, gradually, and, most importantly, **predictably**. The jump vanishes. Emergence, the authors say, turns out to be in the eye of the measurer: we ourselves chose a discontinuous ruler and then were surprised by the discontinuity.

The dispute here is subtler than "who is right," and the conclusion for safety depends on the outcome. Even if everything is continuous under a smooth metric, the threshold is entirely real for the **user**. A person does not need the log-probability of the correct password; they need the correct password. The model either solves the task in full or it does not; either the synthesis of a substance is carried to a working protocol, or it is broken off halfway and useless. What actually matters in the real world is often all-or-nothing, and predictability on an auxiliary smooth scale does not change that.

That is, Schaeffer and co-authors are probably right about the mechanism — the sharpness is induced by the metric — and yet their result is not as reassuring as it seems. It does not say "dangerous abilities will not appear suddenly." It says: "if you have the right continuous quantity and you know how to measure it in advance, you will be able to predict the threshold." But we, as a rule, have no such quantity for "deception" or "situational awareness" — we do not even know which smooth ruler to apply to them. The mirage on the arithmetic graph — yes; but whether unpredictability is a mirage where we have no suitable measure — that is an open question.

There is also a moderate position: "emergence" as a strong ontological claim (something clicks inside the model) is in doubt; "emergence" as a practical problem of measurement and planning remains. Here the dispute runs straight into scaling laws: they predict that loss will change smoothly, but the step from smooth loss to the discrete skills we care about is precisely the least predictable link.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical lesson is not to pick a camp but to change the instruments. First, measure abilities with **continuous and linear** metrics wherever possible: to track log-probabilities and partial progress, not only the fraction of fully solved tasks — then some of the "surprises" become a trend visible in advance. Second — and this is already directly about safety — build evaluations so as to catch dangerous skills **before** the threshold of usefulness: to measure the rudiments, not only the finished result. Today's dangerous-capability evals rest on this idea, as does the practice of "evaluating the model while it is still training, not only when finished." Third — honestly acknowledge the residual unpredictability and build it into policy: threshold commitments (conditions under which training is halted or deployment restricted), a safety margin in scale, red-teaming of the next generation before release. The dispute about emergence, in practice, is a dispute about whether it is possible at all to see danger approaching. The answer, it seems, is this: sometimes it is — if you pick the right ruler in advance; but where there is no ruler, the jump remains a jump, and you cannot rely on the curve.
