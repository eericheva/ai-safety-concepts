### Goal-Directedness (Daniel Dennett, 1987)

A thermostat "wants" twenty degrees. It measures the temperature, compares it with the set point, switches on the heat, switches it off — and if you open a window, it will again stubbornly pull the room toward its cherished twenty. We speak of it in the language of desires without hesitation, though inside there is only a bimetallic strip or a pair of transistors, and no "wanting" at all. Now take a chess engine: it "strives" to win, "sacrifices" its queen for the attack, "fears" perpetual check. And a human being who simultaneously pursues a career, love, peace, and three contradictory vacation plans. Somewhere on this scale — from the strip to the human — lies a question that matters to AI safety more than almost anything else: **when is a system better described as pursuing a goal rather than merely executing a set of reactions?**

The key word here is "better," not "really." Goal-directedness is not a binary switch, and not a substance you can find by prying open the casing. It varies by degree. And a description in terms of goals is not a report on an inner essence but the describer's own strategy.

This is exactly what Daniel Dennett formalized in 1987 in the book *The Intentional Stance*. He proposed distinguishing three "stances," three ways of predicting a system's behavior. The physical stance — through the laws of physics (a stone will fall because of gravity). The design stance — through construction and purpose (an alarm clock will ring at seven because it is built that way). And the intentional stance — when we ascribe to a system beliefs and desires and predict its behavior as though it were a rational agent striving toward goals in light of what it "knows." To reason this way about a stone is pointless. About a chess program it works brilliantly: "it wants to defend its king" predicts the next move better and more cheaply than a simulation of millions of positions.

The subtlety of Dennett's move is that the intentional stance is a tool of the predictor, not a discovery about the system being predicted. A system is goal-directed exactly to the degree that describing it through goals pays off: yields accurate, economical forecasts. For Dennett this is not a trick and not "merely a metaphor" — intentional patterns are as real as any pattern that compresses data. But they are real *relative* to an observer and their tasks, not as a soul hidden inside the system.

**How it came about.** Dennett was writing not about AI but about the philosophy of mind — arguing both with the behaviorists and with those who sought "real intentionality" in neurons. But his frame turned out to be almost perfectly cut to fit the anxieties of alignment, which ripened decades later. The line runs through Stephen Omohundro and his "The Basic AI Drives" (2008) and Nick Bostrom with the thesis of instrumental convergence ("The Superintelligent Will," 2012): if a system is sufficiently goal-directed and sufficiently capable, then under almost any final goal it finds it advantageous to seize resources, preserve itself, and not let itself be retrained — because a dead or rewritten agent will not achieve its goal. From here comes the dividing line that became central to the whole field: goal-directed agents are subject to instrumental convergence and are therefore dangerous, whereas "tools" — predictors, oracles, pure probability calculators — presumably are not, because they have no reason to cling to existence. The whole dispute over whether an LLM is an agent or a tool after all is a dispute over which side of this line it stands on. And it cannot be settled until we have agreed on what "being an agent" means.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first line of criticism strikes at the too-easy inference of goal-directedness from rationality. In the alignment community an argument long circulated: since a superintelligence will be coherent (not violating the axioms of expected utility, not falling for "money pumps" that exploit inconsistent preferences to extract money through repeated trades), it will inevitably turn out to be a goal-directed maximizer. Rohin Shah, in the note "Coherence arguments do not entail goal-directed behavior" (2018), showed that this step is not rigorous. The von Neumann–Morgenstern theorems say only that a coherent agent *can* be described as a maximizer of some utility function — but the utility is defined over histories, and one can be found to fit *any* behavior, including the entirely "instrumental" and safe. From coherence alone, goal-directedness in the dangerous sense does not follow; you need additional assumptions that the goal is simple, defined over world states, and stable. Katja Grace later objected to Shah ("Coherence arguments imply a force for goal-directed behavior," 2021): there is a pressure toward goal-directedness after all, but it is a tendency, not a logical inevitability.

The second line concerns operationalization. If goal-directedness depends on the observer, as in Dennett, then any process can, if you wish, be described through goals (a stone "strives" to minimize potential energy — look, a variational principle!). So for safety we need a criterion tougher than Dennett's pragmatism. Alex Flint in "The ground of optimization" (2020) proposed defining optimization not through ascribed goals but through the system's behavior under perturbations: an optimizing system is one that reliably evolves toward a narrow set of target configurations from a broad set of initial ones and *returns* to the target configurations if you push it aside. A thermostat passes this test, a ball in a bowl passes, but billiard balls on a table or a satellite in orbit do not: nudge them and they never return anywhere.

A similarly rigorous approach tries to catch goal-directedness through *retargetability*: a system is worth regarding as goal-directed if it contains a separable "steering wheel" — a search mechanism that can be re-aimed at another goal without rewriting everything else. For Alex Turner and Prasad Tadepalli ("Parametrically Retargetable Decision-Makers Tend To Seek Power," NeurIPS 2022) this yields a theorem: a broad class of retargetable decision-makers statistically gravitates toward seizing power — not because it is "evil," but because power is useful for *most* of the goals the steering wheel can be turned toward. Vanessa Kosoy goes further still: her learning-theoretic agenda tries to give agency a definition not tied to the observer's willingness to ascribe goals. The general upshot of the criticism: Dennett's frame is brilliant as a diagnosis, but for engineering guarantees it has to be toughened up — otherwise "goal-directedness" blurs and rules nothing out.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is uncomfortable: we do not yet have an instrument that would output for a concrete model the number "goal-directedness = 0.7." And without it we cannot honestly say whether we are looking at an oracle or the germ of an agent. Mesa-optimization sharpens the problem: a trained network may grow *inside itself* a retargetable search with its own goal, different from the one it was trained for — and then the "tool" turns out to be an agent we did not order.

From here come three working approaches. The first — not to argue about the words "agent/tool," but to ask concretely: is there a separable steering wheel inside? does behavior return to the goal after perturbations? does the intentional description pay off better than the mechanical one? The second — to build architectures that *fail* the goal-directedness test where we do not need it: pure predictors, oracles with a limited channel of action, systems without stability under perturbations in the dangerous direction. The third — to take Dennett's lesson seriously in both directions: since goal-directedness lives in the description, then our own tendency to see in a chatbot "a person who wants something" is also an intentional stance, and it can both deceive us (anthropomorphism where there is only an echo of reactions) and lull us (we brush off a real retargetable search mechanism, calling it "just autocomplete"). Goal-directedness is not a beast you catch in a net, but a shadow that now lengthens, now vanishes depending on which lamp you stand under. The danger is that, however the light changes, the beast itself does not go anywhere.
