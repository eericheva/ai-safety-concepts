### The Panopticon (Bentham, 1791 / Foucault, 1975)

Picture a ring-shaped building. Around the perimeter, cells several stories high, each lit clear through: a window facing out, a window facing in. At the center, a tower. In the tower sits a watchman, but blinds, shutters, and zigzag passages obscure the view through the tower's windows, so that from the cell the tower looks like a black eye: the prisoner cannot tell whether it is open or shut. The prisoner is always in view. The watcher — never. And here is the key move the whole construction was devised for: it does not matter to the inmate whether they are being watched right now. What matters is that they can be watched at any moment, and that they cannot check whether they are being watched. So one must behave as if watched always.

Jeremy Bentham, the English utilitarian, called this the "Panopticon" — the "all-seeing" — and was sure he had invented a machine for remaking people. "A new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind, in a quantity hitherto without example," he wrote. Not only a prison: the same scheme suited factories, schools, hospitals, workhouses, infirmaries. One principle for all the institutions where many bodies must behave correctly with a minimum of staff. Bentham even put his own money into the project and for years wore a path to Parliament's door. The prison was never built. The idea outlived the blueprint.

Michel Foucault gave the Panopticon its true second life in the book *Discipline and Punish* (1975). For Foucault, Bentham's prison is not a curiosity from the history of penitentiary architecture, but a **diagram**: the pure formula of how power is arranged in modern society. Formerly power was a spectacle — a public execution, quartering, the body of the criminal torn apart in the square before the crowd. Power showed itself in order to terrify. The Panopticon inverts the optics: now it is not power but the subject that is made visible. The subject stands in the light. Power stays in the shadow.

The mechanism works even when idling. You can remove the watchman from the tower — the effect will persist, because discipline has already migrated inward. The prisoner, Foucault wrote, "inscribes in himself the power relation in which he simultaneously plays both roles; he becomes the principle of his own subjection." The gaze is internalized. You are your own watchman, your own censor, watching yourself so as not to slip. Violence is no longer needed — a constant, unverifiable possibility of being seen is enough. Power, which once broke bodies, now produces obedient souls — cheaply, quietly, continuously. This is not about the prison. It is about how the human being is normalized in a world of exams, medical records, report cards, dossiers, and ratings.

**How it came about.** Bentham sketched the Panopticon in 1786–1787, noting that it was invented not by himself but largely by his brother Samuel — an engineer looking for a way to oversee a large workshop with few masters. Jeremy took the idea to its philosophical extreme and published it in 1791 as a series of letters. Then came nearly two centuries of semi-oblivion: economists remembered Bentham as the father of utilitarianism; the ring-prison was rarely recalled. Foucault pulled the blueprint out of the archive in the early 1970s, when he himself was leading an activist campaign for prisoners' rights in French prisons. He needed an image, and the Panopticon fit perfectly: not a building but a principle, which he recognized everywhere — in the barracks, in the clinic, in the classroom, on the assembly line. From that moment the word became a common noun, and by the start of the 21st century an almost inevitable metaphor in any conversation about surveillance.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first is historical. Historians of criminal law pointed out that Foucault made history straighter than it was: public executions and "disciplinary" practices coexisted for centuries; the transition was not as clean and linear as he depicted it. The diagram is beautiful, but reality is dirtier.

The second — and, perhaps, more important for us — is that the Panopticon as a model of modern surveillance is already obsolete. Gilles Deleuze, in the short and biting note "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990), observed: Foucault's disciplinary societies locked the human being into separate enclosures — family, school, factory, prison, each with its own walls and its own oversight. But this system is crumbling. In its place comes the **society of control**, where there are no walls and no pauses: control is continuous, fluid, following you everywhere — it does not lock you in but tracks your trajectory. Not a tower, but a signal in your pocket. Many critics of digital surveillance — for example, Kevin Haggerty and Richard Ericson in "The Surveillant Assemblage" (2000) — repeat: the Panopticon presupposes a single center and a single observer, whereas modern surveillance is distributed, multiple, without a central tower; the data are gathered by thousands of agents, and many of them are commercial actors, not state actors.

The third objection inverts the metaphor itself. Bernard Harcourt in the book *Exposed* (2015) argues that the digital world is not a Panopticon but its inversion. The Panopticon ran on the fear of being seen. And we **ourselves want** to be seen: we post, upload, check in, chase the like and the view. We are not driven into a cage under the gaze — we ourselves scramble into the light. This is not a dictatorship of surveillance but an "expository society" — people expose themselves to another's gaze, voluntarily and eagerly; the cage has become cozy, and the door is open from the inside.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** And yet for the "Bestiary" the Panopticon remains a living beast — because one core of it has survived all the refutations: **asymmetry of visibility plus uncertainty of observation gives birth to self-discipline.** It is exactly this, and not the architecture of the ring, that is reproduced in the world of AI.

Shoshana Zuboff in *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) describes a new economic logic: human experience becomes raw material from which "behavioral surplus" is extracted, driven through predictive models, and sold on "behavioral futures markets." Here the Panopticon completes itself. It is not enough to see the present — the goal is to predict and gently nudge the future. The gaze is no longer passive: it computes who you will become and adjusts you with a recommendation, a feed, a price, a prompt. Content moderation, scoring, facial recognition, predictive policing — everywhere the same move: the subject does not know exactly when the algorithm is watching and exactly what it is counting, and behaves properly just in case.

This effect has a name — the **chilling effect** — and can be measured. In a 2016 study, Jonathon Penney showed that after Snowden's revelations about NSA surveillance, views of "sensitive" Wikipedia articles — 48 articles on terror groups and their methods, matched to the US Department of Homeland Security's terrorism keyword list — fell in a statistically significant way. People did not forbid themselves to read — they simply stopped, knowing they might be watched. This is the Panopticon in pure form, only the tower is a data center and the blinds are the opacity of the algorithm.

The practical conclusion for those who think about AI safety is twofold.

First, the surveillance frame is double-edged. A significant part of the alignment agenda is interpretability, monitoring, deception detection: we want to make the model **visible**, to read its "thoughts," to catch it when it lies. This is reasonable and necessary. But the Panopticon reminds us: the instrument with which we observe the model, in the same motion, observes people too — users, developers, whole populations. The infrastructure of control does not distinguish whom to control; whoever holds the tower decides.

Second, there is an insidious feedback loop. If a model trains on the behavior of people who already censor themselves under the algorithmic gaze, it internalizes not what people think but what they dare to show. A chilled, smoothed-over, normalized slice. And it returns it to us as the norm — amplifying the chill. The Panopticon, looped back on itself.

What is to be done? To break not the gaze but its asymmetry and opacity. Transparency of the observer (who collects what, and why), verifiable limits on surveillance, the right not to be predicted, the design of systems in which uncertainty works for people rather than against them. The Panopticon teaches a simple and uncomfortable thing: to enslave, one need not coerce — it is enough to watch in such a way that one can never check whether one is being watched. The good news is that this mechanism rests on opacity. And opacity is exactly what alignment does know how to fight. The only question is whose tower alignment will end up shining its light on.
