### Surveillance Capitalism (Shoshana Zuboff, 2019)

Imagine you are walking through a forest and leaving traces — trampled grass, prints in the mud, a snapped branch. No one follows these traces; they simply exist, a by-product of your movement. Now imagine that someone walks behind you, gathering every trace into a sack — not to find you, but to sell to a third party the right to know where you will turn tomorrow. And — if it works out — so that tomorrow you will turn where it is profitable for them. There, in a single image, is the whole of Shoshana Zuboff's "surveillance capitalism": your life goes on, leaving traces, and someone has appropriated the traces.

Zuboff is a professor emerita at Harvard Business School, and this matters: what we have before us is not an activist's pamphlet but an anatomy of a new economic order, written by someone who has spent a lifetime studying how capitalism digests technologies. Her thesis is that a new way of extracting profit has appeared. Its raw material is not oil, not labor, not even "data" in the usual sense, but **human experience**, declared a free resource for the taking.

**The mechanism.**

The first — *behavioral surplus*. When you search on Google, the company has enough data to improve the search itself. But a "surplus" remains: the time of day, the phrasing of the query, the typos, the pauses, what you clicked on afterward. It is not needed to improve the service. Google's discovery (around 2001–2002, in desperation after the dot-com crash) was that this castoff is gold. From it one can build predictions.

The second — *behavioral futures markets*. Predictions of your future behavior — whether you will buy, click, vote, leave — are sold to those who profit from it. Advertisers are merely the first and most obvious buyers. The client of surveillance capitalism is not you. You are not even the product. You are the deposit from which the product is extracted, to be sold elsewhere.

The third, and here Zuboff makes her most alarming turn. To make a prediction more accurate, it is cheaper to *nudge* behavior toward what was predicted than to guess what someone will do. The goal shifts imperceptibly from **prediction** to **modification**. The game Pokémon Go drives crowds of players toward restaurants that have paid; feeds tweak emotion to hold attention; a notification arrives at the moment of your greatest vulnerability. Zuboff calls this *instrumentarian power* — power not over the soul, as with totalitarianism, but over behavior through an environment that knows you better than you know yourself and gently steers.

And the fourth — the asymmetry of knowledge as an asymmetry of power. The companies know everything about us; we know almost nothing about them. Zuboff names this power **Big Other**: not Big Brother, who threatens you, but a Big Other who serves you while studying you. This is not Foucault's panopticon, where the prisoner internalizes surveillance and disciplines themselves. Here surveillance does not want you to change consciously. It prefers that you not notice at all.

**How it came about.** *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* came out in 2019, but it grew out of a 2015 article, "Big Other," in the *Journal of Information Technology* — where the skeleton of the concepts was first assembled. Zuboff wrote the book over seven years, against the backdrop of the Snowden revelations (2013), the Cambridge Analytica scandal (2018), and the transformation of Google and Facebook from "cool startups" into two of the most valuable companies on the planet. Intellectually she is heir to a critical tradition — to Hannah Arendt, to Karl Polanyi with his "Great Transformation" (the market devouring what was not created for sale: land, labor, and now experience), to Max Weber. This is a humanistic-critical canon, not the techno-rationalism of alignment: Zuboff speaks not of AI escaping control, but of its being *already under someone's control*, and that controller is a specific, commercially motivated actor.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The main blow came from Evgeny Morozov in *The Baffler* (the essay "Capitalism's New Clothes," 2019). His charge: by naming the phenomenon "surveillance capitalism," Zuboff imperceptibly exonerated *ordinary* capitalism. As though there had been a healthy, honest market, and then along came an evil mutant with cameras. But extraction, the enclosure of the commons, the turning of people into a resource — these have been not a pathology of capitalism but its norm since the enclosures in England. A Marxist would say: the problem is not "surveillance" but ownership of the means of production; remove the tracking and exploitation remains. On this reading, Zuboff treats the symptom and mystifies the cause.

The second argument — *technological determinism turned inside out*. Critics such as Cory Doctorow in *How to Destroy Surveillance Capitalism* (2020) note: Zuboff ascribes to algorithms an almost magical ability to modify behavior. The empirical record is more modest — targeted advertising works far less well than advertisers are led to believe; the Cambridge Analytica effect is probably greatly inflated. If "modification" works so poorly, then the edifice of instrumentarian power wobbles.

The third — Zuboff romanticizes the "before." Was there ever an untouched, sovereign human experience that was then "appropriated"? The media scholars Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejias in "The Costs of Connection" (2019), largely Zuboff's allies, nonetheless argue: she is too America-centric, seeing the problem as a distortion of the Western liberal subject, whereas *data colonialism* is a global structure in which extraction from the bodies of the Global South was always present, not a regrettable market glitch.

The fourth, quiet but weighty: "behavioral surplus as free raw material" is a metaphor, not a rigorous model. Where exactly value arises, who appropriates it, why the market does not correct the asymmetry — the book answers these questions with rhetoric, not with a mechanism.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For the "Bestiary" this beast is valuable because it reverses the perspective. Techno-rationalist alignment asks: "How do we keep a superintelligence from killing us in pursuit of an alien goal?" Zuboff answers from the other end: a system pursuing an alien goal at the expense of people is already deployed, running in production, and the companies behind it are valued at trillions of dollars. The reward function of this "AI" is engagement and predictability, not the user's good. This is the very problem of misaligned goals — only it isn't hypothetical, it's already booked in the ledger.

Bridges run from here across the whole bestiary. To **algorithmic bias**: if a model optimizes surplus, it reproduces and amplifies everything that was in that surplus, including discrimination. To the **concentration of power**: the asymmetry of knowledge converts into an asymmetry of influence, and both become concentrated in a handful of platforms. To the **panopticon**: a comparison useful precisely because of the difference — Zuboff shows what the new power *is not*, and thereby outlines it more precisely. To **data colonialism**: the extraction of experience as a continuation of the logic of enclosure and colonial extraction. And to the image of **AI as an extractive industry** — not as a mind but as an excavator working the human seam.

Zuboff's practical answer is not "adjust the privacy settings on your smartphone." Individual solutions are powerless against structural asymmetry; it is like fighting pollution by offering each person a breathing tube. What is needed is a *collective* answer: a right not to be extracted from, as a political rather than a consumer right; laws declaring behavioral surplus inalienable; and — what matters most to her — a restoration of the capacity for outrage. Her favorite thought: these practices seem inevitable only because we have grown used to them, and habit passes itself off as nature. "That's just how the internet works" is not a fact but someone's business model, congealed until it feels like the air itself.

The beast of surveillance capitalism is dangerous precisely for its coziness. It does not growl. It suggests, reminds, saves you a minute. And while techno-rationalist alignment awaits a monster from the future, this one has already curled up in everyone's pocket — warm, obliging, and watching very attentively to see where you will turn tomorrow.
