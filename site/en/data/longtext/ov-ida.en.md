### Iterated Amplification (Paul Christiano, 2018)

Imagine you have to evaluate a research paper at the cutting edge of a field in which you are no specialist. On your own you are helpless — you lack both the knowledge and the time. But you have a thousand assistants, each of whom knows exactly as much as you do and not a grain more. You split the task: one assistant checks the derivation of a formula on page seven, another cross-checks the citations, a third reads the section on the method and paraphrases its gist for you in three sentences. Each of them, faced with their subtask, is likewise free to recruit a thousand assistants of their own. And so, out of a swarm of equally limited beings, something emerges that can make judgments none of them could make alone. This is amplification. Iterated amplification is a recipe for turning this fantasy into a training signal for a neural network without losing alignment along the way.

**The mechanism.** The scheme alternates between two steps, hence its other name — IDA, Iterated Distillation and Amplification.

The first step is Amplify. Take the current model `M` and a human `H`. The human receives a hard question, breaks it into simpler subquestions, and hands those subquestions to copies of the model `M`. The human gathers the copies' answers together and issues a final answer. The resulting bundle `Amplify(H, M)` — a composite agent — is smarter and more reliable than a lone `M`: it has more "working hands" and human control over the decomposition. But it is monstrously slow: to answer a single question, you have to run a whole tree of delegation.

The second step is Distill. The slowness is cured by training: a new model `M'` is trained to imitate the answers of the expensive composite agent `Amplify(H, M)` — by ordinary supervised learning or via reinforcement learning on that signal. `M'` is fast, like a single network, but strives to reproduce the judgment of the whole swarm.

Now `M'` is put in place of `M`, and the cycle repeats: amplified — distilled — amplified — distilled. With each iteration both the composite agent and its distillate grow stronger. Christiano's key bet is that alignment is preserved at every step: if the human `H` is well-disposed, and the task decomposition does not smuggle in harmful assumptions, then the composite agent too stays aligned with human intentions, and the distillate inherits that alignment. Oversight scales beyond what a human can check directly — but it is built from the bottom up, out of steps a human can check.

The limiting case of this construction is the thought experiment HCH, "Humans consulting HCH." The definition is recursive to the point of vertigo: HCH is a human who, in order to answer a question, is permitted to ask subquestions… of other HCHs. An infinite tree of delegation unfolds, with identical humans with identically limited attention spans sitting at its nodes: an idealized limit of an infinitely amplified human. IDA can be read as an attempt to approach HCH with bounded computational means: real models instead of ideal human copies, finite depth instead of infinite.

**How it came about.** The idea ripened on Paul Christiano's blog ai-alignment.com in 2016–2017, when he was still formulating the general program of "alignment through amplification" and devised HCH as its theoretical lodestar. In October 2018 all of this took shape as the paper "Supervising strong learners by amplifying weak experts" (Christiano, Buck Shlegeris, Dario Amodei), coming out of OpenAI's safety team, together with the accompanying post on the OpenAI blog "Learning Complex Goals with Iterated Amplification." The paper did not merely describe the method but tested it on toy tasks like shortest-path search in a graph and computations over permutations — tasks that naturally break into subtasks and for which an algorithm can compute the correct answer to check against. It demonstrated the key point: where there is no direct training signal, amplification is able to synthesize one.

As for its lineage, IDA is one of the three main approaches in the "scalable oversight" program, which asks how a weak teacher can train a strong student on tasks the teacher cannot solve. Beside it is recursive reward modeling by Jan Leike and colleagues at DeepMind, where the same idea of recursion is applied to learning the reward function: helper agents are used to evaluate the behavior of the main agent. And beside it is debate by Geoffrey Irving, Christiano, and Amodei, which came a little earlier, in May 2018: there, truth beyond human reach is drawn out not by the cooperation of a tree of helpers but by the contest of two arguing models before a human judge. All three are attempts to answer one question with different geometries: amplification is cooperative and tree-shaped, debate is adversarial and linear, reward modeling recursively builds on top of evaluation.

And, finally, the practical descendant. RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback, which underlies modern chat models — is, in essence, a degenerate first step of IDA: the human evaluates answers directly, without amplification, with a tree depth of zero. IDA was conceived as an answer to the obvious limit of RLHF: what to do when a human can no longer evaluate an answer just by looking at it.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The chief open objection is the accumulation of errors. The delegation tree can run tens of levels deep, and at every joint there is a loss: distillation approximates the composite agent inexactly, the human breaks up the task imperfectly, the model's copies give noisy answers. Small errors, multiplied along long chains and dozens of iterations, risk not averaging out but resonating and diverging. We have no guarantee that the process converges to something sensible rather than drifting.

The second objection, a subtler one, concerns the alignment of the composite agent. The IDA thesis rests on the assumption that `Amplify(H, M)` stays aligned if `H` is aligned. But the composite agent is no longer that human. Splitting the task into isolated subquestions, you give each helper such a narrow window of context that they cease to see the whole picture. And a system assembled out of fragmentary judgments is capable of reaching a conclusion that not one of the participants would have approved had they seen it whole — a kind of bureaucracy without malice, but also without conscience. Whether the swarm stays aligned is not a theorem but a hypothesis.

The third is the question of factorizability (factored cognition). The whole method works only for tasks that really do break into subtasks. But does "human judgment" exist in decomposable form? Much of what we value — taste, intuition, moral appraisal — may turn out to be fundamentally non-factorizable, holistic. The Ought project set out to probe the boundaries of factorizable cognition empirically — and the results came out sobering rather than triumphant: in its 2020 experiments on factored evaluation of arguments, participants disagreed a lot about whether individual steps were valid.

And the fourth, down-to-earth one, is cost. Unfolding an amplification tree is orders of magnitude more expensive than direct evaluation. Christiano describes RLHF as a practical step toward amplification: the more complex schemes depend on the same technical ingredients, especially learning a reward function from an expensive overseer.

**What to do about it.** IDA is worth keeping in mind not as a finished product but as a generative idea — the source from which much of the modern vocabulary of scalable oversight grew. Three things are practically useful. First, the distillation of human task decomposition — a technique that migrated into chains of reasoning, into "model-as-critic" schemes, and into constitutional approaches. Second, the view of RLHF as the zeroth iteration: as soon as evaluating an answer is beyond a human's power, one will have to layer amplification, debate, or a reward model on top of the evaluator — and it is useful to understand in advance which geometry of recursion suits you. Third, the diagnostic question "does my task factorize?": if the target judgment does not disassemble into checkable pieces, neither IDA nor its relatives will save you, and it is more honest to admit this in advance.
