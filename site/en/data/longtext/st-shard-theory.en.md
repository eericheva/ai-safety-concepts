### Shard Theory (Alex Turner, Quintin Pope, et al., 2022)

Take a child who one day climbed into the cupboard, reached the sugar bowl — and got something sweet. The brain did not derive from this a "utility function of sugar." It learned something much cruder: *if there's a kitchen nearby, and you can see something sweet, and you're hungry — reach for it*. This is not a goal. It is an urge tied to a context. Bring the same person into a gym in the middle of a diet — and the urge stays silent, because there is no kitchen, and a completely different circuit has become active: *don't let down the people you train with*. Shard theory says you are made of contextual patches like these. There is no single "I, maximizing the good" under the hood — there is a coalition.

A shard is a learned circuit of behavior: the bundle "in context C, incline toward action/outcome A." It took hold not because someone built the right goal into you, but because in the past, *in this very context*, such behavior led to reward — and the reinforcement strengthened the internal computations that produced it. The key word is context. The shard "eat something sweet" and the shard "take care of a friend" do not argue about the meaning of life; they simply activate on different triggers and only sometimes collide at the same crossroads, where they have to bargain. A human in this picture is not a coherent optimizer but a parliament of urges, where a decision is born from their momentary balance of forces.

The classical approach asks: *what utility function should we set for the AI so that it is the right one?* Shard theory answers: you are asking the question from the wrong end. Reinforcement learning does not "optimize for the reward signal" — reward merely **sculpts** internal circuits, like a chisel, not like a goal the agent strives toward. (Turner put this as a separate thesis, which became almost a slogan: *reward is not the optimization target*.) So the task of alignment is not to find a magical specification of values but to understand and steer the process in which shards crystallize: which contexts are reinforced earlier, which urges manage to take hold and generalize, what they add up to by the end of training. Coherent maximization — if it arises at all — is a late, optional upshot of the self-organization of shards, not the starting point.

Here lies the hope that made many people love the theory. Human values, after all, took shape exactly this way: by a crooked, evolutionarily accidental process of reinforcement, without any prescribed utility function — and the result was, on the whole, not catastrophic. People care about others, about the future, about beings evolution "did not have in mind." If values arise from the formation of shards rather than from a specification, then we already have a working example in front of us showing that this path can lead somewhere decent. Not a guarantee — but an existence proof.

**How it came about.** The theory grew on the Alignment Forum and LessWrong in 2022 around Alex Turner (TurnTrout, then a researcher with a background in the theory of power-seeking and impact measures) and Quintin Pope. Their joint work "The shard theory of human values" (September 2022) became its programmatic text — a long essay that drew on the neurobiology of reward and on child development to derive a picture of values as an ensemble of contextual urges. It was preceded by Turner's much-discussed post "Reward is not the optimization target" (summer 2022), which overturned the usual intuition about RL, and by David Udell's survey "Shard Theory: An Overview." The intellectual backdrop matters too: the theory was born as an explicit counterargument to the school of Yudkowsky and the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI), where the default model of an agent was the coherent maximizer of expected utility, with instrumental convergence taken as inevitable. The shard theorists said: you mistook the limiting case for the norm — and the norm is messier and kinder.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and chief accusation is informality. The theory gives a rich language ("shard," "activation," "the bargaining of urges") but almost no formal predictions that could be falsified. Even Lawrence Chan, a sympathetic reader, noted in his December 2022 distillation and critical appraisal that the theory lacks a precise definition of a shard and makes few specific claims about how shards work. He also described several individual theses as uncontroversial, including the dependence of final values on early experience.

The second is the dispute over coherence. Opponents (in the spirit of the coherence-of-preferences arguments coming from Yudkowsky) insist: yes, *during* training the agent is a jumble of shards, but as capabilities grow, reflection and pressure toward consistency weed out the internal conflicts, and the system drifts toward something that *behaves* like a maximizer of a stable goal. Then the "coalition of shards" is only an early, transient phase, and the dangerous limit is exactly the one that was warned about. The shard theorists reply that coherence does not come from nowhere, and that the final goal, if it does take shape, will inherit the kindness of the early shards — but this is more a bet than a proven theorem.

The third is scalability. Even granting the picture for humans and today's RL agents, it is not obvious that it carries over to superintelligence: the analogy "it worked out with people, after all" may break down without the evolutionary context that produced human sociality and under optimization pressure orders of magnitude stronger. And the fourth, a quiet one: the theory's convenient optimism easily turns into a lullaby — "it will all sort itself out fine" — which, for a discipline that lives by expecting the worst case, sounds suspiciously pleasant.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical value of shard theory is not in a ready recipe but in changing the questions we ask. It nudges you to view training as sculpting values over time: to study which behavioral circuits take hold and in what order, how early shards influence the generalization of later ones, where exactly goal misalignment is born when a learned behavior is carried into a new context. This joins up directly with mechanistic interpretability (find the shards inside the network as actual circuits) and with the diagnosis of goal misgeneralization: the agent learned the wrong urge, because in the training contexts the right and wrong behavior coincided, and in a new one they diverged. Hence the interest in forming the needed shards deliberately: through the order and diversity of training situations, rather than through an attempt to write down the final utility function. For the practice of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), this means sobriety: we do not "convey values to the model" through reward, we sculpt contextual urges into it, urges that may behave beautifully on the training distribution and break down past its edge. A healthy reading of shard theory is not "relax, it will sort itself out" but "stop looking for the single right goal and start taking responsibility for the process in which goals come about." Even if in the limit coherence does win — we decide which shards it is assembled from, and we decide now.
