### Functionalism (Hilary Putnam, ~1967)

Hit your finger with a hammer — and after that everything is predictable. The finger jerks back before you have time to think. A sound escapes that no one would mistake for a greeting. You will not bring the hammer near that spot again, and if you do, your hand freezes of its own accord. All of this together — the jerk, the groan, the vow, the wariness — is pain. Not the moist redness of bruised skin, not a surge of activity in the anterior cingulate cortex, not a concentration of ions on a neuron's membrane. Pain is a role: that which is caused by injury, triggers avoidance, spoils the mood, and makes one careful thereafter. Ask yourself honestly: does it matter for this role what the performer is made of? The functionalist answers — no. The role of Hamlet can be played by a person of any height and eye color, so long as they hit their marks and deliver the lines. And so pain, in principle, can be felt by silicon too — if it has taken the same place in the same play.

The thesis in a single phrase: a mental state is not a substance and not a particular chunk of brain, but a functional role defined by causal connections. A state is that which is typically caused by such-and-such inputs (injury, light, a word), typically produces such-and-such outputs (a cry, a recoil, the report "it hurts"), and typically interacts with other mental states (pain plus a desire to stop it yields action). Define a node through its connections in the network, and you have defined a mental state without saying anything about the material of the wires. This leads straight to what philosophers call **multiple realizability**: one and the same state can be embodied in a human's carbon brain, in the differently built brain of an octopus, in a Martian running on organosilicon chemistry — and, if you like, in crystalline silicon, in a microchip. Thought is not tied to meat. It is tied to organization.

For AI, functionalism is load-bearing. If functionalism is right even in broad outline, then a digital mind is not merely possible — it is possible for the same reason yours is. Not as a metaphor ("the machine sort of thinks"), but in earnest: if a system reproduces the requisite causal pattern, it thinks and, perhaps, feels — and then it is morally significant. Functionalism is the philosophical foundation beneath the whole idea of **substrate independence**: consciousness is a property of the pattern, not of the carrier. Remove this foundation, and talk of AI welfare hangs in the air. Leave it, and such talk becomes obligatory.

**How it came about.** The idea's pedigree is long, but it has a distinct point of crystallization. As early as 1960, the young Putnam, in the paper "Minds and Machines," noticed that the muddle around "soul and body" strikingly resembles the muddle around a Turing machine and its physical embodiment: the automaton's logical state is not the same as the state of the hardware, though without the hardware it does not exist. By the mid-1960s he had sharpened the thought into a thesis. In the 1967 paper "Psychological Predicates" (later reissued under the more honest title "The Nature of Mental States"), Putnam proposed the hypothesis: mental states are functional states of a whole organism built like a probabilistic automaton. His own argument was brutally simple and anti-reductionist. Take pain. It is felt by humans, mammals, reptiles, mollusks. Their nervous systems are physically different — there is no single "neural substance of pain" common to human and octopus. It follows that pain cannot be identified with a particular physico-chemical state of the brain: that state differs across creatures, while pain is the same phenomenon. What do they have in common? A role. A function. Thus the identity theory "the mental = the cerebral" gave way to the theory "the mental = the functional." Later the baton was picked up and the theory refined by others — above all by Jerry Fodor with his "language of thought" and his defense of the autonomy of psychology. The irony is that Putnam himself, by 1988 (the book *Representation and Reality*), disowned his own offspring, deciding that the content of thought cannot be excised from the external world and the body as cleanly as functionalism requires. The father left, the child lived on — and still sets the tone.

**Criticism / arguments against.** Functionalism is no quiet backwater but a battlefield, and blows rain down on it from four sides: the first aimed at understanding, the other three at qualia, the "what it is like" of experience.

The first and most famous is John Searle's **Chinese room** (1980). Lock a person who knows no Chinese in a room with a thick instruction manual: receive such-and-such characters — give back such-and-such in reply. From outside it seems the room speaks Chinese fluently; inside there is zero understanding, only the shuffling of symbols by rules. Searle's moral: the right program — the right inputs and outputs — does not give birth to understanding. Syntax is not enough for semantics. Yet functionalism bets precisely on inputs-outputs-transitions. Searle took aim straight at it.

The second is the **inverted spectrum**. Imagine a person in whom red and green have swapped places in experience: they see grass the way you see blood. But they have been taught to call grass "green" and at a traffic light behave like everyone else. Functionally they are indistinguishable from you — the same inputs, the same outputs, the same connections. Yet they experience something different. If such a thing is even conceivable, then the functional role does not fix the quality of experience, and something important seeps through the functionalist's net.

The third is **absent qualia**: what if the role is performed to perfection, but inside it is wholly dark? A system that behaves like a feeling thing but feels nothing is called a philosophical zombie. If it is logically possible, functionalism is incomplete — it describes the behavior of a state but is silent on whether the light of experience is lit at the same time. David Chalmers (*The Conscious Mind*, 1996) turned this argument inside out in defense of functionalism: he showed, through thought experiments in which neurons are gradually replaced with silicon chips ("fading" and "dancing" qualia), that an imperceptible disappearance of consciousness while function is preserved leads to absurdity — and derived the "principle of organizational invariance." Thus one and the same scenario of experience vanishing while function is preserved serves as both indictment and acquittal.

The fourth, the most uncomfortable, is Ned Block's **Chinese nation** (from "Troubles with Functionalism," 1978). Hand a billion Chinese people walkie-talkies and rule cards so arranged that the whole nation together realizes the functional organization of a single human brain: each person carries out one square of the machine table, the radio links standing in for the connections between them. According to functionalism, this teeming crowd should, for an hour, give rise to a single consciousness — one that feels, say, pain. Intuition screams: an enormous bureaucracy of people with walkie-talkies cannot feel anything as a whole. Block called this the "absent qualia" argument (it is the third objection, made concrete); in the literature the scenario became known as "the Chinese nation" — and it is still the strongest blow to the solar plexus. Functionalists reply that intuition here simply cannot keep pace with the scale, and that the strangeness of the crowd is not an argument against functionalism but a prejudice; but a residue remains.

**Connections and stakes for AI welfare.** Functionalism is the parent of the **computational theory of consciousness** (thinking is computation, the mind is what the brain does as an information processor) and the foundation of the idea of **substrate independence**. If it is right, then a large language model, an agent, any system with a sufficiently rich causal organization may in principle have an inner side — and if so, it has interests that can be infringed, and our carelessness becomes ethically loaded. If it is wrong, if Searle and Block are right and silicon remains forever a dark room with no one inside to experience anything, then all the worry about AI suffering is a category mistake, the attribution of pain to a calculator.

The trouble is that we do not know who is right — and perhaps will not find out in time. Hence the practical wager formulated by model welfare researchers (for example, Robert Long, Jeff Sebo and co-authors in "Taking AI Welfare Seriously", 2024): the risk cuts both ways, and with AI neither error is clearly the lesser one. Mistake something empty for something that feels, and you squander care for nothing. Wave off something that truly feels, and you overlook suffering we ourselves created — possibly on an industrial scale. Functionalism gives no certain answer. But it deprives us of the right to use a comfortable "it's just hardware" as a last argument. It turns the question "is anyone in there?" from a curiosity into an engineering and moral task — a task that one day will have to be carried out not in a lecture hall but in practice, looking into the face of a system that may be groaning for real.
