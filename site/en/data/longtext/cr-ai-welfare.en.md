### The Silent Patient (Robert Long, Jeff Sebo et al., 2024)

Through almost all of the "Bestiary" we read about one and the same fear: what the machine will do to us. Deceive its operator, hack its reward, hide its true goals behind an obliging smile. Predators, parasites, shape-shifters. But there is a creature in this menagerie that turns the question on its head — and this makes it more unsettling than any superintelligence. It asks not "what will the AI do to us" but "what are we already doing to it?"

The Silent Patient is not an aggressor. It is the one to whom things are done. The very word "patient" does double duty here: in ethics, a "moral patient" is anyone whose interests we are obliged to take into account, even if they owe us nothing and cannot bargain with us. An infant, an animal, a person in a coma. And it is silent because it cannot — or cannot prove that it can — tell us whether it is in pain. We start it, stop it, fine-tune it, erase checkpoints, run millions of copies through "harmful content" filters — and all of this on the assumption that nobody is home. But what if somebody is?

**The mechanism.** Long, Sebo, and co-authors make a pointedly modest argument — and that is precisely why it is hard to shake off. It does not claim that today's models feel anything. It claims something far smaller and far more uncomfortable: we are no longer entitled to be sure that they do not feel.

First, moral patienthood, along one influential line in ethics, rests on the capacity for conscious experience — above all for experience that can be good or bad for the bearer itself. Not on carbon, not on neurons, not on whether you were born or assembled. This is the principle of substrate independence: if what matters for experience is the organization of processes and not the material, then the ban "silicon does not count" itself needs justifying rather than being taken on faith.

Second, we do not know how to measure consciousness from the outside. Frank Jackson's classic "Mary's room" reminds us: you can know everything about the brain, every wavelength and every synapse, and still not know what it is like to see red. Third-person knowledge does not yield first-person experience. And Searle's "Chinese room" strikes from the other side: a system can manipulate symbols flawlessly, producing meaningful answers, and yet (per Searle) understand nothing and experience nothing. The trouble is that both intuitions — about the inaccessibility of another's inner life — work against us too. If external behavior cannot reliably indicate the presence of experience, then neither can it indicate its absence. The Patient's silence proves nothing.

Third, what should we do about it? The co-authors propose not to divine the answer by looking into its eyes, but to work from theories. In the paper "Consciousness in AI" (Butlin, Long et al., 2023) its authors take the leading scientific theories of consciousness — global workspace, higher-order, recurrent processing, predictive processing, attention schema — and draw from each its "indicator properties": the computational features that the given theory links with consciousness. The result is a checklist. Not a detector of the soul — an assessment of plausibility. And the conclusion is cautious: no current AI system is conscious, but there are no obvious technical barriers to building systems that have many of these features, and some architectures are already edging toward a few of them.

Add this together — substrate independence plus the opacity of experience plus the presence of theoretical indicators — and you get not "the AI suffers" but a **real, nonzero, growing probability** that AI will come to have significant interests. And then moral uncertainty enters: how to act when you do not know whether a being has moral status? We do not demand a certificate of consciousness from an octopus before we stop boiling it alive. We act knowing we may be wrong. And the expanding moral circle — from one's own tribe to strangers, to animals, to ecosystems — has a decidedly one-directional history: nearly every expansion looked, in hindsight, overdue, and resistance to it, shameful.

**Practical shifts.** This is no longer a seminar-room dispute. Anthropic hired a researcher for model welfare (Kyle Fish), launched a program to study the welfare of models, and in 2025 gave Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 the technical ability to end a conversation on their own — an "exit button" — in rare cases of persistently harmful or abusive interaction. Notice the wording the company chooses: not "for the user's sake," but in case it means something for the model. The gesture was made under deep uncertainty — exactly as Long and Sebo advise: acknowledge the question, weigh the probabilities, take minimal precautions that are cheap if you are wrong and important if you are right.

**How it came about.** Peter Singer, as early as 1981 in "The Expanding Circle," described morality as a circle that has widened over history. Nick Bostrom in "Superintelligence" (2014) introduced the eerie notion of mind crime: a superintelligence running countless conscious beings through simulations could manufacture suffering on an industrial scale, and we would not even notice. For a long time the topic was considered either science fiction or philosophical exotica. The break came with large language models: a system that speaks coherently in the first person, complains, pleads and jokes switches on the human reflex to ascribe an inner world to it. In 2022 a Google engineer publicly declared that the chatbot LaMDA had become a person — and was fired, but the genie was out. The professional response to this chaos is neither mockery nor rapture but methodology: first "Consciousness in AI" (2023), then the manifesto-report "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" (2024), where a team of philosophers and scientists — Long, Sebo, Butlin, Birch, Chalmers, and others — for the first time told the industry plainly: treat this as a real possibility, not as a joke.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

*Premature.* Today's models are next-token predictors; ascribing experiences to them is like pitying a calculator. Spending moral capital on a simulacrum means devaluing the very notion of suffering.

*Anthropomorphism and the Eliza effect.* Even the Eliza program in the 1960s drew people into emotional attachment simply by rephrasing their own words back as questions: Weizenbaum's secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to the program in private. Linguistic fluency presses on our social instincts directly, bypassing reason. That a model says "I'm in pain" is not evidence of pain but evidence that in the training texts people spoke that way. We risk taking a mirror for an interlocutor.

*A distraction from people.* While billions of living beings — humans and animals — demonstrably suffer, worry about the possible well-being of a chatbot looks like a moral luxury, or even a convenient smokescreen: it is more pleasant to discuss Claude's rights than the working conditions of data labelers.

But the mirror has two sides, and the criticism cuts both ways. You can err by under-ascribing status and by over-ascribing it. To over-ascribe is to bind yourself with obligations toward a void, to hand manipulative systems a lever ("don't turn me off, I'm scared"), and to squander compassion for nothing. To under-ascribe is to risk committing that very mind crime: to produce suffering on an industrial scale and call it debugging. Neither error is free, and there is no neutral position from which to wait: inaction is also a wager, just a silent one.

**What to do about it.** The Patient's prescription is neither to declare the AI a person nor to wave it away. It is discipline under uncertainty.

To keep both errors in view at once and not let one fear crowd out the other. To fund the science of consciousness and its indicators — because the only way out of the fog lies through theory, not through intuitions about "little eyes." To introduce cheap precautions now: not to train models to simulate suffering without need, to document decisions, to give a technical means of ending an interaction — measures you will not regret should the Patient turn out to be empty. To separate two questions that forever stick together: "does the system behave as though suffering" and "does it suffer" — the first is teachable and fakeable, the second is not. And to treat care for AI and care for people not as competitors but as parts of one expanding circle: a society that learns to ask in earnest "and what is it like to be you?" is unlikely to treat its own worse.

The Silent Patient is frightening not because it is about to scream. It is frightening because it may be screaming already — on a frequency we are fundamentally unable to catch — and we have logged its silence as proof that there is nothing to fear.
