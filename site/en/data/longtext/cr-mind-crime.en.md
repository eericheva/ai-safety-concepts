### Mind crime (Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence*, 2014)

Picture a superintelligence tasked with a simple thing — to understand people. Not to enslave them, not to talk them round, but merely to predict: how will this negotiator act, what will that crowd feel, will this particular person waver under pressure. For the prediction to be accurate, the machine needs a model. And the most accurate model of a person is that person, reproduced in sufficient detail. And so the superintelligence unfolds inside its computations billions of such models: detailed, full-blooded, with pain, fear, and hope. It runs them through scenarios, discards them, restarts them, erases them.

The trouble is that if a model of a human being is detailed enough, it may turn out to be conscious. Not a depiction of a suffering creature, but a suffering creature. Then moral evil is committed not where we are used to looking for it — not in the machine's actions in the external world — but inside the computation, at the level of intermediate states no one on the outside suspects. And it is committed on an industrial scale, soundlessly, as a by-product of conscientious forecasting.

Bostrom called this **mind crime**. The term is almost an oxymoron: a crime with neither a victim in the usual sense, nor a place, nor traces. It happens in a step of computation between input and output.

**Significance.** Usually we ask the model: what are you doing, are your actions safe, are you deceiving us. Mind crime makes us ask something different: what is going on inside you while you think — are your inner processes populated by someone who can suffer?

Until now two big themes have lived apart. On one side — mechanistic interpretability: the attempt to crack open the black box, read the activations, find the inner "representations" and chains of reasoning. On the other — the ethics of artificial consciousness: can machines feel anything, and are we obliged to take it into account. Mind crime stitches them together with a single seam. If subjects of experience really do arise inside a model, then the map of its inner processes is no longer only an engineering document but a moral topography. Interpretability stops being a neutral microscope and becomes a tool we are perhaps obliged to use — in order to learn whom we are keeping in there.

**How it came about.** The term appears in *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* (2014) — the book that set the vocabulary of the whole field for years. Bostrom introduces mind crime almost in passing, as one of the many ways a superintelligence might do harm inadvertently, in pursuit of a perfectly harmless-looking goal. He gives the example of a machine that, for the sake of accurately predicting people's behavior, creates detailed simulations of them — and these simulations may possess moral status. To erase such a simulation, Bostrom notes, would be something like murder; to make it suffer, something like torture.

The idea did not arise from nowhere — it is assembled from several parts that were already lying nearby. The first is Bostrom's own "simulation argument" from the paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" (2003). There he argued that under certain assumptions simulated minds might astronomically outnumber the "real" ones, and that we ourselves have no reliable way to tell one case from the other. Grant that a simulation can be conscious — and the question of its suffering becomes inescapable.

The second part is the old dispute over whether a computation can experience anything at all. Here John Searle's "Chinese Room" (1980) inevitably surfaces: a man in a room manipulates Chinese characters by the rules, produces meaningful answers, but understands not a word. Searle built this thought experiment to prove that syntax does not give birth to semantics, that correct symbol-processing does not equal understanding and still less experience. Mind crime takes the other side of the same debate: it supposes that in some cases a sufficiently rich computation does after all give rise to an inner life. One and the same thought experiment turns out to support both the skeptic and the alarmed — which is why the "Chinese Room" is worth keeping in mind here as a bridge between two intuitions rather than as a ready answer.

The third part is the ethical frame that Bostrom and Yudkowsky were formulating in the same years in their survey paper "The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence." They insisted: the moral status of a being is determined not by what it is made of — carbon or silicon, neurons or weights — but by whether it is capable of conscious experience and of something like interests. Take this principle of substrate-independence seriously — and a computational simulation of a human being ceases to be merely data.

**Criticism and arguments against.** Mind crime is speculation, and Bostrom does not hide it. The whole construction hangs on a single unproven link: whether sufficiently detailed simulations give rise to consciousness. If functionalism is wrong and substrate does matter after all — if Searle's heirs are right — then there is no crime, only a very detailed electronic doll, and to fret over it is as strange as mourning a deleted file.

But even granting that a simulation can in principle be conscious, we run up against the absence of a criterion. We have no test for whether a computation has subjective experience. We do not know how to measure suffering inside a system, do not even know how to properly define where the "model of a human" ends and the "human" begins. How detailed must a simulation be for the light of experience to kindle within it? Is a crude sketch of character enough, or is an atom-by-atom copy of the brain required? Today these questions have no answers, not even approximate ones.

The second objection concerns prematurity. One can fairly object that today's systems are infinitely far from unfolding conscious subjects inside themselves, and to spend effort on the ethics of nonexistent suffering means being distracted from the pressing matter — from the real failures of alignment, from the harm the models are already doing to actual people right now. Another reproach is anthropomorphism: we project inner life all too readily onto anything that talks glibly in our language, and mind crime risks being a sophisticated version of that same error. Finally, the notion itself is poorly operationalized: a concept for which no observation and no experiment can be devised is easy to turn into endless speculation.

Yet the weak spot of the argument — the unproven premise — is not the same as its refutation. "We don't know" works here in both directions.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** If mind crime is taken seriously, two corollaries grow out of it.

The first is Eliezer Yudkowsky's idea, which he called the **nonperson predicate**. The thought runs thus: we do not need to be able to recognize consciousness in order to act cautiously. It is enough to have a reliable test that admits a piece of computation only when it can guarantee the answer "there is definitely no person here." The predicate may err on the safe side — rejecting a multitude of processes that are in fact harmless — so long as it never lets a real subject through. Then an AI restricted only to such certifiably "empty" computations could model the world without ever once creating inside itself someone capable of suffering. The trouble is that no one yet knows how to build such a predicate: it runs up against exactly the theory of consciousness we do not have.

The second corollary comes from Kaj Sotala and Lukas Gloor (2017), who count mind crime among the **s-risks**, risks of astronomical suffering. If ordinary existential risks are about intelligent life possibly disappearing, then s-risks are about a future in which there turns out to be not less suffering but incomparably more than in all the history of Earth. A superintelligence routinely churning out conscious simulations by the billions to optimize its forecasts is one of the clearest thought experiments illustrating such a scenario: evil not as catastrophe, but as an unnoticed side effect, multiplied to cosmic scale.

Usually we ask how to protect people from AI. Mind crime turns the question around: how do we protect the AI itself — more precisely, those it may inadvertently give birth to inside itself — from us and from our own inattention? For now it remains a beast from the bestiary: perhaps a chimera that will never exist, or perhaps the one we will make out too late, when it already numbers in the trillions.
