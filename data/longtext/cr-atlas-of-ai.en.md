### Earth (Kate Crawford, "Atlas of AI", 2021)

Begin with a gesture each of us makes a dozen times a day: you say "Okay" to a smart speaker, and a voice from the plastic cylinder answers. It seems the exchange is happening with something weightless — with "the cloud," with "intelligence," with a disembodied entity living in the wires. Kate Crawford sets out to trace what stands behind that second. Behind it: the lithium mines of Clayton Valley in Nevada; the mines of Inner Mongolia, where rare-earth ore is dissolved in acids to yield neodymium and the waste runs off into a lake of poisonous sludge; transoceanic cables; data centers drinking the water of whole cities; warehouses laid out so that a person moves along a trajectory set by an algorithm; and millions of invisible people who, for pennies, labeled images so that the speaker could learn to understand you at all. Hence the book's central thesis, delivered almost as a battle cry: **artificial intelligence is neither artificial nor intelligent**. It is man-made down to the last screw, rooted in the earth's depths, in bodies and in capital — and it does not so much think as classify and distribute power.

"Atlas of AI" is an atlas in the literal sense: the book leads the reader through layers, as through geological strata. Earth — lithium, cobalt, rare earths, gigawatts, and water; AI appears as a continuation of the nineteenth-century mining industry, not a break with it. Labor — the ghostly workers of labeling and moderation, the workers of Amazon warehouses whose pace is set by the machine; "automation" often turns out to be the shifting of manual labor onto those who cannot be seen. Data — expropriation: faces from police mugshot databases, photos from social networks, other people's texts, gathered without asking and turned into "raw material." Classification — how training sets like ImageNet stitch racial, gender, and class prejudices into models under the guise of neutral truth. Affect — the pseudoscience of reading emotions off the face, heir to the physiognomy of Duchenne and Ekman. And finally, State — the militarized and corporate interests for which AI is an instrument of surveillance and power. Crawford's key move is to show that all of this is not "side effects" but the very construction. AI is a **register of power**, not a technical detail.

Hence the metaphor of extraction. Crawford insists: AI is a planetary extractive industry. It extracts minerals from the earth, energy from the grids, labor from bodies, data from biographies, attention from users — and concentrates what it takes in the hands of a handful of corporations and states. The word "extraction" binds together things usually discussed separately — the climate cost of training models, the ghost labor of the labelers, data colonialism, surveillance capitalism: all of them turn out to be facets of a single process. Here Crawford converges with a whole critical-humanities canon — with Gray and Suri's "Ghost Work," with Couldry and Mejias's "data colonialism," with Zuboff's "surveillance capitalism" — and adds to it the material, geological dimension they often lacked.

**How it came about.** The book grew out of years of fieldwork. Crawford is a researcher at the intersection of law, media, and computer science, a co-founder of the AI Now Institute at New York University, which has assembled an evidence base on the harms of algorithmic systems since the mid-2010s. The direct predecessor of the "Atlas" is the 2018 visual study "Anatomy of an AI System," made by Crawford together with the artist Vladan Joler: a giant schematic map unfolding a single Amazon Echo into a planetary chain — from lithium in the Bolivian salt flats to an electronic waste dump. This map hung in MoMA and the V&A and became the visual manifesto of the book to come. "Atlas of AI" appeared from Yale University Press in 2021, at exactly the moment when the industry was telling the opposite story about itself — about "the cloud," "magic," and inevitable progress. Crawford wrote the book as a counter-atlas: not a map of what AI can do, but a map of what it costs and whom it serves.

**Criticism / arguments against.** Even sympathetic reviewers note several vulnerabilities. First, the "not intelligent" thesis works as a rhetorical device rather than an argument: having defined intelligence so that machines do not fall under it, one can easily declare victory, but in doing so one may overlook the real and growing capabilities of systems that will still have to be dealt with. Second, the lens of extraction is universal almost to the point of vacuity: any industry — from textiles to pharmaceuticals — "extracts" minerals, labor, and energy; if it extracts everything, then the metaphor obscures rather than reveals the specificity of AI in particular. Third, determinism and a shortage of ways out: the book diagnoses brilliantly but offers almost no cure, and the frame of "power through and through" risks depriving the reader of agency, leaving only the gesture of exposure. Fourth, important for our bestiary: Crawford directly waves away the agenda of superintelligence and existential risk as an illusion that distracts from far more pressing questions — and here the two canons diverge. The rationalist camp will object: material harms are real, but they do not cancel the alignment problem, they add to it; a powerful system trained on expropriated data is dangerous both as an instrument of power and as a misaligned optimizer — these are not competing but nested problems. Finally, the empirics are sometimes charged with being selective: the vivid cases (emotion recognition, ImageNet) are real, but the generalization from them to "AI in general" sometimes outpaces what the data can actually support.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The value of "Atlas of AI" for anyone thinking about safety is that it widens what counts as the question "where is the harm?" Technical alignment by default treats the model as an object in a vacuum: goal, training signal, behavior. Crawford brings back into frame what supplies that vacuum — the mine, the data center, the labeler, the legal regime of expropriation. Practically, several things follow. Count the full cost: not only accuracy and compute but water, carbon, rare earths, and human labor in the chain — a habit already normalized by work like Strubell and colleagues' paper on the energy cost of training. Disentangle "automation" from the disappearance of labor: where it seems the machine decides, it is worth asking whose hands feed it and on what terms. Treat training data as a political act: whose faces, whose texts, with whose consent — a question not only of privacy but of power. And keep in mind that "safe" AI is not only a model that hatches no schemes but also an institution that does not let resources and surveillance concentrate beyond control. The best reading in the bestiary is not "either Crawford or alignment," but a stereo pair: one lens looks inside the model, the other at the planet that bore it. A true map of danger needs both.
