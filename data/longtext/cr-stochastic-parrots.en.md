### The Stochastic Parrot (Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, Mitchell, 2021)

Picture a bird that flawlessly reproduces human speech — intonation, pauses, even a sigh in the right place — but has no access whatsoever to what it is talking about. It stitches sounds together by probability: this set of forms is statistically more often followed by that one. The result is smooth, at times unnervingly apt — and yet empty inside. Emily Bender and her co-authors put precisely this image in the title of a paper that became one of the most cited texts in the history of AI criticism. A large language model, by their formulation, is a "stochastic parrot": a system that, at random but with an eye to frequencies, stitches together sequences of linguistic forms observed in gigantic corpora, **without reference to any meaning**. We, the readers, assemble a coherent message out of its output — but the assembly happens in our heads, not in the model.

The thesis sounds almost insultingly simple, and therein lies its force. It strikes not at particulars but at the load-bearing beam of the whole narrative: as though, by growing parameters and data, we were climbing a staircase toward understanding. No, say the authors, the staircase leads elsewhere. Form is not meaning. A model trained to predict the next token optimizes the plausibility of text, not truth or intent. What we take for a glimmer of mind is our own ineradicable habit of reading a subject into any speech that sounds coherent. We are programmed to find an interlocutor.

But it would be a caricature to reduce the paper to the single phrase about the "parrot." The text beneath the parrot emoji in the title is in fact a list of several risks, and "no understanding" is not even the first item on it.

The first — **ecology and cost**. Training ever-larger models devours energy and leaves a carbon footprint, and the benefits of these expenditures accrue not to the communities that pay the climate price. The authors proposed counting these costs as part of scientific honesty, rather than bracketing them out.

The second — **absorbed biases**. A model trained on a slice of the internet inherits its skews: racist, sexist, ableist patterns, an overabundance of some groups' voices and the silence of others. And the larger the corpus, the stronger the illusion that it is "neutral" and "represents everyone" — when in fact it fixes the linguistic status quo of those who are privileged, who write more, and who are indexed more.

The third — **data documentability**. Hence the famous question from the subtitle: "Can Language Models Be Too Big?" Corpora have grown so large that it is already physically impossible to survey, document, or audit them. And that means it is impossible to answer responsibly for what is inside. Dataset documentation (by then already formalized as Gebru's "datasheets" and Bender's "data statements," both 2018) turns from a bureaucratic formality into an ethical requirement.

And only against this backdrop does the fourth motif sound — **the illusion of meaning**. The danger is not that the parrot is stupid, but that it is convincing. Fluent, authoritative, always-ready text behind which there is neither responsibility nor communicative intent — the perfect fuel for disinformation and for the erosion of trust in language as such.

The story surrounding the paper turned out to be no less loud than the paper itself. Timnit Gebru — co-lead of Google's AI ethics team — submitted the text to the FAccT conference in late 2020. Google's internal review demanded either that the paper be withdrawn or that the names of company employees be removed. Gebru requested an explanation and set conditions; in response, by her account, the company took her letter as a resignation and cut off her access ahead of schedule. Google insisted it had merely accepted her resignation. A few months later Google fired Margaret Mitchell, the second co-author from the company, as well — after an internal investigation. The scandal became a symbol of the conflict between corporate research labs and the independence of criticism: can one study the harm of a technology while on the payroll of those who sell it? The parrot went from a metaphor to the banner of an entire movement.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The most serious reply came from proponents of emergent abilities. In 2022 a group of researchers (Wei and others) described *emergent abilities*: skills — arithmetic, multi-step reasoning, transliteration from the phonetic alphabet — entirely absent in small models but arising in a jump past a certain scale threshold. If a system can do what it was not directly taught and what does not reduce to simple memorization, then calling it "just a parrot" is to look at 2021 through 2021's eyes. Opponents pointed out: the metaphor is static, while the models grow. What looked like a stitching-together of forms on GPT-2 already passes theory-of-mind tests on the next generations of models, solves olympiad problems, and generalizes to unseen combinations.

Hence a still-unresolved philosophical dispute: "do LLMs understand anything at all?" Some (in the spirit of Bender, and before her John Searle with his "Chinese Room") hold that manipulation of symbols without reference to the world and without "grounding" of meaning cannot, by definition, be understanding — scale the form however you like, it will remain form. Others counter that "understanding" is not a binary switch nor a sacred human privilege: if a system's behavior is functionally indistinguishable from understanding across an ever-wider class of tasks, then to insist on the emptiness within is already a matter of faith, not of empirics. The dispute over "real" meaning risks becoming irrefutable — and therefore, by Popper's lights, suspect.

It is worth honestly acknowledging, too, how the argument has **aged**. Part of the empirical criticism turned out to cut both ways: work by Schaeffer and co-authors (2023) showed that many "emergent jumps" are an artifact of sharp, threshold metrics, and that under smooth measurement abilities grow gradually. This, paradoxically, plays rather into the hands of the emergence skeptics — but neither does it resurrect the original parrot: the gradual growth of real skills from "zero" doesn't disappear. The 2021 metaphor foresaw neither code models nor agents nor reasoning systems — and a conscientious reader is bound to ask where the line runs between sober criticism of hype and denial of what already works.

And yet it would be dishonest to write the paper off. Its ecological and ethical theses have not dated by a single day; the question of data documentability has only grown sharper with the growth of corpora; and the very concept of "bullshit" (in Harry Frankfurt's sense — speech indifferent to truth) describes the behavior of hallucinating models perhaps even more precisely than in 2021. The parrot erred in its forecast about the ceiling of abilities — but it was right that fluency and truthfulness are not the same thing.

**Legacy and connections.**

"Stochastic parrot" became a folk term — the American Dialect Society named it "word of the year" 2023 in the AI-lexicon category. The metaphor is genealogically linked to Searle's **Chinese Room**: in both, syntax without semantics, the manipulation of signs without access to meaning. It is also a direct continuation of Bender and Koller's line about the "octopus," which eavesdrops on conversations over an undersea cable and learns to reply without ever having seen the islands or the things being talked about: a test of whether meaning can be learned from form alone.

Along another axis the parrot joins the critique of **algorithmic bias** — Gebru's own work and Joy Buolamwini's on how systems encode and amplify discrimination. And in later texts by Gebru and Émile Torres the metaphor grows into a critique of the ideological bundle **TESCREAL** (transhumanism, extropianism, singularitarianism, cosmism, rationalism, effective altruism, longtermism) — a worldview within which talk of "superintelligence" and "extinction from AI" serves, in the authors' view, as a distraction from present-day harm here and now. Finally, the parrot echoes the engineering answer to the same problem — Yann LeCun's **JEPA** architecture: if token prediction gives no grounding, then perhaps the machine should be taught to predict not forms but abstract representations of the world. The critique of form without meaning here turns into a constructive program.

The parrot still sits on the shoulder of the whole industry. Sometimes it repeats foolishly — and we laugh. Sometimes it utters something so apt that we freeze: what if it does understand after all? The value of the paper by Bender and colleagues is not that it gave a final answer, but that it taught us to ask the right question — and not to look away from the costs while we admire that answer.
