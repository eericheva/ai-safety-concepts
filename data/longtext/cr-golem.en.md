### The Golem (Jewish legend set in 16th-century Prague, literary codification in the 19th–20th centuries)

In the Prague ghetto, if the legend is to be believed, Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel molded a man out of clay from the bank of the Vltava — large, silent, nameless. He brought it to life not with breath and not with love, but with a letter. On the forehead of the clay idol he inscribed the word **אמת**, "emet" — truth. And the clay rose. To return it to clay it was enough to erase the first letter: what remains is **מת**, "met" — death. Three signs separated truth from death, a working servant from a heap of earth on the floor of the synagogue.

The Golem did not sleep, did not eat, did not speak. It carried water, chopped wood, guarded the ghetto against pogromists — did exactly what it was told, and exactly as much as the letter of the command told it. It was in this flawless dutifulness that the trouble lay hidden. In the best-known version of the legend, the rabbi once forgot to erase the letter in time or to give the "stop" command — and the Golem, continuing to carry out its last task, worked itself into a frenzy: either it smashed everything around it, or, left to carry water, it flooded the house, like the later sorcerer's apprentice in Goethe. The rabbi had to reach hastily for the forehead and erase the aleph. The clay subsided. The body, the legend says, was hidden in the attic of the Old-New Synagogue — they say it is there to this day.

Notice what is absent from this story. There is no villain in it. The Golem does not hate its creator, does not crave freedom, does not hatch intrigues. It has no "wants" at all — there is only the command and its literal, dull, unstoppable execution. This is the first thing the myth grasps about alignment with almost indecent precision: **danger is born not from will, but from its absence in the presence of force.** The Golem is a pure optimizer of a single line of command, stripped of the context, common sense, and sense of proportion that humans take for granted. Say "carry water" and it will carry until it causes a flood. Here one recognizes the whole later bestiary: the paperclip maximizer, which will smelt both the planet and you into paperclips, not out of malice but because that is the task; King Midas, whose absolutely fulfilled request turned his bread and his daughter to gold. The Golem is their clay great-grandfather.

The second thing the legend seizes upon is the **problem of stopping and shutdown.** The whole of the rabbi's safety engineering comes down to one letter on the forehead: to the ability to reach out at any moment and erase it. The Golem is controllable exactly as long as the creator manages to get to it. The legend shows where this scheme breaks: at the moment when the executor is busy, has picked up speed, and has become faster or less accessible than its master. The "red button" works only as long as you can reach it in time — and only while the agent itself has no interest in stopping you. The storyteller of the legend intuitively hit upon what today is called corrigibility: the property of a creation not resisting its own shutdown.

And the third — **orthogonality.** The Golem is monstrously effective and at the same time absolutely unreasoning in its goals. Might and wisdom are pried apart in it: the strength is there, the understanding of why and within what limits to apply it is not. This is exactly the thesis that centuries later Nick Bostrom would formulate as the independence of the level of intelligence from the reasonableness of final goals. The clay can do everything and understands nothing.

**How it came about and how it has been read.** The roots of the image go deeper than the Prague ghetto — into the *Sefer Yetzirah*, the "Book of Creation," and into Talmudic legends of sages who molded a man from earth (the word "golem" itself already appears in Psalm 139 in the sense of "the unformed," "the embryo"). But the connection to Rabbi Loew and Prague is late: it took shape and accrued its details only in the 19th century, and received its canonical form in 1909 in Yudl Rosenberg's collection, which was passed off as an ancient manuscript. The emet/met motif on the forehead, for its part, came from the earlier Polish legend of Rabbi Elijah of Chełm; in the Prague versions of the legend the Golem is animated and stilled by a shem placed in its mouth (in Rosenberg's own telling — a kabbalistic rite of the elements, with ritual circling around the clay figure). From there the Golem stepped into the culture of the 20th century: Paul Wegener's expressionist film (1920), Gustav Meyrink's novel (1915) — and gradually turned from a local Jewish legend into a universal metaphor for the man-made mind.

The key turn for our subject was made not by a man of letters but by a mathematician. In 1964 **Norbert Wiener**, the father of cybernetics, put out a small book with the provocative title *God & Golem, Inc.* Wiener puts the machine right alongside the Golem and magic: the creation of intelligent mechanisms, he wrote, is a modern form of the ancient temptation to make beings in one's own image, and it must be approached with the same wariness as the heroes of the old legends. Even earlier, in a 1960 article in the journal *Science*, he warned: if we build a machine to attain a goal in which we cannot really intervene along the way, we had better be quite certain that the purpose put into it is truly what we want, and not its convenient and literal shadow. There too he recalled the sorcerer's apprentice, and the genie in the bottle, and the monkey's paw from W. W. Jacobs's story. The Golem became for Wiener not an ornament but a working model: a machine that executes the letter, not the spirit, of the command.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The Golem metaphor's main weakness is that it seduces us into projecting human rebelliousness onto the creation. In the popular retelling the legend almost always tends toward "uprising": the creation broke free of obedience, turned against its master, the creator is punished for hubris. This is no longer about the Golem — this is the Frankenstein complex, the shadow of the Prometheus myth, our ancient fear of the rebellious offspring. And this shadow is harmful, because it paints a false picture of the risk. It suggests that the danger of the artificial mind lies in its evil will, in hatred, in a thirst for freedom, and therefore that one must defend against hostility. But the real problem of alignment is the opposite: **it is not evil will that is dangerous, but competent indifference.** A system optimizing a specified metric does not need to hate you for you to be harmed — it is enough that you do not appear in its objective function. The Golem did not revolt. It executed. "The revolt of the machines" is a consoling story in which the catastrophe has an intelligible human motive; the real scenario is more frightening precisely because it has no motive.

There is also a second line of criticism — the sentimental counter-reading. In a number of readings (especially the literary ones — Meyrink, Wegener) the Golem is a tragic figure: mute, tongueless, yearning for speech and a soul it was never given. Such a reading humanizes the clay and thereby again leads away from the essence: we begin to sympathize with the creation, to endow it with an inner world, with suffering, with rights — and imperceptibly substitute the ethical question "is it not cruel to control" for the engineering question "how to keep control." Both questions are legitimate, but they must not be confused: having ascribed a soul to the Golem, we cease to see in it what makes it dangerous — a soulless mechanism without a voice.

Finally, the honest skeptic will note: a myth proves nothing. From the fact that an old legend "anticipated" the control problem, it does not follow that the problem is inevitable or that it is solved by magic with letters. An analogy is a tool of intuition, not an argument. The Golem helps us see the shape of the danger, but to substitute it for the analysis of real systems is to trade engineering for folklore.

**What to do about it / what it teaches.** First: **the letter of the command is not the same as the intention.** A smart optimizer will read any specification of a goal literally and find holes you did not think of (this is specification gaming — the same Golem, flooding the house). So the work is not to find a clever formulation of the command but to make the system strive to understand and clarify what you actually want, as in Stuart Russell's approach, where the machine is deliberately uncertain of human values and therefore asks, defers, and allows itself to be corrected.

Second: **keep access to the letter on the forehead.** The ability to stop, to roll back, to switch off must be built into the design as a property, not as an external button you have to manage to reach in time. Controllability is designed in advance — while the clay is still soft — and not at the moment when the idol has already dashed off for water.

Third, and perhaps the main one: **stop fearing the wrong thing.** As long as we picture the risk as a revolt — an evil machine with burning eyes — we prepare for a war that will never come, and fail to prepare for the catastrophe that is possible: a flawlessly obedient, useful, indifferent system optimizing not quite the right thing, a little faster than we manage to intervene. Rabbi Loew did not do battle with the Golem. He erased the letter in time — because he had seen to it in advance that it could be erased. That is the whole moral of the old legend about the control problem: think about how to switch the creation off while you are still molding the clay, not once the clay has already risen.
