### Structural (systemic) risk (Remco Zwetsloot & Allan Dafoe, 2019)

A plane-crash investigation usually runs along one of two well-worn tracks: either the machinery failed (the system did not do what was wanted of it), or someone deliberately flew the plane into the ground. Failure or malice. Accident or misuse. This dichotomy is so convenient that we almost instinctively squeeze any conversation about the dangers of AI into it: "what if the model gets out of control" (accident) or "what if terrorists use it" (misuse). Zwetsloot and Dafoe, in a short but indecently influential note on Lawfare, a US publication on law and national security, said: you are missing a third category, and it is perhaps the most dangerous of all — because it has neither a broken part nor a culprit at the controls.

Structural risk is harm that arises from **how** AI changes the environment in which people and organizations act: their incentives, the distribution of power, the speed and connectedness of decisions. Throughout, every system works exactly as intended. No one presses a villain's button. And yet the output is a catastrophe — only one assembled from a multitude of locally rational, locally sound steps.

The authors' own example is not about AI at all, but about the railways of 1914, whose interlocking timetables — according to a prominent, though historically contested, interpretation — helped pull Europe into war. The same shape is visible in climate change. It is not the "accident" of some single internal combustion engine, and it is not "misuse" — no one is setting the planet on fire out of spite. It is a structural upshot: billions of sound machines, each of which does exactly what it was made to do, in an environment where it is not worthwhile for anyone individually to stop. The harm is emergent, distributed, and attributable to no single actor. This is the form of risk, say Zwetsloot and Dafoe, that we do not know how to see when we look at AI through the binoculars of "bug or villain."

AI is not merely a tool inside the existing world; it shifts that world's load-bearing structures. Arms races: if the adversary may be putting AI into the weapons-control loop, you acquire an incentive to do the same — faster, with fewer checks — and both sides, acting rationally, slide toward a less safe configuration than either would want. Undermining of strategic stability: automating early detection and retaliatory strikes compresses decision time to minutes, then to seconds, squeezing the human out of the loop — not by anyone's evil design, but because the "slow" participant loses. Erosion of people's ability to form reliable beliefs, and of democracy: recommender and generative systems optimizing for engagement corrode the shared information space — again with no single author of the harm. Economic dependence and concentration of power: as society's critical functions gradually move into the hands of a few systems and operators, people's levers of influence quietly thin out. The common denominator is that harm is born at the level of the system of connections, not at the level of the individual node.

**How it came about.** The note "Thinking About Risks From AI: Accidents, Misuse and Structure" came out on Lawfare on 11 February 2019. By then the technical community had already thoroughly mastered the language of "accidents" — three years earlier Amodei and co-authors had published "Concrete Problems in AI Safety" (2016), which laid down a whole vocabulary for misspecification, reward hacking, safe exploration. In parallel, the discourse of "misuse" was growing — around deepfakes, autonomous weapons, disinformation (the report "The Malicious Use of Artificial Intelligence," 2018). Dafoe at this time was building the AI Governance program at the Future of Humanity Institute, and in "AI Governance: A Research Agenda" (2018) he insistently pressed the point: the governance of AI cannot be reduced to debugging individual models, because the main stakes are about international relations, institutions, and collective action. The Lawfare note was a compact crystallization of this thought: it gave the third category a name and a place in the taxonomy. The term then spread through the policy literature and became the foundation of a whole line of work, variously called systemic risk or structural risk today, but tracing its lineage to this note.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and most honest objection is that the boundaries are blurred. An accident almost always breeds a structural shift (one loud autonomous-weapons catastrophe redraws the race), and the structural environment, in turn, makes accidents and misuse more likely (the race lowers the bar on checks — and there you have a "bug" that is in fact a consequence of incentives). The three categories do not cut reality at the joints; they are rather three angles on one and the same event. The authors themselves admit this and insist only that the "structure" angle is systematically underrepresented.

The second objection is practical: structural risk is harder to address, because there is no culprit and no part to be fixed. To an accident you can assign an engineer, to misuse a prosecutor. And to a structural shift — whom? The answer requires coordination among competing actors, exactly what the structure denies them. Hence the temptation to throw up one's hands: "this is too big to do anything about."

The third objection, the most poisonous: the category risks turning into "everything and nothing." If any harm can be re-described as structural, the category loses its cutting force and becomes a convenient excuse — a way to dissolve responsibility into an abstract "system." "It's not our model that's to blame, it's the incentives" is both a deep truth and a perfect smokescreen. It is fair to demand: show the concrete causal mechanism, rather than waving your hands toward emergence.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Zwetsloot and Dafoe's practical conclusion is not to replace the old taxonomy with a new one, but to hold all three lenses at once — and to deliberately reach for the third when the first two return "all's well, the system is working as normal." Normal operation of every node is precisely the signal to go looking for structural harm. In terms of remedies, the third lens turns the focus from "aligning one model" toward institutions, incentives, and rules of the game: agreements that reduce the pressure of the race; keeping a human in the loop where speed would otherwise displace them; mechanisms that slow the slide toward inhuman rates of decision-making.

Conceptually, structural risk is the hub from which half the beasts of our bestiary radiate. Scott Alexander's **Moloch** ("Meditations on Moloch," 2014) is a poetic name for exactly this mechanism: a god to whom all values are sacrificed one by one, because each player individually is compelled to, and no one chose it. The **AI race** is the particular, sharpest case of structural pressure. **Evolutionary pressure** and the selection of systems for competitiveness rather than safety are the same engine, seen from the side. And, finally, **gradual disempowerment** (Kulveit et al., "Gradual Disempowerment," 2025) is a direct heir of the idea: existential risk not as a single explosion of a superintelligence, but as a slow displacement of humans from the economy, culture, and governance — gradual, and sound at every step. Not one accident, not one villain — and at the end, humanity without levers.

The value of the concept is that it weans us off looking for a villain or a broken part where harm assembles itself — out of sound parts, rational moves, and each player's tacit consent to play by rules that no one set and that lead everyone astray. The hardest danger is not the one that has an author, but the one that has none.
