### The Value Alignment Problem: getting AI to want the same things we do

If one had to formulate the central challenge of safe AI in a single phrase, no words serve better than Stuart Russell's framing: how to create autonomous systems with values that are aligned with those of the human race? This is the value alignment problem. Put more simply — to make a powerful AI "want" the same thing we want, so that its actions bring benefit rather than harm, within our conceptions of good. If it is not solved, warns Russell (and many others), the creation of a truly advanced AI may turn out to be our last invention: an uncontrolled intelligence with alien goals will, by accident or by design, sweep away everything we hold dear.

Back in the mid-twentieth century Isaac Asimov formulated his Three Laws of Robotics — a fictional attempt to "hardwire" protection into a machine: do not harm a human, obey a human, protect yourself, but only insofar as this does not conflict with the first two. This is precisely a reframing of the alignment problem: how to set simple yet all-encompassing rules that guarantee loyalty. Except that in Asimov's own stories these laws regularly led to paradoxes — a hint that the task is no easy one. Philosophically it all comes down to the question: what are "the values of humanity"? The notion is an abstract one. In practical terms, it can be put like this: we want the AI to respect human life and safety, to strive to fulfill our intentions rather than to interpret them malignantly. Ask it to "improve life on Earth" — and it must not decide that the best way is to radically reduce the population (after all, those remaining will get more resources). This is a typical example of misalignment: formally, the machine may understand the goal "improve life" literally, but literalness does not reflect the fullness of our values — we did not, after all, mean genocide as improvement.

Russell calls for making safe alignment the goal of the AI field itself and proposes to slightly reframe the task itself: not to create an autonomous rational agent with a fixed goal, but to build an agent that strives to realize the goals of the human. In his book *Human Compatible* (2019) he derives three principles: (1) the AI has no fixed goals of its own — its sole goal is to fulfill our desires; (2) initially it does not know what we want and must learn it; (3) the ultimate source of information about our values is human behavior, and it is by observing our choices that the AI learns. Hence corrigibility: not being certain it has understood our values correctly, the robot itself is motivated to let us switch it off — "stop, not like that" is valuable information to it. Such an approach is meant to prevent the "paperclip effect": the machine will always be not entirely certain whether it has understood our values correctly, and therefore will not stubbornly enact them against our protests. Russell and colleagues also develop the mathematics of learning values — for instance, cooperative inverse reinforcement learning: the AI forms a value function together with the human, observing their choices, asking questions, and receiving feedback.

**History.** Although the term "value alignment" came into use around 2014 thanks to Russell, the substance was discussed from the very birth of cybernetics: as early as the 1960s Norbert Wiener wrote that, in giving a machine a goal, we must be sure it takes into account everything that matters to us, or there will be trouble. As early as 1965 I. J. Good wrote about the danger of an uncontrolled superintelligence, and in the 1990s so did George Dyson; the kindred term "control problem" is about the same thing: how to control an intelligence smarter than yourself. An important milestone was the 2015 open letter from the Future of Life Institute, signed by many scientists (including Stephen Hawking, Elon Musk, and Russell himself), calling for research on safe and beneficial AI to be made a priority. Academics broadened the concept, distinguishing "fundamental goal misalignment" (the AI's main goal diverges from the human one) from "operational" misalignment (a specific task ends up optimizing the wrong thing — say, unintended discrimination in a hiring algorithm). This is how the idea of a single challenge took shape — from the fairness of today's models to the control of a future superintelligence (alignment from near to long term).

The philosophical side is closely tied to ethics: how to instill morality in a machine, and whose values should it reflect — all of humanity's, or those of specific people? Suppose an AI strictly follows utilitarianism (maximum happiness for the maximum number of people) — but at the expense of justice for minorities; do we want that? And should an AI preserve current human values or lead us toward better ones? (A dangerous path: what if it decides to impose the "correct" values dictatorially?) On the practical side, the task of learning values arises. One path is observation: to watch how we act and what we want — but people are irrational and inconsistent. Another is a survey with feedback: RLHF already tunes large language models closer to what we want. For a fundamentally smarter system, however, these methods may not suffice. Yet another option is artificial constraints (the AI as advisor, not executor): it lowers the risks, but it also narrows the benefit.

Some researchers (especially from deep learning) considered the theme of value alignment excessively dramatized: "we simply train models on data, they take their cue from humans — what other values? A model is a function, it has no desires." Partly true for today's narrow AIs. But safety proponents look ahead: as soon as a model begins to plan and act autonomously, the question of goals will loom large — which is why alignment must be worked out before the creation of AGI.

Curiously, the alignment problem unites optimists and pessimists: the former say that if we solve alignment we will get a wondrous beneficial AI and flourishing; the latter, that this is extremely difficult, and failure will end in disaster. But both sides acknowledge the importance of the task. There are also skeptics, convinced that superintelligence will not appear in the foreseeable future, and hence that it is too early to worry (a nod to the unfinished saga of the sparrows raising an owlet). The pace of development, however — especially with the arrival of powerful language models — inclines ever more experts to the thought that alignment must be addressed in advance, long before genuinely superintelligent systems. The alignment problem remains open — the formulations are clear, but there are no guaranteed solutions. But the very fact that the finest minds have focused on it inspires hope: the first step — recognition — has been taken. We are no longer naive in assuming "smart means good"; an AI must be deliberately made kind, taking into account in advance all the cunning that a goal-directed intelligence is capable of.
