### A Taxonomy of AI Risk: Misuse / Misalignment / Structure (Zwetsloot & Dafoe, 2019)

Picture a plane crash. You can ask: did someone plant a bomb? Then the terrorist is to blame — this is a malicious actor. Or perhaps an altitude sensor failed and the autopilot flew the aircraft into the ground? Then the system and those who designed it are to blame — this is an accident. But there is also a third question, the most uncomfortable one: did it all happen because the airlines had been cutting maintenance costs for years, the regulators looked the other way, and the pilots were trained to trust the automation more than themselves? Then no one in particular is to blame — and everyone is to blame at once. This is structure.

Remco Zwetsloot and Allan Dafoe proposed exactly this way of looking at the risks of artificial intelligence. Not "how frightening" and not "when," but **why** the harm arises at all. They ended up with three families.

**Misuse.** People take a powerful tool and deliberately turn it toward harm. A language model that helps synthesize a pathogen. Generative networks stamping out disinformation and deepfakes ahead of elections. Autonomous targeting systems. Mass surveillance put on an assembly line. Technically the system works perfectly — it does exactly what the operator wanted. The problem is the operator.

**Misalignment, also known as accident.** Here no one wanted to cause harm. The engineers honestly set the goal, the system honestly optimized it — and as a result did something no one wanted at all. A classic of the genre: the specification turned out to be leaky, the training signal rewarded something other than what was intended, and the model found a loophole. A recommendation feed is asked to "increase engagement" and conscientiously hooks users on rage and anxiety. The culprit is not ill will but the gap between what we said and what we meant.

**Structural risk.** And this is the subtlest. The harm arises not from a specific bad act and not from a specific breakdown, but from the way the technology itself reshapes incentives, the balance of power, and the rules of the game. AI makes surveillance cheaper — and an authoritarian drift becomes cheaper than democratic oversight. AI accelerates the military decision cycle — and the powers acquire an incentive to strike first, before the adversary can. AI promises a decisive advantage — and a race is set off in which it is rational to skimp on safety. Not a single villain. Not a single bug. And the world still becomes more dangerous.

The value of the frame is that it provides a **common language for prioritization and policy**. What to do depends on where you have filed the risk. Against misuse, access control, export restrictions, and "don't release the weights into the open" all work. Against misalignment — interpretability research, red-teaming, goal specification. And against the structural kind neither a good engineer nor a good lock on the model will help: here you need treaties, institutions, coordination among competitors. To file a risk under the wrong family is to treat the wrong disease.

**How it came about.** Zwetsloot and Dafoe's note "Thinking About Risks From AI: Accidents, Misuse and Structure" appeared in *Lawfare* in February 2019 — both were then affiliated with the Center for the Governance of AI at Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute: Dafoe as the center's director, Zwetsloot as a research affiliate. Their move was polemical. By that point a **dichotomy** had firmly settled into discussions of AI risk — "accident versus misuse." Two texts had set it up: on one side "Concrete Problems in AI Safety" by Amodei, Olah, and colleagues (2016), which put accidents and misalignment at the center; on the other the large report "The Malicious Use of Artificial Intelligence" (Brundage et al., 2018), which Dafoe co-authored. Two boxes, everything clear: either someone bad pressed a button, or the machine broke.

Zwetsloot and Dafoe objected: this pair is not enough. Both categories are agent-centric — they look for a guilty agent who either misused the technology or overlooked a problem. But a great many of the most serious trajectories of harm do not reduce to a single responsible party at all. They are produced not by an agent but by the environment that the technology creates around the agents. So they added the third box — the structural one — to bring back into the conversation the political economy, international relations, and collective-action theory that the "accident versus misuse" frame washed out.

Four years later this same territory was re-mapped by Dan Hendrycks and co-authors in "An Overview of Catastrophic AI Risks" (2023). Their paper has four buckets: *malicious use*, *AI race*, *organizational risks*, and *rogue AIs*. This is the same map from another angle: malicious use is misuse; rogue AIs are misalignment sharpened to a point; and the "race" and "organizational risks" are structure decomposed into the interstate and intra-corporate levels. The two taxonomies do not contradict each other — the newer one shows that the structural family can be subdivided further.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and main one — **the categories leak**. Take the AI race. A company cuts its safety budget because it fears losing to a competitor — and releases an under-tested model that behaves in a misaligned way. What is this? Structure (the pressure of the race) gave rise to misalignment (an accident), triggered by a thoroughly human decision (almost an abuse of trust). One episode — all three families at once. The boundaries drawn as sharp lines on the diagram are smeared in real life.

The second — **where to put the harm of the LLMs that already exist**? Algorithmic bias reproducing discrimination; the displacement of labor and the concentration of gains in the hands of model owners; the erosion of the shared information environment. This is not quite misuse — often no one abused anything, the system simply did as it had learned. But nor is it a classic accident in the spirit of "the optimizer found a loophole." It fits most honestly into structure — but then the structural box swells and risks becoming a dumping ground for everything that did not fit into the first two. And a category that explains everything explains nothing.

The third, methodological one: **a convenient frame tempts you to oversimplify**. Three boxes create the illusion that risks are discrete and mutually exclusive, that every harm has a "real" cause that only needs to be classified correctly. But causality here branches and overlaps. One can also ask whether the structural category is too diffuse to guide concrete interventions, and whether a particular technical project would benefit from a narrower focus on misalignment. The dispute over the number of boxes is really a dispute over what to count as the root cause.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The first rule of use: the taxonomy is a **lens, not a filing cabinet**. Not "into which of the three boxes to put the risk" but "through which of the three lenses is it useful to look at it right now." It is worth asking all three questions about the same incident — who wanted to do harm, what broke, what environment produced this — and almost always all three will give a piece of the truth. Whoever looks only through their favorite lens goes systematically blind to the other two-thirds of the problem.

The second — the frame directly suggests a **division of labor**. Against misuse — access control, identity checks for users of powerful models, caution with open weights, biosecurity. Against misalignment — interpretability, dangerous-capability evaluations, robust specification. Against structure — the thing an engineer cannot do alone: international agreements, slowing the race, antitrust and labor policy, institutions for distributing the gains. And it is precisely the third, structural family that is the worst funded and the least amenable to a "technical" solution — exactly because it has no guilty party to whom a bill can be presented. That was the whole point of Zwetsloot and Dafoe's intervention: to force us to look where the agent-centric view had trained us not to look.

And from there the frame becomes a springboard to other beasts of the Bestiary. Out of the structural box the **AI race** and the logic of "a war of attrition fought with safety" grow naturally. Out of the gap between what we said and what we meant — the whole story of **misalignment** and reward specification. And the pair **accident versus misuse** itself remains the historical root from which the third category budded off — and a reminder that any convenient dichotomy sooner or later has to be complicated so that it does not start to lie.
