### Parfit's Hitchhiker (Derek Parfit, 1984)

Picture this: you are dying in the desert. The second day without water, your lips cracked, the sun hammering into the back of your skull. And suddenly — a plume of dust on the horizon, a car. The driver brakes. He is willing to take you to town and save you — but he is no philanthropist, he is a businessman, and there is one condition: on arrival you pay him 1,000 dollars. You have no cash on you, but there is an ATM in town. The deal seems obvious: a life for a thousand, who would refuse?

But there is a detail that turns everything upside down. The driver is an infallible lie detector. He does not believe promises, he reads *intentions*. He looks you in the eye and sees not what you say but how you will actually act once you are alive and safe at the ATM. And this is where, if you are an honest and clear-headed person, you fall into a trap — one set not by the driver but by your own mind.

Because imagine that you are already in town. Alive. Saved. The threat has passed — the desert is behind you, your thirst is slaked, you stand at the ATM holding your card. What reason is there now to pay? A thousand will no longer buy you your life — your life is already yours, for free. To hand the money over is simply to part with it and get nothing in return. The purely causal calculation is inexorable: there is *nothing* to pay for. The past cannot be replayed, the rescue has happened, and paying that thousand only makes the future poorer. A reasonable egoist will not hand over the money.

And this is exactly what the driver sees in advance — in the desert, when he looks you in the eye. He reads in you a person who will shrug at the ATM and walk away. And he leaves you to die in the sand. Not out of vengeance — out of arithmetic.

**The mechanism: rationality that kills its bearer.** To *survive*, you need to be an agent who will pay — sincerely, with every fiber of your being — even when it is "already too late and pointless" to pay. Not to pretend to be willing — the detector cannot be fooled — but actually to be that way. Yet this very willingness, from the standpoint of narrowly causal rationality, looks like stupidity: you are handing over a thousand for nothing. The paradox is that the "fool" who pays drives out of the desert alive, while the "clever one" who changes their mind at the last moment is left lying among the dunes, certain to the end that they were right.

This is the problem of dynamic inconsistency and credible commitment in all its starkness. Causal decision theory (CDT) evaluates every action by its future consequences *from the point where you stand now*. But at the ATM the future consequences of payment are entirely negative — minus a thousand dollars and nothing gained. A CDT agent is by construction incapable of keeping the promise made in the desert, because at the moment of fulfillment the promise no longer serves any of their goals. Their word carries no weight — and everyone who can read the agent knows this. They are trapped: the rationality of the moment strips them, again and again, of exactly what rationality as a whole would desperately want — the trust that saves a life.

The Hitchhiker is a close relative of **Newcomb's problem** and **counterfactual mugging**. In all three, an agent reads you through and through, and the naively causal choice "here and now it is better not to pay / to take both boxes" leads to systematic defeat. The difference is one of emphasis: Newcomb asks what you will choose *now*, knowing about the prediction; the Hitchhiker asks whether you can *hold to* a decision once its original reason has fallen away.

**How it came about.** The example was formulated by the British philosopher Derek Parfit in the book *Reasons and Persons* (1984) — one of the most influential works of twentieth-century moral philosophy. Parfit's own staging is more modest: a midnight car breakdown in the desert, a promised reward — and a protagonist who is "transparent," unable to lie convincingly and knowing that they never act against their own interest; the driver does not believe the promise and drives away. The traveler dying of thirst, the thousand dollars, and the ATM are a later canonical retelling from the decision-theory community; in this form the problem also entered Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares's paper on functional decision theory (FDT). In the section on rationality and the "self-interest theory," Parfit examined cases where *being* disposed a certain way is more advantageous than acting "optimally" at each separate moment. His Hitchhiker illustrates the thought that sometimes it is rational to want to become a person who will act *ir*rationally in a future situation, because that very disposition bears fruit in advance. Parfit stood on the shoulders of Thomas Schelling, who back in *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960) had shown that the ability to credibly bind one's own hands is not a weakness but a source of strength (the classic example — burning the bridges behind you). And two years after Parfit, David Gauthier worked the same vein: his *Morals by Agreement* (1986) built all of morality on the figure of the "constrained maximizer" — an agent who keeps their word even when breaking it is advantageous, and precisely for that reason is taken on as a partner. From the philosophy journals the problem migrated into the AI alignment community, where it became one of the canonical tests for decision theories — on a par with Newcomb.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first objection comes from CDT's defenders: the example is *rigged*. The world is deliberately arranged to punish a causal agent, and on a rigged proving ground you can "prove" anything at all. Supporters of newer decision theories such as FDT reply that the situation is not made up: it is a pure model of any cooperation where a partner assesses your reliability before the deal. What is punished is not an arbitrary type of reasoning but a concrete defect: the inability to keep one's word.

The second objection is practical: *an infallible detector is unreal*. But an infallible detector is not needed. It is enough that people read sincerity even a little from a face, a reputation, a track record — and the Hitchhiker's logic begins to work in ordinary life: loans, marriages, business ties rest on the fact that "unreliability" shows. The third, deeper: is it not simpler to do without exotic theories and just *value honesty for its own sake* — then you will pay not out of calculation but because you are a decent person? This is true — but it only shifts the question: why did evolution and culture build into us such "uncalculating" honesty? The answer coincides suspiciously with the Hitchhiker's conclusion: because those who could credibly commit survived more often.

Finally, critics point out that the disposition "always pay" is vulnerable to exploitation — you can be milked with empty threats. This is where the subtle boundary lies — between *the commitment to pay for a real rescue* and *capitulation to blackmail* — and the newer decision theories must draw it carefully.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Out of dissatisfaction with the fact that "rationality" ruins the rational agent grew the **functional decision theory** (FDT) of Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares. Its move is simple and radical: the agent chooses not an action but the *logical output of their decision function* — once and for all, for every situation where this function is "read," including the moment in the desert when the driver scans it. An FDT agent at the ATM pays — not because they are a fool but because "being an agent who pays" is precisely the policy that maximizes the outcome across the whole picture: it hauls them out of the sand. They do not replay the past and do not lapse into magical thinking — they simply understand that their decision at the ATM and the driver's prediction are *one and the same computation*, and by choosing to pay they retroactively make true what the driver saw in the desert. The Hitchhiker is the benchmark test that FDT passes and CDT fails, leaving its agent among the dunes.

An advanced agent is readable code that can be simulated, copied, "X-rayed." So the question "can it keep its word before someone who sees right through it" stops being parlor philosophy and becomes a question of design. The capacity for credible **precommitment** is the foundation of cooperation, deterrence, reputation, honest trade between agents smarter than us. But this same capacity has a dark side: **commitment races**, where agents race to bind their own hands in order to force others to yield, and vulnerability to blackmail — up to the grotesque **Roko's basilisk**. Sometimes the best way to win is to deprive yourself of the ability to change your mind. But the art is to bind yourself with a commitment *to pay the rescuer* — and to remain free *not to pay the extortioner*. Drawing that line is one of the unsolved problems of agent theory, and whether we draw it determines whether the machines of the future will be partners you can trust, or hostages to their own flawless logic.
