### Technique / autonomous technology — La Technique (Jacques Ellul, 1954)

Picture a committee convened to slow down a dangerous technology. Clever people, real authority, real will. And so they sit — and right from the first step they ask: how do we make the regulation effective? How do we measure the risk, optimize the procedure, speed up approval, automate the review? Jacques Ellul would say: that's it, you have already lost before you began. Because you are already thinking like Technique — you are looking for a better method. And Technique does not fear the one who looks for a better method. Technique *is* the search for a better method.

This is the central and most uncomfortable idea of the book *La Technique ou l'enjeu du siècle* (1954, in English *The Technological Society*, 1964). Ellul's "Technique" is capitalized, and means not machines, not gadgets, not industry. His definition is almost mathematical: `the totality of methods rationally arrived at and having absolute efficiency (for a given stage of development) in every field of human activity` — as Ellul himself put it in his "Note to the Reader" for the American edition. The steam engine is only one of the fruits of Technique, but so are the assembly-line organization of labor, and a pedagogical method, and the technique of interrogation, and the technique of propaganda, and the technique of managing attention, and the technique of prayer on a schedule — all of it is Technique. Where once a person acted out of habit, intuition, custom, faith, spontaneity — now a question arises: how might it be done more efficiently? And the moment this question is asked in earnest and everywhere, a process is set in motion that Ellul describes through a whole set of characteristics; here are the four main ones.

Autonomy: Technique develops according to its own inner logic, not according to our goals. We think we choose the tools to fit the tasks — but in fact the tools redefine the tasks. Self-augmentation: every technical solution breeds new technical problems, which demand new technical solutions, and so on without end — progress becomes almost impersonal, proceeding "by itself," with no need for a genius inventor. Monism: Technique is a single block: we cannot pry it apart into "good" and "bad" pieces and take the benefit while discarding the harm; it comes whole, with all its consequences interlocked. And universality: it spreads geographically and, more importantly, penetrates into domains that were previously not technical at all — into politics, art, intimacy, the very way we think about human beings.

The key move is about control. Usually we console ourselves: technology is neutral, what matters is whose hands it is in, we'll set limits on it, attach ethics and oversight. Ellul answers: the limits, the ethics, the oversight — themselves become techniques. Applying that logic to contemporary examples: a bioethics commission is a technique of legitimation. Risk assessment is a technique. "Responsible AI" with its metrics and dashboards is a technique. Every time you try to rein in Technique from the outside, you are forced to apply an efficient method — and by that act you draw your instrument of control inside the very system you meant to constrain. The system has no "outside." It is not a conspiracy and not ill will but a structural property, which is exactly why Ellul called his description not pessimism but realism: he was not predicting catastrophe, he was describing a logic.

**How it came about.** Ellul (1912–1994) was a French jurist, sociologist, and Protestant theologian from Bordeaux, a member of the Resistance, and later a Christian anarchist. He was a friend of the thinker and writer Bernard Charbonneau, with whom he had already been formulating a critique of "technical civilization" back in the 1930s. *La Technique* came out in 1954 in France and passed almost unnoticed — the French intellectual scene was busy with Marxism and existentialism, and the book flattered neither the left (progress does not liberate) nor the right. Its second birth happened across the ocean: Aldous Huxley, having read the book, recommended it, and in 1964 John Wilkinson's English translation appeared under the title *The Technological Society*. It was this translation that made Ellul a figure of 1960s counterculture and his work required reading for the later philosophy of technology. Later Ellul refined and developed the thesis in *Le Système technicien* (1977) and *Le Bluff technologique* (1988), where he spoke increasingly of information and of the "technical system" as a habitat.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and chief charge is technological determinism and monolithism. Ellul writes of Technique as a single all-consuming subject with a will of its own, and leaves almost no room for human choice, conflict of interests, the politics of particular actors. The school of the social construction of technology (SCOT) — Wiebe Bijker and Trevor Pinch — and the adjacent sociology of the social shaping of technology (Donald MacKenzie, Judy Wajcman) object: technologies do not fall from the sky by their own inner logic; their form is determined by the struggle of groups, by economics, by cultural agreements; the bicycle, the light bulb, the atomic bomb became what they are because of people, not because of the self-disclosure of "Technique." Second: unfalsifiability. If any attempt at resistance is declared "also a technique," then the thesis cannot be refuted — it explains everything and therefore explains little.

Third: a practical dead end and quietism. If the system has no "outside," why act at all? (Ellul himself, by the way, was a man of action, and in his theological works insisted on freedom and hope — but this does not follow from the sociological books, and the tension remains.) Fourth, more subtle: Ellul underestimates how many definitions efficiency has, and how incompatible they are — the "best means" for whom and by what criterion? Where he sees a single imperative, there is often a dispute over values disguised as a dispute over methods.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For the bestiary, Ellul is the root ancestor of a whole brood of beasts. His "autonomy of Technique" is a direct relative of Heidegger's *Gestell* from "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954, the same year!): in Heidegger, technology is the way in which everything that exists is disclosed as "standing-reserve"; in Ellul, the way in which everything is subordinated to the imperative of efficiency. Langdon Winner directly inherits from Ellul in *Autonomous Technology* (1977) and in the famous essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980): artifacts embody a political order, and so the "neutrality of the tool" is an illusion. Neil Postman in *Technopoly* (1992) translates the thesis into cultural studies: a society that has capitulated to technology. And Evgeny Morozov in *To Save Everything, Click Here* (2013) gives it digital flesh under the name "technological solutionism" — the reflex to declare every human problem a bug awaiting an app.

Moloch from Scott Alexander's famous essay "Meditations on Moloch" is the micro-mechanics of what Ellul described as a macro-process: a coordination trap where everyone sacrifices values for competitive efficiency, because otherwise they lose to whoever does the sacrificing. Ellul's Technique is Moloch grown into a civilization. And the problem of autonomous optimization without a human goal — the central nightmare of AI safety — is literally Ellulian autonomy realized in hardware: a system maximizing an efficient method, which can no longer be approached "from the outside" with human goals, because it will draw even our alignment tools into its own loop. When we build "model evaluations," "red-teaming as a process," "a constitution for the AI" — it is worth keeping in mind Ellul's uncomfortable question: are we not turning control itself into yet another efficient method, neatly built into the optimizing system?

The practical conclusion is not to give up, but to stop seeking salvation solely in improving the method. If Ellul is even half right, then real resistance begins where we deliberately leave zones of inefficiency — decisions made not because they are optimal, but because they reflect the value we have chosen. Not "how do we optimize more safely," but "what do we refuse to optimize at all." For a safety engineer this sounds heretical.
