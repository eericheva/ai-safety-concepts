### The Switchman (Nick Bostrom, 2002/2014)

Picture a junction station where trains come in from every side. You cannot halt the traffic entirely — the trains are already under way, others press close behind each of them, and no one will obey a "stop the whole network" signal. But you hold the switch levers in your hands. You do not decide whether the trains go or not — you decide who passes the junction first. And if you let through the train with brakes and communications and hold the string of explosive-laden tank cars on a siding, a wreck can be prevented without stopping anything. And this is the whole idea of differential technological development — the policy of the switchman, not of the engine-driver, and not of the dispatcher declaring a general strike.

Nick Bostrom formulates the thesis as a refusal of two seductive extremes at once. The first is naive techno-optimism: "progress will sort itself out, any knowledge is a good in the end." The second is relinquishment, the dream of braking science altogether, of declaring a moratorium on dangerous technologies. Bostrom says: a full stop is unattainable and, most likely, harmful — the knowledge we avoid will come to those who do not think about caution at all, and in the bargain we would deprive ourselves of the protective technologies that also grow only out of progress. So the only truly available variable is not "faster or slower in general" but **ordering**. Slow the development of dangerous and harmful technologies and speed the development of beneficial ones, especially those that reduce the risk from the dangerous. Not the absolute speed, but the order of arrival.

**The mechanism.** The value of the approach is that it turns the hopeless question "how to stop progress?" into a solvable engineering-strategic question "in what order?" Take three pairs. In biology: defensive biosecurity — pathogen diagnostics, vaccine platforms, sanitary surveillance — versus the offensive ability to synthesize new viruses; one wants the shield to outpace the sword. In the military sphere: the means of verification and attribution — who struck, who broke a treaty — must arrive before cheap autonomous weapons, otherwise there will be nothing to negotiate about and nothing to negotiate with. And the pair that matters most for our Bestiary: **alignment before capabilities**. If the science of how to make a powerful AI understand our intentions and be controllable lags behind the science of how to make it merely powerful, we get a system we do not know how to steer — and conversely, if alignment and interpretability run ahead, each new leap in capability meets an already-prepared safety circuit. The switchman does not forbid powerful AI. It demands that the brakes be invented before the train is brought up to speed.

The **safety tax** makes sense precisely as the price of keeping the right ordering against the pressure of the race. **Compute governance** is literally the switches: slow the most dangerous stretch (the training of ultra-large models) without touching all the rest, because compute is a scarce, physically trackable, and therefore regulable resource. And in 2023 Vitalik Buterin gave the idea a new name and new energy: **d/acc**, where his "d" unfolds into several words at once — defense, decentralization, democracy, differential. A direct descendant of Bostrom: do not brake technologies and do not speed everything at once (as e/acc does), but selectively accelerate precisely the defensive layer — defense, resilience, privacy, verification — so that a world where technologies are strong remains a world where it is cheaper to defend than to attack.

**How it came about.** The term first appears in 2002 in the paper "Existential Risks: Analyzing Human Extinction Scenarios and Related Hazards," published in the Journal of Evolution and Technology. This is the same text that put the very concept of existential risk into circulation — and it is there that Bostrom proposes differential technological development as the only practical strategy left to humanity if total control over technology is impossible. He gives a short, almost aphoristic formula: slow dangerous technologies relative to beneficial ones. Twelve years later, in *Superintelligence* (2014), the approach becomes a load-bearing structure: there it is built into the analysis of the race for primacy, of what caution costs in that race, and of the question of how one can influence the outcome at all if research cannot be stopped. The idea grows further — into Toby Ord's *The Precipice* (2020), into governance reports, and finally into Buterin's d/acc, where it turns from an academic maxim into the slogan of a whole technological movement.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first — **who, exactly, stands at the levers?** The metaphor presupposes a single switchman with a view of the whole station. In reality there are thousands of switches, in the hands of competing companies and warring states, and there is no common console. A participant in the race will not voluntarily hold back its own train — for it, "slow the dangerous" means "hand the lead to whoever will not slow." This is a classic **coordination failure**, that very Moloch: an ordering advantageous to the participants collectively is disadvantageous to each individually.

The second, more caustic still — **dual use blurs the very boundary**. The approach requires reliably sorting technologies into "defensive" and "dangerous," but most of them are both at once. Pathogen diagnostics and pathogen synthesis are neighboring pages of one textbook. Interpretability makes AI both safer and more capable. Arms verification and target guidance feed on the same sensors. If "shield" and "sword" are often different facets of the same object, then how do you tell accelerating the defensive from speeding up the dangerous? The switch turns out to be not between two tracks but in the middle of one.

The third — **the risk of misjudgment**. We may sincerely deem a technology risk-reducing and invest in it, only for it to turn out to be an accelerant of the threat. History knows such reversals: fertilizers feed billions and feed explosives; nuclear energy is both a power station and a bomb. The assessment of "what is defensive" is made today, and the reckoning comes tomorrow, when the decision can no longer be undone.

The fourth, and most insidious — **the slogan justifies anything**. "We are engaged in differential acceleration" is the perfect rhetorical wrapper for any selective support you already wanted to give anyway. Under the banner of "defensive acceleration" it is convenient to push through exactly what is profitable, declaring competitors "reckless accelerationists." A strategy meant to discipline progress easily turns into an indulgence for the one who hands out the money. And here too hides the **unilateralist's curse**: if even one strong player decides that a given dangerous technology is really "defensive," it will accelerate it single-handedly — and zero out everyone else's restraint.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** None of the objections cancels the approach — they clarify what it requires in order to work. The switchman needs more than the wish to throw the switch; it needs levers, a view, and comrades at the other posts.

- **Lean on manageable bottlenecks.** The idea is powerless where a technology can be neither tracked nor slowed, and powerful where it can. So the center of gravity shifts to compute: training the largest models requires a scarce, physically tangible resource — this is the very switch one can actually reach by hand.
- **Compress the gap, do not forbid.** The practical aim is not to stop the dangerous train but to speed the safe one enough that it is guaranteed to go first: invest more and earlier in alignment, interpretability, and dangerous-capability evaluations than in the buildup of raw power. The cheaper safety is, the smaller the temptation to cut it for speed.
- **Build a common console.** Against coordination failure it is not the heroism of a lone actor that works but verifiable rules of the game: agreements on ordering with a checking mechanism, licensing of training, mutual commitments subject to audit. A treaty with no way to catch a defector is exactly what Moloch devours overnight — so the key lies in verification, which the approach itself commands us to develop first of all.
- **Distinguish honestly.** Since the "defensive/dangerous" boundary is blurred, it cannot be drawn behind closed doors or out of self-interest. An external, transparent assessment is needed of what we are accelerating — otherwise d/acc degenerates into safety-washing, a showy ritual instead of substance, and the slogan becomes a screen once and for all.

Where does this lead? To the admission that humanity most likely has no brake for the whole network — only switches. Bostrom's **vulnerable world hypothesis** carries the thought to its limit: if a "black ball" lies somewhere in the urn of technologies — a discovery that makes universal destruction cheap — then the absolute speed of progress determines only how soon we draw it out, while the ordering determines whether we will manage by then to grow the means to handle it. The switchman does not promise that there will be no wreck. It says: as long as the train cannot be stopped, the only thing still in our power is to decide which train passes the junction first. And better that it be the one with brakes.
