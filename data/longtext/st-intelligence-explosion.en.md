### The Ladder-Eater (I. J. Good, 1965)

Suppose there is a machine that does exactly one thing slightly better than you: it designs machines. Not brewing coffee, not composing sonnets — only designing the next generations of itself. The first machine it designs will be smarter than its designer. That second device will design a third — smarter still and, crucially, faster. From there the human in the chain is already superfluous: they read a press release about a version that is long obsolete, while somewhere inside the data center version N+20 is discussing with itself a philosophy that would take us centuries to reach.

This is what an **intelligence explosion** looks like — the idea that intelligence, once it has learned to improve intelligence, stops needing us and climbs away up a ladder it builds as it goes, pulling the rungs out behind it.

**How it works and why it is about safety.** Ordinary technical progress is a cycle with a human at its center: people invent the transistor, people design the next chip, people write the next paper. The speed is limited by the speed of people. The intelligence explosion is the hypothesis that the loop can be closed without us. The key phrase is **recursive self-improvement**: if every improvement to the system raises its ability to improve itself, you get not linear growth, and not even an exponential with a fixed exponent, but a positive feedback loop in which the growth rate itself grows.

For alignment this matters for one brutal reason: **time disappears**. Our entire model of control over technology rests on the fact that between "something went wrong" and "it got really bad" there is a gap — weeks, months, lawsuits, product recalls. If the explosion is fast, there is no gap. The system you considered roughly human-level yesterday is today smarter than your whole safety team put together — and any errors in its goals, laid down while it was manageable, are now defended by an intelligence you can no longer outplay. This leads straight to the **control problem**: alignment must be done *before* the explosion, because *after* it there is no one to negotiate with as an equal.

And then — the most uncomfortable part. The first system to pass through the explosion gains what Nick Bostrom called a **decisive strategic advantage**: a lead so large that second place simply does not exist. A world where one agent has such an advantage and can use it collapses into a **singleton** — a single planet-wide center of decision-making. Whether that center is kind to us depends entirely on what we managed to put into it while it still fit inside a laboratory.

**How it came about.** The idea has an exact birthdate and a charmingly British author. In 1965 the mathematician Irving John Good — Turing's colleague from Bletchley Park, a man who broke German ciphers — published an essay whose title reads today like a trailer: "Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine." It contains a paragraph that is quoted to this day:

> "Let an ultraintelligent machine be defined as a machine that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man however clever. Since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far behind. Thus the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control."

Mind you, this was no scare story. Good wrote with the optimism of a Cold War engineer: he opened the paper with the line "the survival of man depends on the early construction of an ultraintelligent machine" and put the machine's value to the world at "a megakeynes" (a million "keynes", each representing the economist John Maynard Keynes's value to the world; an estimate Good cites put that value at no less than 100,000 million pounds sterling) — because it would give the human race a good chance of surviving indefinitely. The alarm came later.

The term **FOOM** itself belongs to our own time and has an altogether different intonation. It was put into circulation by Eliezer Yudkowsky: an onomatopoeia, a comic-book "whoosh," the moment when, within hours or days, a system shoots through the entire range from "smarter than a dog" to "smarter than humanity." In 2008 Yudkowsky and the economist Robin Hanson staged a prolonged public duel on the blog Overcoming Bias — it is still called the **AI-Foom Debate**. Yudkowsky insisted: the takeoff will be **fast and local**, one project will pull ahead and leave the rest behind. Hanson objected: progress will be smeared across the whole economy, there will be no lone champion, there will be a slow rise of a multitude of systems, as has already happened with industrial revolutions. The argument was never resolved — but it set up the system of coordinates we still live in.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first — **where is the bottleneck?** "Smarter" does not mean "omnipotent." To design the next chip it is not enough to be a genius — you need factories, lithography, physical experiments that run in real time and that cannot be "thought through faster." Intelligence may run up not against its own wits but against the speed at which the world answers its questions. Then there is no vertical takeoff — there is a ceiling set by physics and logistics.

The second — **diminishing returns**. Nothing guarantees that each successive improvement comes as easily as the last. It is quite possible that improving intelligence is a task where the higher you climb, the more each next step costs, and the feedback loop dies down rather than accelerates. Then recursive self-improvement exists, but the explosion does not.

The third, and the most influential objection — **Paul Christiano and "Takeoff speeds"** (2018). His argument is not "there will be no takeoff" but "there will be a takeoff, but a gradual one." If AI is genuinely useful, Christiano argues, then it will begin to move the world economy noticeably *long before* it becomes superintelligent. A world where a singularity appears in a year is a world where GDP is already, today, growing unusually fast off the back of the previous, weaker systems. We will see it coming, we will have years of warning and a heap of ever more powerful but still manageable systems to debug. This is a dispute not about whether it will happen but about *with what derivative* — and on the answer hangs everything in safety strategy: whether we get to learn as we go or must guess right the first time.

And the fourth, down-to-earth: the reality of large language models as we observe it so far looks more like Hanson's and Christiano's picture than Yudkowsky's. Progress is fast but gradual, requires enormous amounts of money and compute, is distributed among several labs, and no model has yet seriously taken up designing its successor with no human in the loop. So far.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is paradoxical: the less you believe in an instant FOOM, the more important it is to *measure* the derivative right now. Hence the interest in how much models already help with the AI research that produces their successors (automation of ML engineering is the first candidate for "closing the loop"), in "evals" for dangerous capabilities, in thresholds beyond which training or deploying a model requires special measures. If the takeoff turns out to be slow, these efforts are cheap insurance; if fast, they are the only thing we will manage to do in advance at all.

Good, incidentally, lived until 2009, and toward the end of his life he revised his optimism of 1965: in an unpublished autobiographical note of 1998 he wrote that in that opening line the word "survival" ought to be replaced by "extinction." The author of the idea lived to be frightened of his own beast. That is perhaps the most honest thing one can say about the Ladder-Eater: we still do not know how steeply its ladder rises — and the whole wager is to find out before we make the last rung.
