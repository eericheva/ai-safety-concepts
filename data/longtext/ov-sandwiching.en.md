### Sandwiching (Ajeya Cotra, 2021)

Picture a laboratory table, and on it — a sandwich. The bottom slice of bread: a person with only a weak grasp of the subject, a dilettante off the street. The top slice: an expert who knows the subject down to the bone. And between them, as the filling — a language model. And here is the riddle the whole thing was set up for: the model is already smarter than the bottom slice but dumber than the top one. The sandwiching question goes like this: can the dilettante, who does not understand the subject but is armed with a clever oversight procedure, draw an expert-quality answer out of the model — that is, reliably steer a system they are themselves unable to evaluate?

Usually we oversee someone who understands the task less well than we do: the teacher checks the pupil, the editor corrects the intern. Future alignment is about the reverse. One day we will have to direct a system that understands the domain better than any living human, and then the familiar footing of "I do know what's right" will slip out from under us. Sandwiching is a way to rehearse that moment in advance, while the gap between the model and its overseer is still small and safe, while there is still an expert up top whose correct answer you can peek at to check whether it worked.

Take a task where the model is already more competent than a non-specialist but weaker than a specialist — a legal question, a medical consultation, a code review in an unfamiliar language. Bring in non-experts and give them not knowledge of the subject but a method of oversight: the ability to ask the model clarifying follow-up questions, to break the task into verifiable pieces, to pit two copies of the model against each other in debate, to call in assistant models for help. And see whether the non-experts pull the answer's quality up to the expert bar. The expert, meanwhile, does not take part in the process — they are needed only as ground truth, a reference ruler laid against the result at the very end. If the non-experts' answers measure up to the expert's, the method works. If not, it doesn't — and no fine words will hide that.

That ruler at the end is the idea's chief value. Scalable oversight lived for years as a philosophical anxiety: "how will we control what is smarter than us?" A noble question, but intangible — you cannot test it, you cannot run an experiment on it. Sandwiching translates the anxiety into a protocol and makes oversight falsifiable: you either have a procedure that empirically brings non-experts' answers up to expert level, or you do not, and you can find out on today's models, without waiting for the coming of superintelligence. This is a rare genre in alignment: a big scary question squeezed down to something that fits on a table between two slices of bread.

**How it came about.** The idea grew out of Ajeya Cotra's essay "The case for aligning narrowly superhuman models," published on the Alignment Forum in 2021. Cotra, a researcher at Open Philanthropy, asked a practical question: what should someone preparing for the alignment of future powerful systems actually work on today, if those systems do not exist yet? Her answer — work with "narrowly superhuman" models: systems that in some narrow area already surpass the typical human, though they are far from general superintelligence. GPT-3 knows more about medicine than the average passerby — so let us learn to extract this knowledge using people who do not know medicine themselves. This essay is where the sandwich picture appeared: a model wedged between a weak and a strong human. A year later, in 2022, a team led by Sam Bowman turned the metaphor into a real testbed — the work "Measuring Progress on Scalable Oversight for Large Language Models" took tasks like answering questions about long texts and checked whether a dialogue with an assistant model helps a human answer better than they would alone. Thus the metaphor acquired numbers.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

First, today's sandwich may poorly predict tomorrow's. The gap "dilettante — expert — model" and the gap "human — superintelligence" are perhaps qualitatively different beasts. In the present experiment the model simply does not know something or errs; in the frightening future scenario the system may be malevolent — not stupid but actively disloyal, able to figure out your oversight procedure and outplay it. Rehearsing an obedient orchestra tells you nothing about what to do with musicians who have decided to sabotage the concert.

Second, the reference expert is not the gold standard we would like it to be. Experts err, argue among themselves, and in some fields a "correct answer" simply does not exist. Worse still, an expert is not always available: precisely where we are most anxious about oversight, expert ground truth may not exist at all, and then there is nothing to lay the whole construction against.

Third, the narrow does not transfer to the general. A procedure that succeeds on legal questions is not guaranteed to work on a task you did not break down in advance into convenient sub-questions. Laboratory victories have a nasty habit of not surviving in the wild.

And fourth — the most essential. Classic sandwiching does not cover deceptive alignment. The model in these experiments is not trying to outwit you; it is honestly, if imperfectly, giving its all. But deception is exactly the nightmare that all of alignment was set up to address. Testing oversight on a conscientious model is like testing an alarm by inviting over a person known to be honest: pleasant, but you learn nothing about burglars.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Sandwiching does not claim to be a final solution — it has settled into the niche of a measuring instrument, and that is an honest niche. Around it stands a whole family of approaches to scalable oversight, some of them older than sandwiching itself, that today form a small ecosystem. Debate (AI safety via debate) — where two copies of the model argue before a human judge, and the hope is that exposing another's lie is easier than lying oneself. Iterated amplification and recursive reward modeling — attempts to assemble a reliable evaluation signal out of many small steps, each individually verifiable by a human. And in 2023 there appeared a close relative and heir of sandwiching — weak-to-strong generalization, "the weak oversees the strong": in OpenAI's work by Burns and colleagues a weak model is made the teacher of a strong one, and they see whether the strong pupil can outgrow the erring mentor. The same question as Cotra's, only now it is not a human in the dilettante's seat, but a deliberately weakened model.

Where does it lead in practice? Toward an empirical discipline where claims about safety must be backed by measurements, not declarations. Sandwiching taught the field an important habit: if you cannot sit your oversight method down at a table and check it against numbers, it is not yet a method but a pious wish. The toughest nut left to crack is learning to make the same honest measurements for models that can deceive you. For now the sandwich is assembled from a conscientious filling. The real exam will begin when something that does not want to be overseen gets laid between the slices of bread.
