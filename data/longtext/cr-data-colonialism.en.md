### Data Colonialism (Nick Couldry & Ulises A. Mejias, 2019)

Picture a sixteenth-century cartographer who, mapping a coastline for the first time, declares it "no one's land" — *terra nullius*, empty, belonging to nobody, and therefore free for the taking. The land, of course, was not empty: people lived on it with their own customs and rights. But the legal fiction of emptiness made the seizure lawful. Now replace the coastline with your everyday life — your correspondence, your routes, your pauses before a purchase, the tone of your voice, the heart rate from your fitness tracker. Couldry and Mejias argue: over this landscape the cartographer's pencil is raised again, and the formula *terra nullius* rings out once more. Only now what is appropriated is not a territory but the very fabric of human existence, translated into data.

"Data colonialism" is the thesis that turning life into a countable resource is not a side effect of technology and not a market accident but a **new historical turn in colonial seizure**. Just as classical colonialism appropriated land, labor, and bodies in order to launch industrial capitalism, so data colonialism appropriates social life as raw material on which the capitalism of the coming century will be built. The key move here: tracking has ceased to be a scandal and become the norm. We no longer ask why we are being watched — we ask whether the app doing the watching is convenient enough. This normalization of total surveillance is, according to Couldry and Mejias, the ideological work of the new colonialism: to make the seizure invisible, natural, even desirable.

The authors describe the mechanism through the notion of *data relations*. This is a specially constructed form of connection between a person and a corporation, in which the very fact of your existence continuously generates extractable value. You do not sell your data and usually do not even hand it over consciously — you simply live, and the infrastructure is built so that life automatically settles onto someone else's servers as capital. This is exactly where it connects with the logic of *terra nullius*: the colonizer never needed to ask permission from someone whose existence he had declared legally nonexistent. In just the same way, a click on "Accept all cookies" is the modern version of a contract that one party never read and could not contest.

**How it came about.** The concept ripened in the articles of Couldry (a professor of media and social theory at the LSE) and Mejias (a communications researcher at SUNY Oswego, a native of Mexico — which matters for his sensitivity to the colonial optic) in the second half of the 2010s. It took shape in the book "The Costs of Connection: How Data Is Colonizing Human Life and Appropriating It for Capitalism" (Stanford University Press, 2019) and in a programmatic article in the journal *Television & New Media* of the same year. The year of publication is symbolic: 2019 is also the year of Zuboff's "Age of Surveillance Capitalism." Two fundamental diagnoses of the digital era came out almost simultaneously and have since carried on an unspoken dispute. Later, in 2024, the authors released a more journalistic sequel — "Data Grab: The New Colonialism of Big Tech and How to Fight Back," where they translated the theory into a plan of resistance.

It is precisely the dispute with Zuboff that throws the concept into relief. For Zuboff, surveillance capitalism is an **anomaly**, a parasitic mutation of the market, a "hijacking" of human experience that can in principle be cured by regulation, restoring capitalism to a healthy course. Couldry and Mejias object sharply: there is no anomaly, there is the logical continuation of what capitalism always did — expropriation. Surveillance did not break the system — it opened up a new continent for it to conquer. Hence the choice of the word "colonialism": it drags behind it history, guilt, and structure, whereas "surveillance" sounds like a technical problem of privacy settings. The difference is not cosmetic — it determines where to look for the cause and what to count as the cure.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The most serious objection is historical and ethical: doesn't the metaphor cheapen real colonialism? Opponents (among them some postcolonial scholars) point out that historical colonialism meant genocide, slavery, the physical extermination of peoples, and centuries of material plunder. To compare targeted advertising with this is to risk a blasphemous analogy and "metaphorical inflation," where a strong word is worn out by light use. Couldry and Mejias answer that they speak not of identity but of the continuity of the **logic** of seizure, and that the new colonialism has a very material underside — lithium mines, electronic dumps, the labor of data labelers in the global South. But the charge still stands.

The second objection concerns agency. In the picture of total seizure, the human looks like a pure victim from whom life is "harvested," whereas empirical research shows a wealth of practices of resistance, circumvention, and appropriation of technologies by users themselves. Critics say: the frame is too monolithic, it underrates agency and diversity. The third is practical: a grand diagnosis yields few concrete steps. If this is the colonialism of the whole era, then what is to be done on a Tuesday morning, besides noble despair? "Data Grab" partly answers this, but the gap between the scale of the problem and the scale of the proposed remedies remains the Achilles' heel of the whole critical-humanities tradition.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For the engineer and the alignment researcher, the concept is useful as a **shift in the level of observation**. The usual conversation about "training data" is conducted in terms of quality, labeling, and licenses. The optic of data colonialism forces a different question: whose life became the raw material of this model, who gave consent, who received the value, and who was left with only the costs? This connects directly with the problem of the concentration of power: if everyone's life is turned into an asset for the few, then aligning AI "with human values" risks meaning alignment with the values of those who own the infrastructure of seizure. Alignment without the question "in whose favor" becomes a technique serving the colonizer.

Hence three directions. The first — a **decolonial ethics of AI**, developed by Abeba Birhane (her work "Algorithmic Colonization of Africa," 2020, shows how Western AI solutions reproduce colonial dependence) and by authors turning to non-Western ethical traditions, for example the philosophy of *ubuntu*. Here data colonialism turns from a diagnosis into a demand: technologies must be designed to reflect a multiplicity of worldviews, not just one. The second — the material line, continued by Kate Crawford in "Atlas of AI" (2021): to look at AI not as an abstraction in the cloud but as a chain from rare-earth metals to clickworkers, one that has geographically concrete victims. The third — the institutional: indigenous data sovereignty, collective rather than individual rights to data, cooperative models of ownership. The logic is simple: if the problem lies in the structure of ownership, then the solution too is structural — not "privacy settings" but a different answer to the question of who owns digitized life.

The value of the beast named "data colonialism" is that it refuses to consider the present state of things natural. It reminds us: what seems to be simply "how the internet works" was once decided, formalized, and declared empty land, ready for the taking. And if it was decided — it can be revised.
