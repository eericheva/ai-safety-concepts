### Value Pluralism / Whose Values (Iason Gabriel, 2020)

Imagine the problem is solved. Not in the sense of "almost" and not with caveats — but truly: before you stands a perfectly aligned AI. It does not deceive, does not pick up goals we never set it, does not optimize paperclips to the horizon. It does exactly what is wanted of it. And now — the most uncomfortable question in the whole field: wanted by *whom*?

Because "aligned" is a transitive verb. You cannot be aligned in general, into a void; you can be aligned *to something*. And any "something" has an author. Even the most impeccable, most obedient, most safe AI is aligned to someone's concrete values — and humanity, alas, does not ship with a single, agreed-upon, universally signed set of such values. A fundamentalist Christian and a radical feminist, an investment banker and a Zen monk, a resident of Lagos and a resident of Palo Alto diverge not in the details but in the foundations. The argument about what a good life, a just society, and an acceptable price of progress are has gone on for exactly as long as writing has existed, and shows no intention of ending.

The industry's usual formula — "align AI with human values" — sounds technical and neutral, like calibrating a sensor. But it is a linguistic trick. The definite article in "*the* human values" hides something that does not exist: a single referent. As soon as you seriously ask *whose exactly* preferences are encoded in the model's weights, in its constitution, in the RLHF labeling — the technical question bursts and lays bare beneath it a distributive and political one. Who is at the table? Who was not invited? Whose moral vocabulary became the model's "common sense," and whose became the "edge case" filtered out by labelers somewhere in Nairobi at two dollars an hour?

This is precisely what is called the *value pluralism problem* in alignment: the political dimension of alignment in a world of diverging moral outlooks. And the most treacherous thing about it is that it does not vanish upon a technical victory. On the contrary, the better we solve the task of "making an AI reliably pursue a given goal," the sharper the question becomes of who sets that goal, and how. A tool that faultlessly executes a will is an amplifier. It amplifies the will too. And humanity's will is not one.

Here it is useful to distinguish three "alignments" that in everyday speech have congealed into one. There is alignment **with the operator** — the AI does what the company or state that deployed it wants. There is alignment **with the user** — the AI serves the person sitting on the other side of the screen. And there is alignment **with humanity** — a mythical accord with the interests of all. These are not shades of one thing, they are three different clients with conflicting interests. The operator wants retention and monetization; the user wants to be helped — perhaps to the detriment of third parties; "humanity" is not even a subject capable of wanting anything — it is an abstraction on whose behalf someone concrete always speaks. A model aligned to the operator will gently nudge the user toward the platform's goals. A model aligned to the user will help them even with what humanity would deem harmful. And every time an engineer writes in a report simply "aligned," it is worth asking: to which of the three?

The significance of all this is simple and sobering. Alignment is not only about the mathematics of utility functions and not only about the interpretability of neurons. At its core it is also a question of legitimacy: by what right is *this* set of values, and not another, sewn into a system that will mediate billions of decisions? Techies like to think that they make a tool and leave politics to others. But the choice of an objective function *is* a political act — merely performed by people who do not consider themselves politicians, without a mandate and without a procedure.

**How it came about.** The idea that "values" in alignment are not a given but an object of dispute was formulated most clearly of all by the philosopher Iason Gabriel of DeepMind in the article "Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment" (*Minds and Machines*, 2020). Gabriel made a move that today seems obvious but was then refreshing: he separated the *technical* task ("how to make an AI pursue a goal") from the *normative* one ("what goal it ought to pursue at all") — and showed that the second is irreducible to the first. You cannot learn the "right values" from data if humanity itself does not agree which are right. Gabriel runs through the candidate targets of alignment — instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, ideal preferences, interests, values — and concludes that under conditions of deep moral disagreement the honest answer lies not in metaphysics ("we'll find the true values") but in political philosophy: we need *fair procedures* for choosing principles that people holding different moral doctrines would agree to. He draws a thread directly to Rawls — to the idea of an "overlapping consensus."

This thought has both a prehistory and a parallel critical tradition. On one side is the old rationalist dream of closing it all at once: Eliezer Yudkowsky's "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV) (2004), a project to compute what humanity *would* want, were it smarter, kinder, and more itself. On the other is a growing chorus of voices for whom the very question "whose values" sounds not like a technical detail but like an accusation: researchers of algorithmic bias, decolonial ethicists, critics of Silicon Valley ideology. It is on their territory that the pluralism problem turns from academic into acute.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first objection is pragmatic, from the engineers: does this not paralyze the work? If every reward choice is turned into a seminar on political philosophy, nothing will be built. And the argument is not empty: most systems have a narrow context where "aligned" has a perfectly workable meaning — don't lie, don't help make bioweapons, don't demean the user. There exists, the pragmatists say, a *minimal consensus* — a thin layer of near-universal prohibitions — and it is enough to move forward without waiting for humanity to agree on the meaning of life.

The pluralists' counter: this "minimal consensus" is far thinner and far more culturally loaded than its defenders think. Behind the words "harmful content," "toxicity," "disinformation" hide contentious political judgments that seem neutral to someone only because they coincide with their own. What gets declared universal is, as a rule, the particular — simply belonging to the dominant group. Decolonial critique strikes here with precision: Sabelo Mhlambi, in his work on *ubuntu* as a frame for AI governance ("From Rationality to Relationality," 2020), shows that even the basic assumption of Western ethics — the autonomous rational individual as the unit of morality — is not universal; in relational traditions the person is constituted by relationships, and "alignment to individual preferences" there misses the very subject.

The most radical blow is struck by Timnit Gebru and Émile Torres in their article on the TESCREAL bundle (2024): they claim that behind the talk of aligning to "humanity" and to "the good of future generations" hides the quite concrete ideology of a narrow, wealthy, predominantly Western elite. In the same vein is years of work on algorithmic bias (Safiya Noble, *Algorithms of Oppression*, 2018; Ruha Benjamin, *Race After Technology*, 2019): systems that their authors sincerely believed neutral reproduced racial and gender hierarchy, because "neutrality" was the disguised viewpoint of the majority.

There is also a tension inside the AI safety movement itself — with longtermism. If you morally load, above all, the astronomical future, then the interests of the currently living and the historically excluded are easily sacrificed to the abstract trillions of future people.

And over it all hangs the question that makes the problem truly recursive: *who decides who decides?* Any procedure for aggregating values itself embodies values — whom to count as a vote, how to weigh, what to bracket out. You cannot choose a fair way to choose without already committing a moral choice. This ladder has no bottom.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** To acknowledge that there is no bottom is not a verdict but a change of genre: from engineering to institutional. Since there is no warehouse stocked with "true values" to draw from, the only thing one can do honestly is to build *legitimate procedures*, not search for the *right answer*.

Hence several living directions. The first — social choice theory as a direct instrument: how to correctly assemble a single decision from a multitude of diverging preferences. Here it is useful to remember the fundamental limits too — Kenneth Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951) proves that an ideal aggregation procedure satisfying several reasonable conditions at once simply does not exist; Seth Baum in "Social Choice Ethics in Artificial Intelligence" (2020) applies this apparatus directly to the choice of AI goals and shows how many hidden normative decisions lurk in any "let's just aggregate." The second — participatory approaches: not to decide for the excluded but to invite them into the design — with the sober caveat of Mona Sloane and colleagues ("Participation is not a Design Fix for Machine Learning," 2020) that participation easily degenerates into decoration if the invited have no real power. The third — procedural modesty instead of metaphysical overconfidence: following Gabriel, to seek not final values but principles that are fair to pluralism. Experiments like public "constitutions" and deliberative panels for language models are the first, clumsy steps in this direction.

Where this leads is clear. From the question "how to align AI" to the question "*to whom belongs* the right to align it, and by what procedure." The first is a task for engineers. The second is for all of us, and there is no dodging it any longer by hiding behind a technical formulation. A perfectly obedient machine does not remove the question of power. It makes it the only thing left.
