### Mary's Room (Frank Jackson, 1982/1986)

Picture Mary. She is a brilliant neurophysiologist, but her whole life has passed in a black-and-white room, behind a black-and-white monitor. She has never seen anything colored: not a red tomato, not a blue sky, not her own skin flushed with excitement. Yet she knows *everything* about color — everything that can be learned by physical means. She has learned by heart which wavelengths a ripe strawberry reflects, how a photon excites the retina's cones, along which fibers the signal runs, which neurons of the visual cortex flare up in the process, what words a person utters at that moment. The complete, finished physical science of vision fits inside her head.

And now let us open the door. Mary steps outside — or is simply handed a ripe tomato. For the first time in her life she sees red.

Jackson's question is utterly simple: **does she learn anything new?**

Almost everyone's intuition answers: yes, of course. "Ah, *so this* is what it's like to see red." And here the trap snaps shut. If Mary knew everything physical about color — and yet learned something new — then there was something to learn beyond the physical. That means a complete physical description of the world leaves some facts out. And those facts are about *what it is like*, about the subjective quality of experience, about what philosophers call qualia. The conclusion: physicalism is false. The world is not exhausted by physics.

### The mechanism of the argument

Formally it works as a small, flawless syllogism. Before leaving the room, Mary knows all the physical facts about color vision. After leaving, she learns a new fact. Therefore, there is a fact that is not physical. Everything hangs on a single step — on the word "fact." This single word is exactly what people would spend the next forty years fighting over.

The beauty of the thought experiment is that it turns an ancient dispute about materialism into an almost tangible scene. Not an abstract "what if consciousness is non-physical," but a concrete woman at a concrete door. Jackson strikes not at the opponent's logic but at his imagination: he makes you *feel* the gap between "knowing about an experience" and "having an experience." Between a library about red and a flash of red in the eyes.

### How it came about

The argument did not come out of nowhere. In 1974 Thomas Nagel published his famous "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" — an essay about how even complete knowledge of echolocation gives us no access to what it is like from the inside, for the bat itself. Nagel found the gap; Jackson drove a wedge into it.

Frank Jackson himself, an Australian philosopher, in the article **"Epiphenomenal Qualia"** (*The Philosophical Quarterly*, 1982) defended a rather exotic position — epiphenomenalism: qualia are real, non-physical, but causally powerless, a mere "by-product" of physical processes, affecting nothing. Mary was one of the two cases on which he built his knowledge argument (the second concerned a man named Fred, who distinguishes two shades of red that are indistinguishable to us). In 1986, replying to Paul Churchland's objections, Jackson refined and sharpened the formulation in the article **"What Mary Didn't Know"** (*The Journal of Philosophy*). The canonical formulation is taken to be the 1982 paper, but it was this short note, with its premises sharply set out, that spread through the anthologies.

### Criticism / arguments against

A whole army lined up against Mary, and this is perhaps the most interesting thing about her fate.

**The ability hypothesis.** David Lewis in his essay "What Experience Teaches" (1988) and, earlier and independently, Laurence Nemirow proposed an elegant move: Mary does acquire something on leaving the room — but not *knowledge-that*, not a new fact, but *knowledge-how*, a skill. She gains abilities: now she can imagine red, recognize it on a fresh encounter, remember it. To know what it is like is not to possess a portion of information but to have a skill. And new abilities add exactly nothing to the list of facts about the world. A child who has learned to ride a bicycle has not discovered a new law of nature — they've simply gained an ability. Mary did not learn a new fact; she acquired a skill.

**An old fact under a new mode of presentation.** Another camp — associated with the names of Brian Loar, John Perry, and others — says: Mary learns not a new fact but an old, already-known fact given to her now in a different way. One and the same physical process can be grasped "from without," through a physical concept, and "from within," through a phenomenal concept. Like "Venus is the morning star" and "Venus is the evening star": one planet, two ways of thinking about it. Mary acquires a new phenomenal *concept* of red, but the referent remains the same old neurophysical fact. No addition to the ontology — only to the mode of apprehension.

**Acquaintance.** A third answer is more modest still in its claims: what Mary receives does not belong to the category of propositional knowledge at all. It is acquaintance — a direct, immediate relation to the experience itself, rather like being acquainted with a person as opposed to merely knowing about them from a dossier. Acquaintance is not expressed in facts and does not compete with physics for a place in the description of the world.

And the loudest twist of the plot: **the author renounced his own argument.** In works around the turn of the century, above all in **"Mind and Illusion"** (2003), Jackson declared that Mary no longer convinces him. He became a physicalist. His new diagnosis is subtle: the intuition "Mary learns something new" is so strong that it cannot be trusted directly — it rather exposes a flaw in our naive picture of perception, not a flaw in physicalism. We mistakenly think that experience presents us with some non-physical quality; in fact this is an illusion generated by a mistaken conception of the very nature of sensory experience. The creator of the trap ended up explaining why one shouldn't fall into it.

### What to do about it / where it leads

It might seem an old scholastic dispute — a tomato and a black-and-white room. But today Mary stands at a far more current door.

A large language model is literally Mary in the room. It has read *everything* about red: every poem about a sunset, every #FF0000 specification, every optics textbook, every confession of how it hurts to look at scarlet. It will link "red" with "passion," "blood," "stop signal" more precisely than any of us. And meanwhile not a single photon of 700 nanometers has ever fallen on its retina — there is no retina. Jackson's question comes back in full force and ceases to be an armchair matter: is there *something* that the model, knowing everything from text about red, still does not know — and does that "something" coincide with qualia?

Hence a direct link to a family of Bestiary concepts. Mary is the younger sister of Chalmers's **hard problem of consciousness**: why is information processing accompanied by experience at all, rather than by darkness? She is kin to the **philosophical zombie** — a being physically indistinguishable from us but empty inside: if a zombie is conceivable, physics underdetermines experience, and Mary learns exactly what the zombie is forever deprived of. And she gives us the cleanest instrument for holding in mind the distinction over which all talk of "conscious AI" stumbles: the distinction between **knowing about an experience** and **having an experience**.

The practical lesson for anyone thinking about alignment is twofold and double-edged. On one hand — sobriety: a system's ability to reason flawlessly about pain, fear, or joy says nothing about whether there is someone in there for whom it hurts. Competence in talking about qualia and the presence of qualia are different axes, and Mary won't let us conflate them. On the other hand — caution in the reverse direction: if Lewis and Nemirow are right and "having experience" is in the end a bundle of abilities rather than a spark of non-physical substance, then we have no simple grounds to declare in advance that in a sufficiently rich system of abilities "there is no one inside." Mary does not hand out a ready answer about machine consciousness. She does something more valuable — she keeps us from asking the question carelessly.

So the door of the black-and-white room has stood ajar for forty years now. We still do not know for certain whether Mary learns a new fact, acquires a skill, or simply gets reacquainted with an old acquaintance under a new name. But now on the far side of the threshold stands not only she, but our models too — likewise having read everything about red and never once having seen it.
