### The Pharmakon (Bernard Stiegler, 2010)

Let us begin with an injection. A diabetic carries insulin with them — a substance that keeps them alive. The same substance, administered at many times the dose, kills within hours. The difference between medicine and poison here lies not in the nature of the substance but in the measure, the dosage, in who applies it and how. The ancient Greeks knew this before we did and had one word for it — φάρμακον, *pharmakon*. It meant at once a remedy, a poison, and a sorcerer's potion. Not an "ambiguity" that the translator must resolve in favor of one meaning, but an ineradicable duality of the thing itself.

Bernard Stiegler takes this word and says: every technology is a pharmakon. Not "may have side effects," not "a double-edged sword" in the sense of the banal saying — but by its very structure both poison and medicine, and the one impossible to separate from the other. The writing that preserves our memory is the same writing that weans us off remembering. The smartphone that extends my connection to the world is the same smartphone that shatters my attention into shards. And — this matters for us — an AI system that amplifies my thinking is exactly the system that can replace that thinking. The question is not "good or bad" but "in what dose, under what care, managed by whom."

**The mechanism.** The human being, for Stiegler, is a creature that is prosthetic from the outset. We are not born with a ready-made memory of the species inscribed into instinct; we inherit a past we did not live through ourselves, by way of external carriers — tools, writing, rituals, media. Stiegler calls this *tertiary retention* (as distinct from Husserl's primary and secondary — that which is held in living perception and in personal memory). Tertiary retention is memory reified outside the body: the notch, the scroll, the photograph, the database. It is precisely this that makes us human. But it is precisely this that sets us up for a fall.

Because the very same external medium that carries knowledge can *take* that knowledge away from me — turn me from one who knows how into one who merely consumes a skill built into a machine. Stiegler borrows from Marx the word **proletarianization** and radically broadens it. The proletarian is not a pauper but one who has lost *knowledge*: the worker at the conveyor lost the knowledge of the craft (savoir-faire), which migrated into the machine; the consumer lost the knowledge of how to live (savoir-vivre), which migrated into marketing; and now, Stiegler says, we are losing the knowledge of how to think itself (savoir-théoriser), delegating it to algorithms. Proletarianization is when the prosthesis, instead of supporting an ability, atrophies it. The medicine turns into poison.

Hence his diagnosis of modernity: the **attention economy**. Attention is not a metaphor but a limited psychic and collective resource, one that is moreover shaped by culture (ambiguously in French: *l'attention* is at once attention, care, and expectation). A child learns sustained, deep attention by reading, listening, concentrating; on this rests the whole capacity to think and the whole capacity to care for another. Media built on the capture and resale of attention systematically undermine precisely this capacity for forming it — they exploit attention faster than it can reproduce itself. Stiegler speaks of the *destruction of attention* as a disease of whole generations.

What is to be done with a poison that cannot be thrown away, because it is also the medicine? Stiegler answers: what is needed is a **pharmacology** — but not a science of pills, rather a discipline of care, *le soin*. One can neither reject technology (this is impossible — we are constituted by it) nor surrender to it naively (this is fatal). One can only work therapeutically with the poisonous side while cultivating the healing one: change the dosage, invent practices, institutions, an upbringing that turn the pharmakon into a remedy. Care is precisely the art of dosage.

**How it came about.** The plot is two and a half thousand years older than Stiegler himself. In Plato's *Phaedrus* the Egyptian god Theuth brings King Thamus an invention — writing — and praises it as a *pharmakon* for memory and wisdom. The king objects: this remedy is not for memory but for *reminding*; people will cease to remember from within and will rely on external signs, gaining not wisdom but its semblance. Plato, characteristically, records this warning against writing — in writing.

In 1968 Jacques Derrida, in the essay "Plato's Pharmacy" (in the collection *Dissemination*, 1972), shows: Plato wanted to fix upon writing an unambiguously bad meaning, but the very word *pharmakon* resists this — it oscillates between poison and medicine and does not let metaphysics draw a clean boundary between "living" speech and "dead" writing. Derrida makes of this a figure of undecidability.

Stiegler is Derrida's student, but he turns the line of thought. He takes the undecidability not as a trap of language but as an *ontological condition of technology* and turns it into a program. In the multivolume *Technics and Time* (1994–2001), beginning with the first volume, *The Fault of Epimetheus*, he builds a philosophy of the human as a being that invents itself through tools. And in the 2010 book — *What Makes Life Worth Living: On Pharmacology* — he puts the word *pharmacology* into the title for the first time and makes it the center of his late thought, linking it to a critique of digital capitalism, the destruction of attention, and to the work of the association he founded, Ars Industrialis.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first objection is tautology. If *everything* is a pharmakon, if any technology is at once poison and medicine, then the thesis ceases to distinguish anything: it is always true and therefore predicts nothing concrete. Critics say that "pharmacology" risks becoming an elegant way of solving nothing — for the dosage must be determined anew each time, and Stiegler gives no universal criterion.

The second — the political vagueness of *le soin*. "Care" sounds noble, but who does the dosing? Stiegler appeals to new institutions, to upbringing, to a non-commercial "economy of contribution," but the concrete mechanisms remain a sketch; where an analysis of power and capital is needed, a moralizing rhetoric of care sometimes stands in its place. From the standpoint of political economy (for instance, among the theorists of surveillance capitalism) this looks like a lack of structural analysis.

The third — a trace of cultural pessimism. The anxiety about the "loss of attention" and the "proletarianization of the spirit" comes dangerously close to the eternal genre of complaints that the younger generation reads the wrong things in the wrong way — a genre which, as the example of Plato already showed, recurs at every change of media and each time turns out both right and wrong. To separate real degradation from the panic of elders is a task that the concept itself does not solve.

And the fourth, a counter-objection: determinism in reverse. In insisting that technology constitutes us, Stiegler risks overestimating its formative power and underestimating how much it is practices, not devices, that determine the outcome. The same smartphone in different hands is a different pharmakon; then perhaps it isn't about the technology at all?

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The value of the pharmakon lies not in prediction but in posing the right question. It forbids the two most common positions in the conversation about AI: rapturous **solutionism** ("a new technology will save us all by itself") and its mirror image, alarmism ("switch it off and ban it"). Both are equally naive, because both ascribe to technology an unambiguous sign that it does not have.

For the "Bestiary" the pharmakon is a nodal creature, drawing together several of our motifs. It continues Jacques Ellul's **autonomous technology**, but disputes its fatalism: for Stiegler technology is not hopelessly autonomous, it can be *treated*. It gives a philosophical root to what Shoshana Zuboff would later describe empirically as **surveillance capitalism** — the turning of attention and experience into raw material. It converges with Ivan Illich's **conviviality**: for Illich the tool, too, crosses a critical threshold beyond which it turns from servant into master — and both seek that dose at which the instrument remains commensurate with the human being.

And the applied question is this: does a large language model amplify my thinking — or replace it? The answer, according to Stiegler: both, and the choice between them is not built into the model, it is decided by the practice of use and the institution of upbringing. An assistant that writes my draft so that I can think further is medicine. The same assistant, to which I turn in order *not to think at all*, is poison, atrophying the very ability it was meant to serve. The dosage is prescribed not by me alone and not by the engineer — it is set by the culture of attention that we either build or lose.

The pharmakon gives no peace, and that is its honesty: it does not promise a side that, once you take it, lets you stop thinking. It demands holding both meanings at once — and treating.
