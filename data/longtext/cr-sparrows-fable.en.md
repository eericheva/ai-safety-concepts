### The Unfinished Fable of the Sparrows (Nick Bostrom, 2014)

It was the height of the nesting season, and a flock of sparrows, worn out by days of hard toil, was resting in the evening light — chirping, dozing, complaining about life. And then someone put forward an idea that took everyone's breath away. What if they got themselves an owl? Not for the soup — as a helper. An owl is strong, sharp-eyed, flies without a sound. It could carry twigs, look after the elderly and the young, keep an eye out for the neighborhood cat, and the sparrows would at last live in ease and plenty. "Let us send out scouts in all directions," proposed Pastus, the elder-bird, "and try to find an abandoned owlet somewhere, or maybe an egg." The flock burst into delighted chatter, already imagining how easily life would flow beneath the wing of a mighty protector.

And only one sparrow — Scronkfinkle, one-eyed, nervous, with a perpetually anxious temperament — dared to object. "This will surely be our undoing," he croaked. "Should we not first give some thought to the art of taming and domesticating owls, before we bring such a creature into our midst?" Reasonable. But the flock was already worked up. "Taming an owl sounds exceedingly difficult," Pastus waved him off. "It will be difficult enough to find an owl egg. So let us start there. Once we've managed to raise an owl, we can think about taking on that other challenge." "There is a flaw in that plan!" squeaked Scronkfinkle. His objections, however, were drowned in the clamor: the flock had already taken wing in search.

Only two or three remained. They set about racking their brains over how to tame an owl. It soon became clear that, with no owl on hand — not even one, for practice — thinking about it was agonizingly hard. And yet they wrestled with the problem as best they could, gnawed by the fear that the flock would return with an egg before a solution was found. Here the fable breaks off. Bostrom confesses that he does not know how it ended.

**The moral.** Power comes easily, and controlling it is hard, and so there is a perpetual temptation to put off the second until "later," once the first is already in hand. But "later" may never come: the owlet you meant to get around to taming later grows into a predator while you are off looking for the next twig. The fable is about a fatal reordering of priorities: capability runs ahead of safety. Work on alignment (that very "art of taming") cannot be deferred until the moment the system already exists — because by then you may have no levers of influence left. First learn to control, then summon the force. Not the other way around.

This is precisely why Bostrom prefaced this parable — and the whole book — with a dedication: **"To Scronkfinkle and his followers."** The few whom no one heeded.

**How it came about.** The fable opens *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* (Oxford University Press, 2014) — the treatise that essentially started the modern public conversation about the risks of strong AI. The owl looks out from the cover for a reason: it is that very superintelligent system which humanity, like the sparrows, has resolved to take in without really understanding its nature. Shortly after the book's release, the Oxford University Press blog published the excerpt with the fable (August 2014) — and the parable took on a life of its own, reproduced in reviews, lectures, and debates. Bostrom, a philosopher and founder of Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute, wrote it as an epigraph-warning: not a prophecy of inevitable doom, but a diagnosis of a collective psychology: enthusiasm about a powerful tool systematically outpaces sober-mindedness about controlling it. The sparrows here are not fools. They are merely in a hurry, there are many of them, and each one imagines that caution is somebody else's concern.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The fable, like the whole book, was met not only with applause.

First, the charge of scaremongering. Skeptics observe: the parable emotionally prejudges its conclusion. The owl is appointed a predator in advance, before any inquiry — and so the reader is gently led toward panic, bypassing the question of whether future systems really will prove uncontrollable. Rhetoric substitutes for proof: frightening is not the same as likely.

Second, and this is more serious, the analogy limps at its most vulnerable point. The owl does not build itself. The sparrows receive a ready-made predator whose nature is set by evolution and does not depend on them. But humans do not find superintelligence in an abandoned nest — they design it, layer by layer, train it, test it, discard versions. We have what the sparrows lack: access to the "egg" at every stage, and the ability to influence what the chick will hatch into. If so, the panicky "taming first, owl second" turns into a false dilemma — taming and rearing go hand in hand, not strictly one after the other.

Third, a call to caution easily turns into a brake. Absolutize the fable's moral, and you arrive at the conclusion that nothing can be built until a problem is solved that seemingly cannot be solved at all apart from the actual system (those very two or three sparrows struggling to tame an owl without an owl). The result is paralysis: safety requires experience, experience requires systems, and systems are forbidden until safety is guaranteed. Critics reasonably ask whether caution ends up devouring itself.

**What to do about it / what it teaches.** And yet it is too soon to write the fable off — read honestly, it says not "stop" but "distribute your forces." Note: Scronkfinkle does not forbid the search for an owl. He asks that part of the flock take up taming *in parallel*, not afterward. This is the practical conclusion — not a moratorium, but priority and proportion: the share of effort invested in controllability must grow together with the system's power, not trail behind it.

The fable is the keystone of a whole bestiary. Threads run from it to all the other monsters. To the gorilla problem: the sparrows risk repeating the fate of gorillas, whose very existence now depends entirely on the whim of a more intelligent species that they spawned but do not control. To the control problem — the very one over which the two or three who stayed behind struggle in vain: how to hold on to a system that surpasses you in everything. To the drive for power: the owl is dangerous not because it is evil, but because almost any sufficiently ambitious goal pushes a powerful agent to accumulate resources and influence — and makes it harder to tame the stronger it grows. From here too — a distrust of the temptation to "raise it first and sort it out later": recall the young CEO handed unlimited authority on their word of honor that they would not abuse it. And, finally, to the pivotal act — the dream that it is enough to tame the owl for a single key task (to place two identical strawberries on a plate, and thereby stake out a safe future) — a dream that itself demands exactly the level of control we do not yet have.

What the sparrow parable teaches is modesty in the order of operations. Not "don't summon the owl" — but "don't summon it before you understand how to live with it." Between these two phrases lies the whole discipline of alignment. And, perhaps, our whole future: for Bostrom never did finish writing how it ended. The ending is ours to write.
