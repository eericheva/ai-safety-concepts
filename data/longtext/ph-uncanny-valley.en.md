### The Uncanny Valley (Masahiro Mori, 1970)

Take a close look at a prosthetic hand from the middle of the last century. Outwardly it is almost a living hand: pink skin, fingernails, little creases at the knuckles. And that is exactly why it makes you uneasy. As long as the prosthesis was an obvious hook, it evoked sympathy and respect for its owner; but the moment it became "almost a hand," the brain refuses to round it out into something alive, stumbling on the coldness, the stillness, the slightly wrong hue. It was on this very example that the roboticist Masahiro Mori in 1970 built his famous conjecture: the more humanlike an object, the more likable — but only up to a certain boundary, at which likability plunges into sharp revulsion.

Mori drew a graph. On the horizontal axis — the degree of human likeness, from an industrial manipulator to a living person. On the vertical axis — the emotional response, *shinwakan* (the hard-to-translate Japanese "sense of closeness, kinship"). The curve climbs confidently upward: a toy robot is dearer than a machine tool, a humanoid dearer than a toy robot. And then — right at the mark "indistinguishable from a human" — it crashes into a deep dip. At the bottom of this dip live corpses, zombies, prostheses, overly realistic androids. And only after passing through the valley does the response soar again toward the summit: on the climb beyond the valley stands Mori's bunraku puppet — not very realistic up close, but captivating from the audience's seat — and at the very top of the second peak, a healthy living person. Between "cute robot" and "human" lies not a smooth ascent but a chasm.

A key detail often forgotten: Mori maintained that **movement amplifies the effect**. A motionless corpse is eerie; a stirring corpse is unbearable. The amplitude of the dip for moving objects is, according to Mori, deeper than for static ones. And so a twitching prosthesis, a blinking android, or the slightly desynchronized facial expression of a digital avatar strikes harder than a photograph.

Why do we even have this dip? Mori was an engineer, not a neuroscientist, and offered a moral conclusion rather than a mechanism: do not chase full likeness; it makes more sense to stop at the first peak — to make the robot amiably stylized rather than frighteningly real. As to the cause, he offered only a passing guess: that eeriness is part of the instinct for self-preservation, guarding us against dangers close at hand, such as corpses and other species. The mechanisms were worked out after him, and there are several. The pathogen-detection hypothesis: an "almost human" with wrong features reads as a sick, infected, or dead conspecific — and an ancient revulsion switches on, guarding against pathogens and corpses. The expectation-violation hypothesis: the more human the appearance, the stricter our expectations of behavior, and the tiniest mismatch (dead eyes with a living smile) gives a sharp error signal. The categorical-uncertainty hypothesis: the object gets stuck between "human" and "non-human," and the very impossibility of assigning it to a category disturbs us. Finally, the threat-to-distinctiveness hypothesis: an almost-human blurs the boundary that makes us unique, and subconsciously reminds us of our own mortality and replaceability.

**How it came about.**
The essay "Bukimi no tani" ("The Valley of Eeriness") appeared in 1970 in the small-circulation Japanese journal *Energy* — a short, almost informal three-page text, without a single experiment. The word *bukimi* means "eerie, sinister," and this is a direct echo of an older line of thought about the disquieting: Ernst Jentsch in 1906 wrote of the anxiety arising from the impossibility of telling whether what is before you is living or mechanical (wax figures, automata), and Sigmund Freud in 1919 developed this in his essay on *das Unheimliche* — the "uncanny" as something at once alien and achingly familiar. Mori translated this aesthetic-psychoanalytic intuition into the language of robotics engineering.

For decades the idea lived almost as an urban legend among roboticists — cited but unverified, because there was no proper canonical English translation. Only in 2012 did Karl MacDorman and Norri Kageki publish an authorized English translation in *IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine* — and it was from this moment that the "uncanny valley" finally entered the mainstream of cognitive science, game design, and HCI; its serious empirical testing had by then already been under way since the mid-2000s.

**Criticism / arguments against.**
The beautiful curve turned out to be surprisingly hard for science. The first objection is **weak reproducibility**. Mori measured nothing; he presented an intuition in the form of a graph. When experimenters took up the testing, the results scattered: a review by Jari Kätsyri and co-authors (2015) went through the main hypotheses and found that the canonical U-shaped curve as a function of human likeness was supported by only one study out of eight; what was robustly supported instead was a different hypothesis — that eeriness comes from a *mismatch of features*, a blend of the artificial and the human in one face. In other words, the valley exists, but under special conditions and not for the reason Mori named. Nor did his signature claim about movement survive: not a single study showed that motion deepens the dip. A review by Wang, Lilienfeld, and Rochat (2015) in *Review of General Psychology* flatly called the effect's existence disputed, and the explanations competing and unproven.

The second — **it may be not a valley but a cliff**. Some data show not a dip-with-a-way-back-up but simply a sharp fall in liking at the boundary of realism with no subsequent rise: that is, the edge of a plateau, beyond which is a cliff, rather than a neat trough between two peaks. The shape of the curve is not a fact but an artifact of which stimuli and which scale you chose.

The third — **cultural and individual variability**. Attitudes toward androids, dolls, images of the dead depend heavily on culture (compare the Japanese bunraku and animatronic tradition with Western attitudes), on familiarity with the technology, on personal anxiety. A "universal" ancient mechanism sits poorly with the fact that the reaction is so plastic.

The fourth — **methodological**. Many "confirmations" were obtained on morphed faces (a smooth transition from a person's photo to a 3D model), where image-processing artifacts crop up — and it is unclear whether we are catching "the eeriness of the almost-human" or simply a visual defect. That said, in fairness: the work of Mathur and Reichling (2016) on a large set of real robot faces found a statistically measurable trough — so it is too early to bury the effect. The upshot is cautious: there is something there, but this "something" is narrower, smaller, and more capricious than the smooth curve of the 1970 essay.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**
For the "Bestiary" the uncanny valley is a beast at the junction of perception and trust, and it is directly about how we get along with AI.

First, it is the flip side of **anthropomorphism**. We readily endow machines with a face, a voice, and a character — but anthropomorphization has a cliff: overdo the humanness a little, and liking turns into distaste. The design of assistants balances right on this edge: an avatar too much like a living interlocutor, but with dead eyes and lagging facial expressions, will inspire less trust than an honestly stylized blob or a bodiless voice.

Second, it is a language for talking about **deepfakes and synthetic faces and voices**. The valley explains why early synthetic voices were "eerie," while today's ones alarm us no longer by eeriness but by smoothness: the technology has stepped over the dip, and now the danger is not that the fake repels us but that it no longer repels us. We lose the very signal "something is wrong here." The valley was a free detector of falsehood — and it is switching off.

Third, it is useful to keep the **ELIZA effect** nearby as the opposite pole. If the uncanny valley is excessive distrust of the almost-human, then the ELIZA effect is excessive trust in the obviously-non-human: people ascribe mind and sympathy to a simple program that substitutes templates. Between these two beasts lives the whole psychology of human-machine interaction: on one side, irrational revulsion; on the other, irrational attachment. Modern chatbots are interesting in that they have no face and no body, and so they bypass the visual valley — and fall straight into the embrace of the ELIZA effect. Text is never a corpse; text can be too warm.

The practical conclusion for the designer is exactly the one Mori himself proposed half a century ago: it is not always necessary to clamber toward the second peak. Often it is more honest and safer to stop at the first — to make the system pleasantly non-human, so that the user does not lose sight of whom (or rather, what) they are talking to. The uncanny valley reminds us: trust in AI is not a monotonic function of "resemblance to us," but a capricious terrain with cliffs. And the most disturbing turn of the plot is that technologies are already crossing the valley — and along with it vanishes that inner shudder which used to honestly warn us: before you is not a human.
