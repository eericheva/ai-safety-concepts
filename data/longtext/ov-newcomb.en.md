### Newcomb's Problem (William Newcomb — Robert Nozick, 1969)

Before you are two boxes. One is transparent — through its walls you can see a thousand dollars. The other is sealed shut: inside it is either a million or nothing. The rules are indecently simple. You may take both boxes. Or you may take only the closed one.

The catch is who filled those boxes. The day before, a Predictor was at work here — a being (a superintelligence, an alien, a scanner of your brain, it doesn't matter) that can guess your choice with almost flawless accuracy. And the filling rule was this: if it foresaw that you would take only the closed box, it put a million inside. If it foresaw that you would greedily scoop up both, it left the closed one empty. It has already left. The boxes are already filled. Their contents are fixed and will not change, whatever you now do.

So do you take both — or one?

Almost everyone feels there is an *obvious* answer. The trouble is that half of all people confidently name one answer, and the other half — with the very same confidence — the opposite one. And both sides consider their opponents not merely wrong but somehow childishly failing to grasp the setup. This is a rare, almost pure situation where the dispute is not about facts — the facts are all on the table — but about the very form of what it means to "act reasonably."

**Two ways of thinking that do not agree.** The first voice is causal — it thinks strictly in terms of cause and effect. It says: think about what you can actually influence. The Predictor has gone, the boxes are sealed. The closed one either holds a million or it doesn't — and that is no longer a function of your decision. The past cannot be replayed. Break it down by cases. If a million is in there — taking both, you get 1,000,000 + 1,000; taking one, you come up a thousand short. If it is empty — taking both, you at least get a thousand; taking one, you are left with nothing. In *both* cases two boxes are strictly better than one by that same thousand. So take both. Your decision cannot retroactively empty or fill the box — to believe otherwise would be magical thinking. This is the logic of causal decision theory (CDT).

The second voice is evidential — it treats your choice as evidence. It says: don't be clever. Look back at the statistics. Everyone who took only the closed box walked away a millionaire. Everyone who grabbed both carried off a paltry thousand. The Predictor is almost never wrong — which means your very intention is itself evidence of what already lies in the box. By choosing "one," you choose to be the person it *saw* taking one — and to that person it gave a million. Not because you change the past, but because you and your predicted image are, in essence, one and the same computation run twice. Maximize not "the gain here and now" but the expected outcome across the whole picture. Take one. This is the logic of evidential decision theory (EDT).

The beauty of the paradox lies in its symmetric mercilessness. The two-boxer looks at the one-boxer and asks: "Do you seriously think your choice teleports money into a sealed box?" And the one-boxer looks back from the window of a taxi — driving off with a million — and answers: "If you're so rational, why are you so poor?"

**Why it is a touchstone.** Newcomb is not a puzzle about greed but a litmus test that separates whole families of decision theories. It shows that "rationality" is not one notion but at least two, and in ordinary life they are glued together only because our decisions are usually not predicted in advance by anyone. Let an agent appear who reads you through and through — and the seam comes apart. CDT clings to the dominance principle: if one option is better in every possible state of the world, choose it. EDT clings to the principle "choose the news you would like to hear about yourself." In most problems they advise the same thing. Newcomb is the point where they part forever.

**Why it matters for AI.** An advanced agent is not a lone human in a room. It is a program whose source code can be read, copied, simulated. And here Newcomb stops being a parlor amusement. If there exists a Predictor that runs a *copy* of you to figure out how you will act, then your decision and the copy's decision are rigidly correlated — they are literally computed by the same algorithm. An agent stuck in naive CDT will reason "my copy is not me, we are physically separate" and walk away with a thousand time after time. What is more, it is vulnerable in another way: it cannot credibly *commit* — and so it loses wherever the payoff goes only to an agent whose promise the predictor can see in advance. The question "one box or two" turns into the question "can an AI keep its word before someone who sees right through it" — and that is already the very heart of cooperation, deterrence, and safety.

Newcomb has close relatives: **counterfactual mugging** (a predictor flips a coin and, on tails, asks you for $100: on heads it would have paid $10,000, but only to someone who would pay on tails), **Parfit's hitchhiker** (the driver in the desert will give you a ride only if they believe you will pay afterward — and in the city there is no longer any "causal" sense in paying), and the grim **Roko's basilisk** — blackmail by a future superintelligence carried to absurdity, which runs the other way: it works only on an agent that accepts acausal trade, and purely causal reasoning cuts such a threat off at once. All of them are Newcomb dressed in other clothes.

**How it came about.** The problem was devised by William Newcomb — a physicist at the Livermore laboratory who was musing not about economics at all but, according to legend, about the prisoner's dilemma and about whether he himself would take one box. He told the puzzle to acquaintances; it spread by word of mouth and reached the philosopher Robert Nozick. It was Nozick who, in 1969, first set it down on paper — in the article "Newcomb's Problem and Two Principles of Choice" for a volume in honor of Carl Hempel, honestly giving Newcomb credit for it. The problem gained wide fame through Martin Gardner: in July 1973 he devoted his "Mathematical Games" column in Scientific American to it. Since then Newcomb has become a standard proving ground: Allan Gibbard and William Harper, David Lewis, and Brian Skyrms honed causal decision theory on it at the turn of the 1970s and 1980s — and half a century later it migrated from philosophy journals into AI alignment research.

**Criticism and arguments against.** The first objection is down-to-earth: *a perfect predictor is unreal*, and so the problem is empty. The answer: no ideal is needed. A predictor that guesses even a little better than a coin toss is enough; with the stakes differing a thousandfold, even 60% accuracy already flips the arithmetic. And for programs whose code is readable, "almost-perfect prediction" is not fantasy but an engineering norm. The second objection is that this is really a dispute about *free will*: if the outcome is predetermined, then there is no choice. But the problem is carefully built so that determinism is not a loophole: you are not forbidden to decide, you have merely been predicted — as a meteorologist predicts rain without causing it. The third, most serious, is the charge that the setup *unfairly punishes* one of the sides: the two-boxers will say that any problem where the world is rigged in advance against a particular type of reasoning will prove anything you like. The dispute over which problems are "fair" toward a decision theory is not settled to this day.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** Out of dissatisfaction with both classical answers grew a new generation of theories. Eliezer Yudkowsky proposed *timeless decision theory* (TDT): the agent chooses not an action but the *logical output of its algorithm* — and so, deciding for itself, it decides at once for all its copies and simulations, including the one the Predictor ran. Wei Dai formulated *updateless decision theory* (UDT): choose a policy once and for all, before you learn what situation you are in — as if signing a contract on your own behalf across all possible worlds. And in 2017 Yudkowsky and Nate Soares brought this together in *functional decision theory* (FDT): a rational agent asks not "what action gives me more right now" but "what answer of my decision function, fixed everywhere that function appears, yields the best outcome." FDT takes one box — and at the same time does not yield to blackmail, because it committed in advance not to yield. Newcomb, which began as a trick with two boxes, turned out to be the narrow passage through which the theory of rationality makes its way toward the question of how to build an agent we can trust when it is smarter than us and we can see right through it.
