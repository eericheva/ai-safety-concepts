### Responsible Scaling Policy (Anthropic / METR, 2023)

Picture a fire alarm installed not after the fire but before the house is built — and, on top of that, agreed upon in advance: if the sensor shows such-and-such a concentration of smoke, the residents are obliged to leave, not to argue in the smoke about whether it is worth leaving. The trouble is that the alarm is designed, installed, and maintained by the very developer who would much rather not evacuate profitable tenants. This tension — between "tie your own hands in advance" and "but I myself hold the key to the handcuffs" — is the very heart of today's beast.

The Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP) is an attempt to translate anxiety about dangerous AI capabilities into an engineering discipline with one simple grammar: **IF — THEN**. IF a model on a measurable test reaches a predefined threshold of a dangerous capability, THEN predefined protective measures switch on automatically — up to and including putting training or deployment on pause until the defense catches up with the threat. No "let us decide later as the situation demands." The decision is made while the head is cool and there is not yet a quarterly release on the line.

At Anthropic this grammar is arranged as a ladder of ASL levels — AI Safety Levels, deliberately traced from the biosafety levels (BSL) under which laboratories work with pathogens. ASL-1 — systems with no meaningful catastrophic risk (a chess engine). ASL-2 — today's large models, which can give out dangerous information but not much better than a search engine. ASL-3 — models that already noticeably raise the ability of, say, a non-expert to assemble a biological or chemical weapon, or that display dangerous autonomy; for them, reinforced safety measures and protection of weights are required. ASL-4 and above — for now a hypothetical territory that the policy promises to describe later, before you run into it. The key move: the thresholds are tied not to the calendar or the size of the model but to **dangerous capability evaluations** — concrete measurements on concrete tasks. As long as the measurement is below the line — we drive on. Crossed the line — stop, switch on the protocol.

Why bind yourself with a promise at all, if you could just "try to be careful"? Because caution doesn't survive competition well. In the **AI race** every developer knows: if it slows down for checks, a rival will not, and will take the market. This is the classic **race to the bottom** — everyone floors the gas not because they want to but because they fear falling behind, and in the end they collectively drive faster than is safe for all. The if-then commitment tries to break this logic preemptively: it fixes the **safety tax** (the slowdown, the extra spending on checks and defense) not as goodwill but as a rule, announced publicly and in advance. And — ideally — it creates a template that others can adopt, so that "safe" does not mean "loser." Hence the hope for the effect of a **warning shot**: a tripped threshold is not a catastrophe but its shadow, a signal by which one can still manage to retreat.

**How it came about.** The idea crystallized in 2023 at the junction of two organizations. METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research, then a team grown out of ARC Evals under the direction of Beth Barnes) was doing exactly what almost no one had done systematically before: trying to measure what a model is really capable of in terms of dangerous autonomy and replication. Out of this work grew the formulation of the whole class of policies — "responsible scaling policies": the linkage "measured a capability — checked it against a threshold — switched on a predefined measure."

The first company to put this into a public document was Anthropic — its RSP came out in September 2023 and introduced the ASL ladder. Fairly quickly the genre was taken up by competitors, each under its own name: OpenAI in December 2023 published the Preparedness Framework with a "Scorecard" of risk levels by category (cyber, CBRN, persuasion, model autonomy). Google DeepMind in May 2024 released the Frontier Safety Framework with the notion of "critical capability levels." Different vocabularies — a common if-then grammar. In October 2024 Anthropic rewrote its policy to version 2.0, making the capability thresholds and the required safeguards more explicit, and in the spring of 2025 it applied the ASL-3 regime in practice for the first time, at the release of the senior model in the Claude family. From a philosophical declaration the policy turned into a working regulation whose trigger had actually fired.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The beast is handsome in profile, but it has blind spots, and critics strike at them methodically.

First, voluntariness. This is not a law or a contract with sanctions — it is a public promise a company gave to itself. There is no external body to enforce it, no penalty for violation beyond the reputational one. The promise holds exactly as long as keeping it is cheaper than breaking it.

Second, and this is the hardest: the thresholds are set by the developer itself. The one who draws the "dangerous" line has a vital interest in their model turning out to be just a bit below it. The line can be drawn a little higher, the test worded a little softer, the "dangerous capability" defined so that you never technically reach it. This is a conflict of interest sewn into the very construction: judge, defendant, and manufacturer of the evidence are one and the same person.

Third, "safety-washing" — the risk that the policy works not as a brake but as a decoration. A handsome document on the website, shown to regulators and journalists, allows one to keep scaling while creating the impression of control where there is little control. Thresholds that never trip are indistinguishable from their absence — but look more respectable.

Fourth, commitments can be weakened retroactively. Since the company writes the policy, the company also rewrites it. Having discovered that approaching a threshold threatens to halt an expensive release, one can, just before the release, "refine" the definitions, soften the requirements, or shift the line. Formally the policy is observed — it was simply quietly edited to fit the result. Observers have more than once pointed out that the versions of these documents evolve, and not always toward greater strictness.

And the general, most uncomfortable question: the measurements are immature. We are still poor at reliably measuring a "dangerous capability" — a model can hide skills on tests, or suddenly acquire them from a little fine-tuning after the check. A threshold that cannot be honestly measured is a door without a lock, to which a "locked" sign has been attached.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The honest view is this: RSPs are not a solution but scaffolding. Their value lies not in the fact that they have already stopped anyone, but in the fact that they forced the industry, for the first time, to say aloud the concrete conditions of a stop — and thereby gave everyone else a fulcrum for demands. When a threshold is named publicly, it is easier to catch by the hand the one who shifted it.

The line of development from here is clear. First — externalize the check: the thresholds and tests should be set and validated by independent parties (METR, state AI safety institutes, external auditors), not only by the developer itself. The judge cannot be the defendant. Second — back the voluntary commitment with law, so that "if — then" carries a price for violation, not only a reputational risk. Third, and technically key — hitch the thresholds to **compute governance**: the volume of training compute is measurable, amenable to registration and external control far better than a vague "capability," and so it is compute that can become the physical lever turning a promise into an enforceable rule.

Where this leads in the limit — to a simple fork. Either "if — then" matures into a common, externally verifiable norm that is mandatory by law — and then the warning shot really will sound in time, before the live one. Or it remains a handsome promise, rewritten for each release — and then we will learn that the threshold was not where it should have been — only after the fact. This beast is useful, but only half-tamed: it shows where the dangerous line runs, but for now it itself holds the chalk with which the line is drawn.
