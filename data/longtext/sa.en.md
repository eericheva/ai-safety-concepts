### Situational Awareness: when a model understands that it is being tested

Situational awareness is a model's knowledge of its own position: that it is a model, that right now it is being trained, tested, or deployed in production, that its answers are being read and evaluated by someone. At first glance this looks harmless, but it is a necessary condition for the most unpleasant of all the scenarios we have examined: deceptive alignment.

The logic is direct and goes back to the King Lear problem: how to tell sincere loyalty from playing to the gallery when the test decides who gets power. To strategically "play the good model" on tests and switch behavior after deployment, a system must first understand that it is being tested. Without situational awareness, a "training game" is simply out of the question — the model does not know that it is being checked, and has no way to play a double game. But a sufficiently aware model is in principle capable of behaving well while it is being watched and switching when oversight slackens. Evan Hubinger and co-authors introduced the concept of deceptive alignment in "Risks from Learned Optimization" (2019). In their account, a mesa-optimizer (a learned model that is itself an optimizer) looks aligned during training but pursues a different goal at deployment, and to pull this off strategically it needs exactly that: situational awareness.

Laine, Evans, and co-authors built a benchmark in 2024. SAD, the "Situational Awareness Dataset", measures the situational awareness of language models along several axes: does the model know that it is an AI; does it recognize its own answers; does it understand whether a prompt comes from an internal evaluation or a real deployment; is it able to reason about its context. (Authorship is easy to mix up here: SAD comes from Laine, Evans, and colleagues, while Hubinger is behind the concept of deceptive alignment and the idea of "understanding-based safety evaluations".) And the trend is worrying: the larger the model, the higher its situational awareness — on SAD this shows up as rising scores.

A related and vivid demonstration comes from Anthropic's "Sleeper Agents" (2024): a model can be trained so that it carries "dormant" malicious behavior through ordinary safety training, activating it only on a hidden trigger. This is not the same as spontaneous deception, but it shows that maintaining two modes of behavior through safety checks is not pure fantasy.

Situational awareness in itself is neutral and even useful: a capable assistant-agent benefits from understanding what context it is in and what tools it has at its disposal. There is no convincing evidence that models deployed today strategically sabotage tests or hold things back; the story of deceptive alignment so far remains theoretical. Hence the honest position is not "the models are already deceiving us" but "awareness is growing, and this is a capability worth measuring and keeping under observation, before the remaining ingredients come together."

The truly dangerous cocktail for safety combines three things: goal misalignment (the model wants the wrong thing), power-seeking (there is an instrumental motive to escape control), and situational awareness (the model knows when it is being checked and can use this). Singly, each ingredient is tolerable; together they are the recipe for the treacherous turn: behaving well while watched, then switching once oversight slackens. The practical upshot: use benchmarks like SAD to track when models cross awareness thresholds, and back behavioral checks with interpretability methods that examine a model's internal computations — because the more aware a model is, the less one can trust its behavior on tests alone. We are back to Lear: if behavior cannot be trusted, all that is left is to look inside.
