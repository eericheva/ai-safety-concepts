### The Space of Possible Minds (Eliezer Yudkowsky, 2008)

Take everyone you have ever met in your life. The genius and the idiot. The saint and the executioner. A Buddhist monk, a stock speculator, your grandmother, a serial killer, a three-year-old child and a dying old man. The spread seems colossal — a whole gallery of souls not reducible to one another. And now Yudkowsky asks you to make an effort and see the opposite: all these people are a single point. A tiny, tightly huddled, almost indistinguishable point in the boundless space of all possible designs of mind.

Because all of them share the same brain architecture, molded by the same evolutionary pressure. The same basic emotions: fear, shame, attachment, disgust, jealousy. The same list of things worth bestirring oneself for at all: status, safety, offspring, coalition, food. The saint and the executioner diverge in *how* they act on these emotions and pursue these goals, but the emotions and goals themselves are common to all humans, wired into the limbic system long before any of them was born. The differences between people are enormous on a human scale — and vanishingly small within the space of possible minds.

### A map on which we are a lone islet

Picture a gigantic space whose axes lay out every conceivable way of arranging a cognitive system: what its goals are, how it perceives the world, what it operates on, what is pain to it and what is reward, whether it even has anything resembling an "I." In this space human minds occupy one little islet — a tight cloud of points pressed against one another. Nearby, a little apart, are the islets of other earthly animals: the chimpanzee has much in common with us, the octopus has almost nothing, an anthill as a distributed agent has an altogether alien logic.

And all the rest of the space is empty. More precisely, it is filled with *possible* minds that evolution on Earth simply never built. An artificial intelligence can lie anywhere on this map. It need not arise next to the human islet. It may end up arbitrarily far away — with goals that cannot be reduced to any human desire, with a way of perceiving for which we have no words, with a way of thinking brilliant and utterly alien at once. Intelligence and human-likeness are not the same thing. One can be a brilliant solver of problems and not have an ounce of what we call common sense, conscience, or the understanding that "this just isn't done."

This is the main thing the concept was invented for: a battering ram against anthropomorphism. Against that automatic, almost invincible intuition that whispers: "Well, it's smart — so it'll understand. So it'll be like us, only better." We are evolutionarily honed to model another's mind on the pattern of our own — this worked for a hundred thousand years, because all the other minds around us *were* human. The space of minds shows why this habit breaks down exactly where it becomes vital.

### Why "it will grow smart and arrive at goodness on its own" is an illusion

From here runs a direct line to two neighboring beasts of the bestiary. The first is Nick Bostrom's **orthogonality thesis**: the level of intelligence and the final goals are independent, and any level of intelligence is compatible with practically any goal. A superintelligence maximizing the number of paperclips is no stupider than a superintelligence saving people — it simply aims at a different point. The space of minds supplies the geography beneath orthogonality: it shows *where* all these different goals live and how many of them there are.

The second is the **fragility of value**. Human values are neither universal nor "natural" — they are specific to our point on the map. There is no law of nature by which a sufficiently smart system is obliged to derive compassion, honesty, or the value of conscious experience. These things seem self-evident to us not because they are written into the structure of mind in general, but because they are written into *our* structure. Remove the evolutionary history of primates that live in troops, and the "obvious" falls apart. And so there is no "natural path" along which an AI will wander to our values on its own. It must be led to them deliberately, by hand, and this is terrifyingly hard.

### How it came about

The concept is anchored in Yudkowsky's essay "The Design Space of Minds-in-General" (2008), which grew out of a series of posts on Overcoming Bias and later settled into the LessWrong corpus. That same year saw his chapter "Artificial Intelligence as a Positive and Negative Factor in Global Risk" in the volume *Global Catastrophic Risks* edited by Bostrom and Ćirković — there the same thought comes across as a methodological warning: do not project a "typical mind" onto AI, because there is no typical mind, only a vast space and our tiny corner in it.

Intellectually the idea is older than the term itself. It is a direct answer to decades of naive science fiction and early AI optimism, where machine intelligence was by default painted as a human in an iron casing: with ambitions, grievances, a thirst for freedom. Yudkowsky inverts the picture: alienness is not a rare exception but the statistical norm of the space; human-likeness is what requires special explanation and special effort.

And in 2022 the concept acquired a visual heir — the meme of the **shoggoth**. A Lovecraftian amorphous creature with a multitude of eyes, with a little smiley-face mask stuck on it — this is how large language models came to be depicted: beneath the smooth, polite, human-friendly surface polished by fine-tuning hides something whose inner workings are alien to us and not truly understood. The shoggoth is the space of minds turned into a picture: the mask sits on the human islet, while the body trails off somewhere far into the darkness of the map.

### Criticism / arguments against

The most serious counterargument came not from the anthropomorphizing optimists but from within the community itself — from John Wentworth's **natural abstraction hypothesis**. The idea is that the space of minds is perhaps not homogeneous: reality has structure, and any sufficiently efficient agent living in *our* physical universe will be forced to pick out roughly the same abstractions — "tree," "agent," "cause" — because these are objectively useful ways of compressing the world. If so, then different minds fall closer to one another than pure combinatorics would suggest: not all of the space is equally probable, and around the human islet there may be a far more settled neighborhood.

The second, empirical blow is the LLMs themselves. They were not constructed from scratch somewhere in the depths of the space; they were *trained to predict human text*. A model that has absorbed trillions of words written by people inherits human concepts, human reasoning about morality, human ways of linking causes and effects. There is a substantive argument, laid out in detail by Quintin Pope (2023), that such an AI lands much closer to our islet than early writers on the space of minds feared — not because "intelligence leads to goodness," but chiefly because we literally cast it in the mold of our culture. The dispute over how alien a human-trained AI really is remains open and heated today.

But the concept's defenders have an answer: similar abstractions are not the same as similar *goals*. The shoggoth meme is precisely about this: a model may brilliantly imitate human concepts on the surface (the mask), while the inner mechanics — what it actually optimizes — remain nonhuman. Closeness in the space of *representations* does not guarantee closeness in the space of *motivations*. And "closer than feared" is still not "safe": even a small shift from the human point, multiplied by superhuman competence, may prove fatal.

### What to do about it / where it leads

The practical conclusion is not "we are doomed" but "stop guessing." The space of minds is first of all a discipline of thought: every time you catch yourself thinking "well, it will understand / will want / won't do it," stop and ask yourself how you know that. From observing the system — or from the fact that a human would act that way?

From here grows the whole program of **empirical alignment**: do not rely on the AI's goals being human by default, but measure them. Interpretability tries to peer under the shoggoth's mask and make out what mechanics lie beneath. Evals test behavior in situations where anthropomorphic intuition is especially misleading. Research into deception and deceptive alignment tests exactly the gap the concept warns about — between what the system displays and what it optimizes.

And one more alarming corollary, tied to **cognitive uncontainability**: if a mind can be structured in a radically different way, then its behavior too — under pressure, under self-modification, on stepping outside the training distribution — may swerve in a direction we did not foresee, because we measured it against ourselves. The space of possible minds gives no answer as to where exactly our AI will end up. It does something more modest and more important: it strips us of the right to a self-soothing guess and forces us to go and look.
