### Swampman (Donald Davidson, 1987)

Donald Davidson is standing by a dead tree in a swamp. Lightning strikes. His body instantly disintegrates into its elements — and the tree standing nearby, by the purest chance, turns into an exact copy of him, though built of entirely different molecules: the same brain mass, the same synapses, the same curve of the lips. Swampman climbs out of the mire, walks to Davidson's house, moves into it, recognizes — as it seems to him — his friends, sits down to write a paper on radical interpretation. Atom for atom, gesture for gesture, word for word he is indistinguishable from the man who died. And here is the thesis Davidson devised this scene for: Swampman has no thoughts. His "words" mean nothing. He remembers nothing and recognizes no one — because he has nothing to remember and no one to recognize. He has never seen these trees, never learned this language, never met these people.

Intuitively this is outrageous. A being physically identical to a thinking one behaves like a thinking one — and is suddenly declared empty inside. But Davidson is no mystic and no dualist; he does not say the copy lacks a soul. He says something more uncomfortable: the content of a thought — what the thought is about — does not lie entirely inside the head. It is constituted by a history of causal connections between the person and the world. A thought of a tree is a thought of a tree not because the right pattern lights up in the brain, but because that pattern arose and took hold in interactions with real trees. In the copy the internal states are the same, but there is no causal history: the light that first assembled these neurons came not from things but from a lightning discharge. To mean something is to stand in a certain relation to what you mean. Swampman does not stand in those relations. He is perfect form without a single gram of history, and therefore, for Davidson, without a gram of meaning.

This is **semantic externalism** carried to its limit: intentionality — the directedness of thought upon an object — is not an internal property but one stretched between head and world across time. Hence the provocation: two physically identical beings may differ in what they think about — and even in whether they think at all. "Narrow" content, the kind that fits inside the skull, is for Davidson insufficient; what is needed is "wide" content, rooted in a biography of contacts with referents.

**How it came about.** The thought experiment appears on the opening pages of Davidson's programmatic paper "Knowing One's Own Mind" — a presidential address to the Pacific Division of the American Philosophical Association, published in 1987. The paper's main theme is not fantastical: how to reconcile a person's privileged access to their own thoughts (I know what I think without observing myself) with the fact that the content of those thoughts is set by the external world and by society. Davidson slots into the wave of externalism raised by Hilary Putnam ("The Meaning of 'Meaning'," 1975) with his Twin Earth and his famous "meanings just ain't in the head," and by Tyler Burge with his social externalism. Swampman is a rhetorical blow in this context: if meaning were internal, the copy would think; since it does not think, according to Davidson, meaning is external. Tellingly, earlier still and independently, Ruth Millikan had arrived at the same conclusion in teleosemantics (*Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories*, 1984): a copy that arose by chance has no evolutionary and learning history to set the function of its states — and without a function there is no content. Swampman became a common focal point for a whole family of "historical" theories of meaning.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and most stubborn objection is intuition. To a great many philosophers the opposite seems obvious: a copy indistinguishable inside and out thinks exactly the same. Proponents of **internalism** (in various versions — from Jerry Fodor to defenders of "narrow content") insist that what is psychologically relevant is precisely what is in the head; the causal prehistory is metaphysical bookkeeping that changes neither behavior, nor phenomenology, nor the capacity to reason. If Swampman conducts a flawless dialogue about Kant, it is strange to deny that he is thinking about something.

The second objection is about time. Even granting externalism, one may ask: how much causal history is needed? Within a day Swampman has already entered into real causal connections — touched real trees, heard real speech. So his content is accruing, and the "emptiness" is a property of the first instant only? Then the dispute is not about principle but about the timescale of initialization, which drains most of the drama from it. Davidson partly concedes this: he speaks precisely of the copy's first thoughts, of the moment when nothing yet stands behind the words.

The third is the charge of sleight of hand. Critics note that Swampman exploits the fogginess of the very concept of "meaning": it elegantly shows that our everyday conception of content is overloaded — it crams together internal role, external reference, and history — but it does not prove which component is the chief one. The thought experiment reveals a discord of intuitions rather than resolving it. Finally, there is a purely scientific discomfort: externalism sits poorly with the idea that psychology should explain behavior through internal states — since the behavior of copy and original is explained identically.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Forty years on, Swampman has ceased to be armchair exotica — we have built an entire fleet of Swampmen. A large language model is, quite literally, trained on "swamp text": on a corpus where the word "tree" never once occurred in the presence of a tree. The model has no causal history of reference — only colossal statistics of form. And Davidson's question now looms as large as ever: do the model's words have meaning — or only a syntactic shadow of meaning, imitating it perfectly at the output?

Here Swampman joins hands with two neighboring beasts of the Bestiary. The first is Stevan Harnad's **symbol grounding problem** ("The Symbol Grounding Problem," 1990): how does a symbol acquire content if it is defined only through other symbols and not through a sensorimotor connection with the world? A dictionary in which every word is glossed by other words is a carousel with no exit; for meaning to arise, the circle must somewhere touch the ground. The second is the argument of Emily Bender and Alexander Koller ("Climbing towards NLU," 2020) with their thought-experiment octopus eavesdropping on a telegraph: a system trained only on form can flawlessly predict replies without ever connecting them to meaning — and will collapse where reference to a real, unseen world is required. This is Davidson retold in the language of NLP: without causal contact with referents — only form.

But Swampman gives alignment no convenient conclusion — it gives a blade that cuts both ways. On one hand, it counsels caution: do not ascribe understanding, memory, intentions to a model merely because its output is indistinguishable from a human's; "seems to recognize friends" is exactly what the copy does while recognizing nothing. On the other, it undercuts our own complacency too: if content is set by a history of causal connections, then a model's training is also a history of causal connections — mediated by text, to be sure, but still stretching toward the world through the people who wrote that text while looking at trees. Multimodality, learning through interaction, agency in a real environment — all this can be read as an attempt to give the model that very biography of contacts of which Swampman is deprived in the first second and not deprived on the second day.

And then the practical turn is this. The dispute over "form or meaning" is too binary to be useful; it is more productive to ask, as Davidson does, what history of connections the system commands and what that history suffices for. For model evaluation this means testing not the smoothness of form but behavior at the boundary of reference: there, where one must track a real object across time, update its state, be answerable for truth rather than for plausibility. Swampman is neither a death sentence for artificial mind nor an indulgence granted to it. It is a demand not to confuse a flawless imitation of meaning with the presence of meaning — and at the same time to ask ourselves honestly whether we are sure that we ourselves stand on this side of the lightning.
