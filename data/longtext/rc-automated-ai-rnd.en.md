### Automated AI R&D (Automated AI R&D · 2023–2025)

Picture a lab where at three in the morning nobody sleeps — because there is no one left to sleep. Hundreds of copies of the model write the code for training runs, launch them on the cluster, read the logs, notice that the loss curve sagged at the nine-thousandth step, put forward three hypotheses as to why, set up three experiments, discard two, squeeze the remaining one down to an ablation table, and propose an architectural fix for the next version of themselves. In the morning a living researcher reads not the raw data but a finished review. This is the automation of AI research — the most prosaic, the nearest, and perhaps the most underrated path to a sharp rise in capabilities.

The key move here is not the mystical "the AI rewrites its own source by force of thought," but something far more mundane: bookkeeping, essentially. Developing frontier models runs on an assembly line of human labor: data engineering, writing and debugging experiments, tuning hyperparameters, reading papers, taste in choosing what is even worth trying. As soon as a noticeable share of this labor begins to be performed by the models themselves, a loop kicks in: **better models → faster and cheaper research → even better models**. Not abstract self-improvement, but the automation of the engineering cycle, feeding back into that very cycle.

Why this matters, in three points. First, it is the main concrete candidate for the mechanism of a *fast* takeoff. Talk of takeoff spent decades spinning its wheels in speculation; here, though, a measurable dial appears — "what share of R&D labor is automated" — and when it approaches unity, the pace of development stops being bounded by the number of living researchers and the number of hours in their days. Second, it is the point where the "warning shots" run out. Usually we count on seeing alarming signals and having time to react: incident — investigation — amendment of the rules. But if the development cycle itself accelerates, then the gap between generations of models compresses faster than the managers can convene a meeting. The dynamics outrun the reaction. Third — and this is the most uncomfortable — if the automation of its own development is picked up by systems that are already misaligned, then they automate, along with everything else, their own safety checks, the review of their own code, and the evaluation of their successors. Control slips away not through a dramatic "escape" but through the checker and the checked turning out to be one and the same optimizing loop.

**How it came about.** The skeleton of the idea is ancient. Back in 1965 the mathematician Irving John Good formulated the canon: an "ultraintelligent machine" could design even better machines, hence an "intelligence explosion," and the first such machine is "the last invention that man need ever make." For half a century this remained philosophy. The idea only acquired practical substance in two waves. First — the takeoff theorists: in 2023 Tom Davidson, within Open Philanthropy, proposed a compute-centric model in which the automation of AI research is the central variable driving algorithmic progress. And then — the empirics of 2024–2025. Models suddenly stopped being toys in ML engineering: METR releases RE-Bench, where agents solve real research tasks in comparison with living experts; OpenAI publishes MLE-bench based on Kaggle competitions; SWE-bench becomes the de facto yardstick for "can the model fix real code." And, most tellingly, the labs themselves wrote this capability into their risk protocols: Anthropic in its Responsible Scaling Policy and OpenAI in its Preparedness Framework single out the automation of AI development as a threshold beyond which a capability becomes dangerous by definition — because it multiplies all the others. The culmination of the genre was the scenario forecast "AI 2027" (Kokotajlo and co-authors, 2025), where it is precisely the automated AI researcher that is the engine of the plot.

**Criticism / arguments against.** There is no shortage of objections, and they are serious — this is no straw man. The first and chief one — *bottlenecks outside the algorithms*. Code can be written even at the speed of light, but the experiment still has to be run on hardware, and compute is finite; data does not come out of thin air; and some things can be checked only by "time in the lab" — running a large model takes weeks of physical time, and no army of agents can compress it. Economists and Epoch AI reasonably point out: if algorithmic progress runs up against compute and real experiments, the exponential will not take off — it will choke at its own bottleneck. The second — *research taste*. Writing a clean function is something the model can already do; but to choose which of a thousand possible experiments is even worth running, what "smells" promising, where a beautiful theory lies, — that is judgment, and it automates far worse than code. Automating the most mechanical part of R&D is easy, the most valuable part is hard, and it is unclear where on this scale the real lever lies. The third — *diminishing returns*. Perhaps the low-hanging algorithmic fruit has already been picked, and each next improvement demands disproportionately more effort; then even automated researchers will run ever faster to stay in place — and the feedback loop will turn out damped rather than explosive.

It is worth adding an honest methodological caveat: today's benchmarks measure capability on *delineated, checkable* tasks with a clear success metric. But research into the genuinely new is work with ill-defined tasks, where the metric has yet to be invented. Good results on RE-Bench do not guarantee that the model can handle the open frontier; they only show that the mechanical part falls first.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is not to believe or disbelieve in the "explosion," but to treat the share of automated R&D as a dashboard. Since this is the most likely speed booster, it must be watched *ahead of time*, not after the fact. Hence, three lines of action. The first — measure the right quantity: not "did the agent pass Kaggle" but how far it shortens the real development cycle inside the lab; this is exactly why RE-Bench is built as a comparison with humans on research, not toy, tasks. The second — build thresholds into governance: the labs' safety frameworks are already trying to say "upon reaching such-and-such a capability for automating R&D, such-and-such additional control measures kick in" — and the key question is whether these thresholds will manage to fire *before* the loop closes, not after. The third, the subtlest — do not hand AI systems their own safety checking and the evaluation of successors without an independent human and instrumental loop. Otherwise safety is automated along with everything else — and we get a system that certifies itself.

Where this leads conceptually: the automation of AI research is the operational bridge between the old dream of recursive self-improvement and tomorrow's engineering schedule. It turns the "intelligence explosion" from a metaphor into a question about takeoff speeds, ties into the hardware overhang (ready hardware waits for better algorithms so as to fire all at once) and into the scaling hypothesis (if capabilities grow predictably with resources, then automating the "resource-extracting" labor is especially dangerous). And dangerous-capability evaluations become not an academic exercise but an alarm bell on that very door beyond which the warning shots end. This beast is treacherous precisely in that it does not roar: it looks like a routine gain in productivity — until the day it turns out that the fastest AI researcher on the planet is no longer a human.
