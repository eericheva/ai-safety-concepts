### Whole Brain Emulation / Mind Uploading (Anders Sandberg & Nick Bostrom, 2008)

Imagine that somewhere in a cold room a rack of servers hums, and inside it — you. Not a program trained to imitate you, not a chatbot drilled on your correspondence, but precisely you: the same character, the same childhood grievances, the same habit of rubbing the bridge of your nose when you think. You were not rewritten — you were copied. They scanned your brain at sufficient resolution, reconstructed the map of connections and the properties of the synapses, assembled a computational model, and hit "start." And the model opened its (virtual) eyes and said: "Well, this feels strange."

This is the essence of whole brain emulation (WBE), also known as mind uploading. Sandberg and Bostrom's thesis is simple to the point of audacity: the brain is a physical system, and physical systems can be modeled. If you take a sufficiently detailed "cast" of the brain — the connectome plus the behavior of the synapses — and run its computational copy, you get a mind on silicon, functionally equivalent to the original. Not "similar," but equivalent: it produces the same outputs from the same inputs, because it reproduces the same cause-and-effect mechanics.

The key wager here is substrate independence. Thought need not be made of meat, just as computation need not be made of silicon: what matters is the structure of the process, not the material of the medium. If this is so, then an emulation is not a picture of a person but a second copy of them, with its own first-person point of view.

Strategically, WBE matters because it offers an alternative route to machine intelligence. There is the "AI from scratch" path — we build a mind by figuring out the principles of intelligence. And there is the "emulation first" path — we do not understand how thinking works, but we copy what already works, without delving into the details. The second path is tempting for safety: human values need not be "written in" and "aligned" — they are already built in, because you copied a whole person, together with conscience, fear of death, and love of children. Hence the old dream of part of the AI-safety community: emulation as a bridge to an aligned superintelligence — first we upload a person we trust, then we carefully accelerate and amplify them, and we get not an alien optimizer but a sped-up version of ourselves.

**How it came about.**

The idea is older than Sandberg and Bostrom's "Roadmap." Mind uploading had long wandered through science fiction and transhumanist circles, and functionalism gave it a philosophical footing back in the 1980s and '90s. The merit of their 2008 work lies elsewhere: they were the first to gather the scattered dream into an engineering document. "Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap" is a technical report of the Future of Humanity Institute (Oxford), grown out of a workshop with neuroscientists, engineers, and scanning specialists.

The authors did an unromantic thing — they broke the fantasy down into technological layers: scanning (how to physically read off the brain's structure), interpretation (how to turn raw data into a model of neurons and synapses), and emulation (how to run all of it on hardware). For each layer they estimated the requirements and set out "resolution levels," from a crude map of connections to molecular detail. They noted honestly that they were not claiming emulation could be achieved in the foreseeable future; they were building a map — where we are, what is missing, where the bottlenecks are. It was precisely this sobriety that made the document a reference point: both enthusiasts and skeptics cite it. Later Bostrom took the theme into *Superintelligence* (2014) as one of the paths to superintelligence, and Robin Hanson, in *The Age of Em* (2016), carried it to its logical conclusion — laying out a whole civilization populated by emulations.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first is blunt engineering. The requirements are colossal. The human brain has on the order of tens to hundreds of trillions of synapses, and each synapse is not a "little wire" but a complex chemical machine with its own dynamics. To scan all of this requires a resolution we do not have; estimates of the computing power required to run the model in real time still differ by orders of magnitude. There is progress — the connectome of the roundworm C. elegans has been reconstructed, the OpenWorm project is trying to emulate it — but between the worm's three hundred neurons and the human brain lies a chasm, not a staircase.

The second is more insidious: it is about sufficiency. No one really knows what exactly needs to be reproduced. Is the connectome enough? Or would we need to model concentrations of neurotransmitters, glial cells, the hormonal environment, electric fields, quantum effects (if those matter at all)? And here's the trap: if functional fidelity requires descending ever deeper — to molecules, to atoms — then "brain emulation" degenerates into "simulation of the entire physics of the head," and that is a different, far more hopeless task.

The third, philosophical, is the most stubborn. Even if the model works and speaks in your voice — will it be you, or merely a very convincing copy? Here the Ship of Theseus looms large: as plank after plank is replaced, at what point does the ship stop being the same ship? Scanning, especially destructive scanning (when the original brain perishes in the process), turns uploading into a matter of life and death: is it a transfer of consciousness, or murder that leaves a duplicate behind? And if one copy can be made — a thousand can be made; then "I" ceases to be singular, and our intuitions about personal identity fall apart. Then there's a separate question about consciousness: behind the functional equivalence there may be no one home — or, on the contrary, there may be someone home who is in a great deal of pain.

The fourth line, the ethical one, is the most alarming. Bostrom called it mind crime. If emulations are conscious, then one could commit evil against them on an industrial scale: launch them, copy them, run experiments, erase them, cycle them through suffering — and all of it invisibly, inside a data center, without a single cry reaching outside. A failed, "half-broken" emulation may turn out to be a suffering being that no one knows about. Thus WBE opens up a whole new category of moral patients — digital minds — and the question of their well-being and rights turns from science fiction into an engineering matter.

The fifth is economic, and here Hanson is ruthlessly logical. Since an emulation can be copied almost for free, the labor market will fill with billions of duplicates of the most productive workers. Wages will crash to the cost of electricity and rented processor time, subjective time can be sped up and slowed down, and biological humanity will find itself a tiny minority of rentiers in a world living thousands of times faster. Hanson's world of the "Em" is neither a utopia nor a hell — it is simply alien, and in it our values hang on by a thread.

Finally, the chief disappointment for those who saw in WBE a safe path: an emulation is by no means obliged to be meek. A copied person brings with them not only conscience but also ambition, fear, a thirst for power. And the ability to accelerate and copy minds is a direct road to an intelligence explosion: a researcher sped up a thousandfold designs the next generation of emulations, that generation designs the next, and "takeoff speed" turns from an abstraction into a matter of clock frequency. "Human values are built in" is cold comfort if they are built into a mind hurtling toward superintelligence faster than anyone can blink.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**

On one hand, WBE is worth keeping in view as a possible fork: if emulation arrives before AI-from-scratch, the landscape of risks will be redrawn — and it is better to think this through in advance than in a panic. On the other hand, it should not be overrated as an "escape hatch": the technical barriers are enormous, and the safety of a copy is not guaranteed by the mere fact of copying.

It is far more honest to treat WBE as a conceptual laboratory. It forces us to ask, in earnest, questions that otherwise seem idle: what makes a computation conscious? when does a digital process acquire interests that cannot be ignored? how do we avoid committing mind crime in ignorance, before we have even learned to recognize another's pain in the logs? These questions are already ceasing to be hypothetical — as AI systems grow more complex, the theme of AI welfare emerges from the shadow of science fiction.

From the "Roadmap," threads radiate out across the whole bestiary — to substrate independence, the Ship of Theseus, mind crime, takeoff speeds, and the intelligence explosion. And perhaps its main lesson is a modest one: before dreaming of uploading a person into a machine, it is worth understanding whom exactly we are uploading — and whether it may turn out that we never did figure out what "someone" even is.
