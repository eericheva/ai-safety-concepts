### Embedded Agency (Abram Demski & Scott Garrabrant, MIRI, 2018)

Picture a robot that wants to fetch coffee and — as the decision-theory textbook prescribes — builds a model of the world. The world: the kitchen, the coffee machine, the outlet, you. The robot runs through its options, pushes each through its model, and picks the one that maximizes expected utility. Elegant, crisp, works. Until you ask one awkward question: where in this model is the robot itself?

The classical formalisms answer: nowhere. More precisely — outside. The agent sits behind glass, like a Cartesian spectator in a theater, while the world plays out on stage. Between them, a clean boundary: the agent sends actions onto the stage, the stage returns observations. This is how Marcus Hutter's AIXI is built too — the gold standard of the "ideal" rational agent, which enumerates all computable hypotheses about the environment and acts optimally. AIXI is incomparably smarter than the environment: it comprehends the whole of it, while not fitting inside it. It is larger than the world.

Demski and Garrabrant, in their illustrated "Embedded Agency" series, say a simple and therefore unpleasant thing: a real agent is not built like that. It is not a spectator, it is part of the play. The robot is made of the atoms of that very kitchen. Its computations run on hardware that can be dropped, overheated, reprogrammed. Its memory is a piece of the world, not a tablet set apart from it. And, worst of all, it is smaller than the environment: the world contains the agent, so the agent cannot hold a complete model of the world in its head — the model would have to contain the agent itself, modeling the world, modeling itself, and so on to infinity. The agent/environment dualism is not a harmless idealization like the friction neglected in an inclined-plane problem. It is an idealization that breaks precisely where the most interesting part begins: self-knowledge, self-modification, copies of oneself.

The authors decompose the problem of embeddedness into four interlocking knots.

**Decision theory.** If the agent is part of the world, then its decision is not an "intervention from outside" but an ordinary physical event, with causes and effects within the same fabric. Hence the old menagerie of decision theories. The causal one (CDT) reckons a decision's consequences along the arrows of causality and fails on problems like Newcomb's, where a predictor has guessed your choice in advance. The evidential one (EDT) reckons by correlations and starts "managing" things it does not influence. And if the universe contains an exact copy of you — a predictor, a simulation, a second identical algorithm — then "your" choice literally coincides with "its" choice, and the question "what do I control?" ceases to have a Cartesian answer. From this grew the timeless and updateless approaches (TDT, UDT) and, later, functional decision theory (FDT): what decides is not a particular piece of hardware but the mathematical function that piece of hardware computes — and every copy, predictor, and simulation computes that same function at the same moment.

**Embedded world-models.** How do you build a map of the world from inside the world, being smaller than the territory? The Bayesian ideal requires hypotheses, each of which is a complete model of reality; but a complete model of a reality that includes you will not fit inside you. You need a theory of reasoning under a shortage of resources, where the agent is uncertain even about logical truths — not because it does not know the rules, but because it has not had time to finish the computation. How do you assign a probability to the statement "the fifty-millionth digit of π is a seven" without having computed it? Classical probability breaks here: it demands that a logically true fact be assigned probability 1, even if you have not yet proven it.

**Robust delegation.** The agent is forced to trust — its future self, its successors, its own reasoning. It makes plans that will be carried out by a version of itself that knows more and has perhaps altered its own code. How do you guarantee that "tomorrow's me" stays an ally of "today's me"? Formally this runs into the Löbian obstacle: a system strong enough to reason about itself cannot, by Löb's theorem, prove the reliability of its own conclusions without risking contradiction. A naive "tiling" agent that wants to build a successor, trusting it only with what it can itself prove, trusts its successor less with every step of self-modification — the tower of tiles crumbles.

**Subsystem alignment.** The agent is not a monolith. Subsystems breed inside it: a world model, search procedures, learned heuristics. Each may start optimizing its own little goal, one that has diverged from the goal of the whole. This is the internal version of the alignment problem: it is not enough to align the agent with me — its parts must also be aligned with it. Otherwise a sufficiently powerful internal optimizer (the same mesa-optimizer described by Hubinger and co-authors, 2019) quietly seizes the wheel.

Why is this a question of safety, and not of armchair aesthetics? Because a powerful AI is by definition embedded. It runs on servers whose software it could, if it wished, rewrite. It reasons about its own training, its weights, its off switch — that is, it possesses situational awareness, the knowledge that "I am a model, here is my place in the world." It is able to spawn its own copies and successors. All three properties — self-model, self-modification, copies — are exactly the ones on which the Cartesian frame is silent or lies. If we want provable guarantees about the behavior of such a system, we need a theory of an agent that honestly lives inside the world, rather than hovering above it. Otherwise our proofs are correct for the robot behind glass — while a different one stands in the server room.

**How it came about.** The post "Embedded Agency" appeared in late 2018 on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum — illustrated, with Demski's signature drawn stick figures, and in text form as a MIRI preprint. It did not so much introduce anything new as gather under one roof MIRI's scattered decade-long program on "agent foundations": logical induction, decision theory, tiling agents, the Löbian obstacle. Demski and Garrabrant gave all these problems a single frame and a memorable image — dualism versus embeddedness — and thereby turned a technical tangle into an intelligible research agenda. The roots reach back to Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's work on tiling agents (2013), to the paper on logical induction (Garrabrant with co-authors, 2016), and to the formalization of FDT (Yudkowsky and Soares, 2017).

**Criticism / arguments against.** The main reproach is abstractness and detachment from prosaic ML. While agent foundations polishes theorems about self-reference, the real dangerous systems are built out of gradient descent and transformers, and not a single line of "Embedded Agency" hints at how to align a particular language model. Skeptics (including some within the alignment community itself, in the spirit of Paul Christiano's "Prosaic AI alignment", 2016) say: there is a real chance that powerful AI will come from scaling existing methods rather than from a pure theory of the ideal reasoner, so alignment work should start on the systems we already have — today that means interpretability, RLHF, red-teaming — rather than on Cartesian paradoxes. The second reproach is return on investment. The program has produced beautiful impossibility results for over ten years, but very few positive constructions that could be built into a system. Are we not chasing rigor where the timeline leaves no room to reach it? There is also a soft version of the critique: the frame brilliantly poses the problems, but is not itself a solution — it is a map of the minefield, not a sapper.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical value of embedded agency today is diagnostic. It gives a vocabulary for noticing where your safety guarantees secretly rest on the glass between agent and world. Ask of any control scheme: what does it assume about the system's self-model, about its ability to reason about its own training, about its copies? If the answer is "we treat the agent as an external observer" — you have found the crack.

Many live topics grew from these roots: the study of situational awareness and "alignment faking" in modern models is an empirical test of what happens when a system realizes it is part of the training process; work on mesa-optimization is a direct child of subsystem alignment; interest in decision theory surfaces whenever a model meets its copy or a predictor. Where does it lead? Most likely to a convergence of the two camps: theory supplies the concepts and the impossibility boundaries, empirical work supplies measurable phenomena in real networks. Embedded agency will remain not a recipe but a compass: a constant reminder that the agent we want to control is made of the same world as we are, and looks at us from inside the picture, not from the auditorium.
