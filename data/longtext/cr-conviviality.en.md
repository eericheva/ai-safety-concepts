### Conviviality (Ivan Illich, 1973)

A bicycle does not know who is riding it. A child, a courier, a minister, an old man — the machine is equally obedient to the foot; it can be repaired with a wrench on the roadside, taken apart in the yard, handed to a neighbor. It adds to the human leg exactly as much as a person is willing to put into it, and dictates nothing about where they should go. The automobile works differently. It demands roads, filling stations, insurance, licenses, factories, and a whole civilization devoted to servicing it; it drives the pedestrian off the street and eventually makes it impossible to get to work without one — so a convenience turns into an obligation. Ivan Illich took this pair — the bicycle and the automobile — and turned it into a watershed running through the whole of technology. On one side lie **convivial** tools, on the other **manipulative** ones. And the question is not whether a tool is good in itself, but who serves whom.

The word "conviviality" goes back to the Latin *convivium* — a shared meal, a feast, communal living. A convivial tool is one that a person holds in their own hands, not the other way around: it expands autonomy, encourages creativity, and binds people to one another, instead of locking each one into a single vendor. The library, the telephone, the hand tool, the mail, the bicycle — here is Illich's bestiary of good things. Their common feature is accessibility without a specialist middleman, and control from below. The manipulative tool, meanwhile — the school that appropriates a monopoly on learning; the medicine that produces illnesses faster than it cures them; the high-speed transport that takes more time from society than it saves — is built to dictate a person's needs rather than satisfy those already present.

The mechanism rests on **two thresholds**. Every tool growing in scale passes a first threshold, beyond which it brings clear benefit: vaccinations save people from epidemics, roads connect villages. But if the growth is not stopped, the tool crosses a **second threshold** — and becomes counterproductive. It begins to generate the very need it promised to slake: the automobile creates distances that would be insurmountable without a car; the school convinces people that one can learn only under a teacher's supervision. Illich calls this a **radical monopoly** — not the monopoly of one firm over a market, but the monopoly of one *way* over the very possibility of doing otherwise. When a city is built up for cars, walking becomes not a choice but a sign of poverty. The radical monopoly takes away not a commodity but autonomy: it makes unsanctioned independent action physically impossible. The task, according to Illich, is to keep technology below the second threshold through political control, at a scale where it still serves people.

**How it came about.** Ivan Illich (1926–2002) is a figure hard to pigeonhole. An Austrian of Viennese origin, a Catholic priest, and a polyglot, Illich founded a research center in the Mexican city of Cuernavaca in 1961; by the mid-1960s that center had grown into CIDOC, which for a decade was a crossroads of radical Latin American thought. From there came his famous tetralogy of indictments against the "services" of the industrial world: "Deschooling Society" (1971) — against the educational monopoly; "Tools for Conviviality" (1973) — against the industrial tool as such; "Energy and Equity" (1974), where the bicycle is glorified as the most egalitarian means of transport; and "Medical Nemesis" (1975) with its notion of iatrogenesis — illness generated by medicine itself. "Tools for Conviviality" is the methodological center of this corpus: it is here that the particular indictments (school, transport, medicine) are generalized into a single theory of the threshold and the monopoly. Illich wrote in the shadow of an older critic of technology — the Frenchman Jacques Ellul with his "autonomous technology" (*la technique*), which spreads by its own logic — and, deeper, of postwar cybernetic anxiety itself. The book appeared in 1973, a few months before the oil embargo, when the very thought of the limits to growth first began to sound not eccentric but urgent.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

First, a **romantic bias**. The pair "bicycle versus automobile" is beautiful but suspiciously convenient. Too much of what lets people live long and eat well lies on the "bad" side of the threshold: anesthesia, clean water on the scale of a megacity, vaccines, power grids — all of these are industrial, capital-intensive, specialist-dependent systems that no autonomous craftsman will assemble in their yard. One can object that a consistent Illichism would have to be paid for with a return to a shorter and more painful life.

Second, the **threshold is hard to measure**. Where exactly does the "second watershed" run? Illich offers a vivid image, but no yardstick. In practice, the decision that a tool has now "outgrown" its benefit turns out to be political and value-laden — and thus contestable. It easily becomes a weapon for any side: both the Luddite and the techno-utopian will find in Illich something to quote.

Third, a **genetic and teleological naivety**. The thesis that a tool has an inner tendency toward enslavement sometimes slides into technological determinism in reverse — as if a thing's fate were inscribed in itself rather than in the institutions, ownership, and distribution of power around it. Marxist critics objected: it is not the "tool" that enslaves but the relations into which it is built; the same automobile in another configuration of ownership would behave differently.

And yet the core of these objections does not cancel Illich out. Even if Illich overrated craft and underrated the plumbing, his main question survived: **which tool has a built-in tendency to create dependence and erase the alternative — and who pays for that tendency to go unnoticed?**

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For the Bestiary, Illich is the missing humanities link between the old critique of technology and today's conversation about AI. His "second threshold" is a close cousin of Ellul's **autonomous technology**: in both, the tool at some point ceases to be a means and becomes an environment dictating its own conditions. His anxiety about the human reduced to an appendage of the system rhymes almost word for word with **Norbert Wiener**'s "human use of human beings": Wiener, as early as 1950, warned that cybernetics could either extend people or turn them into feedback cogs. And his diagnosis of the radical monopoly is an early, pre-digital formula for what Evgeny Morozov would later call **technological solutionism**: the habit of seeing in every human situation a bug waiting to be patched, and thereby redefining the very problem to fit the solution the industry knows how to sell.

Applied to artificial intelligence, Illich's frame poses a single test: **does this system expand human autonomy — or substitute for it?** Convivial AI is AI that remains a tool in the hand: it helps you think but leaves judgment to the human; it is open to inspection and readjustment; it does not lock the user into a single vendor. Manipulative AI crosses the second threshold when it becomes an indispensable intermediary between a person and their own capacities — when unlearning how to write, count, remember, and decide for oneself turns out to be not a choice but a new background condition, like the impossibility of walking in a city built for cars. The radical monopoly of the twenty-first century looks not like an evil superintelligence but like something mundane: like the moment beyond which refusing the assistant becomes professional unfitness, and an alternative, unmoderated way to know and do becomes technically unavailable.

Illich's practical conclusion is not Luddite but political, which is exactly what gives it value next to the more fatalistic neighbors on the Bestiary's map. The threshold, he insists, is a matter of conscious, collective, legal choice, not of fate. Therefore the question about AI is not "stop or accelerate" but something more concrete and sober: which properties make a tool controllable from below (openness, the right to repair and to refuse, the absence of monopoly, reversibility), and how to keep the technology at a scale where it still amplifies people — before they themselves become its continuation.
