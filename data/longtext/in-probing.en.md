### Probing / linear probes (Alain & Bengio, 2016)

Take a trained neural network, freeze its weights solid, and never touch a single parameter again. Pass a sentence through it and at some middle layer intercept the activation vector — several thousand numbers, the model's internal state at this moment. And now pose a naive question: can you guess from this vector whether the word the model is now processing is a noun? Or whether the statement it is about to utter is true?

To answer, you train a tiny thing on top of these activations — usually a linear classifier, a logistic regression, a single layer without any frills. This technique is called **probing**, and the little classifier itself is the **probe**. The big model itself stays frozen: only the probe learns, and it learns exactly one thing — to read the property of interest out of the innards. If a little probe can read out the property, the conclusion goes like this: **then the model represents this property** — it holds the part of speech, the syntactic role, the sentiment, or the truth in an explicit, linearly accessible form somewhere in its activations. If even a powerful probe cannot read out the property, the property is probably not there, or it is encoded in a way that cannot be pulled out linearly.

The probe's simplicity here is not laziness but method. We do not want the classifier to *compute* the part of speech from raw numbers itself; we want it merely to *read off* what is already there. So the probe is deliberately weak. Probing is the litmus test of the linear representation hypothesis: the idea that meaningful features lie along straight-line directions in activation space and can be separated by a hyperplane. If a linear probe works, the hypothesis is confirmed for this property at this layer.

**How it came about.** The term "probe" in this sense was cemented by Guillaume Alain and Yoshua Bengio in the 2016 work "Understanding intermediate layers using linear classifier probes." They stuck linear probes into different layers of convolutional networks to see how the linear separability of classes builds up from layer to layer — in essence measuring how far the representation has "matured" at each depth. The idea proved contagious for NLP. When contextual embeddings arrived — ELMo, then BERT — probes became the main tool for answering the question "what has been learned in there." Alexis Conneau and co-authors (2018, "What you can cram into a single $&!#* vector") assembled a battery of linguistic probes for sentence embeddings. John Hewitt and Christopher Manning (2019) used a structural probe to show that a whole syntax tree is linearly encoded in BERT's activations — the distances between words in the tree are recovered from the geometry of the representations. By the early 2020s probing had become a standard genre, and Yonatan Belinkov took methodological stock of it in the survey "Probing Classifiers: Promises, Shortcomings, and Advances" (2022).

For alignment, the interesting turn came when probes were aimed not at grammar but at **truth and honesty**. Amos Azaria and Tom Mitchell, in a 2023 work with the telling title "The Internal State of an LLM Knows When It's Lying," trained a simple classifier on a language model's hidden activations to distinguish true statements produced by the model from false ones — and on a test set that was half true and half false it reached an average accuracy of 71% to 83%, depending on the model, against 50% for chance. The thought is heady: the model may put out an untruth in words, yet its internal state "knows" it is an untruth — and a probe catches this desynchronization. Samuel Marks and Max Tegmark ("The Geometry of Truth," 2023) went further and showed that for datasets of true/false facts a stable linear structure arises — almost a separate "axis of truth" in activation space. Hence the safety researchers' dream: a lie detector that reads deception not from behavior but from the innards — because a sufficiently smart model can learn to say exactly what we want to hear, but rewriting its own activations is far harder for it.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

First and foremost — **correlation is not causation**. That a probe reads a property out of the activations does not mean the model *uses* this property in producing its answer. The feature may lie in the representations as a byproduct, a "trace" that plays no further role in the computation. The probe confirms the presence of the information but is silent about its functional role. Hence the shift toward causal, interventional checks: not merely to read a direction but to intervene in it and see whether behavior changes. Yanai Elazar and co-authors proposed "amnesic probing" (2021): remove from the activations exactly the information the probe reads, and check whether the model's performance on the task drops. If we erased information about the part of speech and the next-word prediction did not suffer — then the model was not actually relying on this information, however much the probe found it. Activation steering works in the same spirit: the direction found by the probe is added to the activations and we watch whether behavior moves where we expect. If steering works, the direction is causal; if it does not, you were reading a ghost.

Second — **the probe may turn out too powerful**. The classic work by John Hewitt and Percy Liang, "Designing and Interpreting Probes with Control Tasks" (2019), posed an unpleasant question: has the probe itself learned the task? Given enough capacity, a classifier will extract the part of speech even from random, untrained embeddings — simply by memorizing the correspondences. Then the probe's high accuracy speaks of the probe's abilities, not the model's contents. In response they proposed *control tasks* and a *selectivity* metric: the probe is trained also on a meaningless task with random labels, and a good probe is one that solves the real task well and the fictitious one poorly. The gap between the probe's accuracy on the two tasks measures whether the information lay in the representation or was conjured up by the probe.

Third, technical but important — **overfitting of the probe and leakage in the data**. A probe easily latches onto surface correlations: sentence length, token frequency, artifacts of a particular dataset of "true/false" facts. A "lie detector" risks turning out to be a detector of topic or style — it distinguishes not lying as such but whatever happened to coincide with lying in your training sample. So generalization is shaky: an honesty probe trained on arithmetic may fall apart on statements about politics. And a subtle conceptual snag: "the model knows it is lying" anthropomorphizes a linear classifier. The careful formulation is more modest — in the activations there is a direction statistically correlated with the truth label in this distribution.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is not to abandon probing but to wrap it in safeguards. Always attach a control task and look at selectivity, not at bare accuracy. Do not stop at reading — check causality by intervention: erase (amnesic), add (steering), patch activations between runs. Treat the linear probe's result as a hypothesis, not a verdict, and deliberately try to break it with a distribution shift: if the honesty direction survives the move from one topic to another, it can be trusted a little more.

Where this leads strategically — to ELK, the task of Eliciting Latent Knowledge. Honesty probes are an early, naive assault on the same mountain: how to extract from a model what it "really thinks," bypassing what it says. Collin Burns and co-authors ("Discovering Latent Knowledge in Language Models Without Supervision," 2022) made a beautiful move — they searched for a truth direction without any labels at all, relying on logical consistency (a statement and its negation cannot both be true). This removes part of the leakage-through-labels problem, but it does not remove the chief alignment anxiety: against a *deceptively aligned* model that purposefully hides its goals, a naive probe is powerless by construction — a sufficiently capable optimizer can learn to keep "clean" activations just as it keeps clean speech.

Probing gives us a flashlight to peer inside the black box — and this is a huge step forward compared with reading the outputs alone. But the flashlight shines where we aim it, and shows correlations, not intentions. That is why the current front of interpretability is not "does the property read out" but "does the model use it and will the reading hold under pressure." The probe answers whether the property can be read out almost for free. Establishing whether the model uses it and whether the reading holds under pressure requires interventions to test causality, distribution shifts to test robustness, and an eternal suspicion of one's own instrument.
