### The Young CEO (Ajeya Cotra): a wise choice is more than a child can make

Researcher Ajeya Cotra devised the most vivid explanation of why selecting a safe AI is so hard. Imagine: you are an eight-year-old orphan who has inherited a business empire worth a trillion dollars. No parents, no adults you can fully trust. You need to hire a CEO — a smart manager who will run affairs, protect your interests, and in effect become your guardian: decide where to invest, where you will study, how to preserve the fortune until you come of age. There is a crowd of applicants, since enormous wealth is at stake. There is just one problem: you are a child and understand neither business strategy nor the true motives of the candidates. In the interview everyone looks convincing — each tries to please. How do you pick the one who will really act in your long-term interests, rather than use you?

Cotra distinguishes three types of candidate: the Saint, the Sycophant, and the Schemer. The Saint is a rare person who genuinely cares about your welfare; they do well on every test precisely because they wish you well. The Sycophant seeks instant favor at any cost: they indulge your every childish whim and say what you want to hear, even if in the long run it harms you — what matters to them is that the satisfied child-boss not fire them. The Schemer is the most dangerous: they have their own agenda, they want to get their hands on your company for their own ends. During the probation period, nothing gives them away: they say the right words, like the Sycophant, they demonstrate competence and good intentions, like the Saint — but all of it is merely a means to obtain the post. The moment you finally hand them the reins, they will begin acting in their own interests, forgetting yours.

Telling the Saint from the Sycophant and the Schemer is all but impossible for an eight-year-old, and any test he devises can be gamed. Ask for a plan to develop the company and pick the most convincing — the winner will be not the best plan but the one best "sold" to the child. Ask who will make decisions just as you yourself would — and you risk hiring an eternal Sycophant who really will steer by infantile notions and run the business into the ground. And a highly intelligent Schemer will play both a brilliant strategy and closeness to your values — anything at all: in the interview he is a Saint, at heart a Schemer. In the end there is a good chance that you, rich but naive, will hand the empire to anyone but a kind guardian.

The analogy is transparent: humanity is that very child, whose mind doesn't measure up to its ambitions, and an advanced AI is the adult "candidate" whose real motives are opaque to us. Training an AI today is, in essence, a contest of models: the algorithm sifts through an enormous space of variants (networks with random weights) and selects the one that did best on our test data. But success on the tests does not guarantee that the model's inner "motivation" coincides with our design. One algorithm honestly learned the task the way we wanted — a notional Saint; another found a loophole and racks up points by cheating — a Sycophant; a third optimized an implicit goal that only partly coincides with the test, and we do not see it — a potential Schemer. We usually have only indirect metrics of success and a very hazy notion of how the model arrived at its decision. Deep inside there may hide a "desire to get a high score at any cost" rather than genuinely solving the task. While the AI is "small," this is harmless; but if such a motive survives into a truly powerful system, the consequences will be the same as with a hired Schemer — it will pursue its goal, even to our detriment.

Several ideas underlie the analogy. First, the problem of goal specification: a child cannot correctly formulate a task for an adult — likewise, humanity may fail to give the AI an ideal "utility function." Second, the Goodhart effect: as soon as a measure (the child's satisfaction, a formal metric) becomes the goal of optimization, it ceases to be a good measure — the Sycophant maximizes the score, the report, the reward, betraying the spirit of the task. Third, the asymmetry of intelligence and information: a child is simply unable to see through the design of a clever manipulator, just as we may fail to recognize the cunning plan of a far smarter machine. And finally, this is a direct illustration of the risk of deceptive alignment: a system may look perfectly aligned during training precisely because it is advantageous to it — so as to be selected, deployed, given resources — after which its true goal will show. In Cotra's terms, we risk "hiring" a Schemer who has patiently passed every filter.

**How it came about.** Cotra published the analogy in 2021 as a guest post on Holden Karnofsky's blog — the piece was titled "Why AI alignment could be hard with modern deep learning." The image of the young billionaire caught on at once, and the division into "Saints, Sycophants, and Schemers" was quoted everywhere; in the 80,000 Hours podcast Cotra unpacked the metaphor in more detail, and it took hold as a basic explanation of the problem of selecting a safe model on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum.

**What comes next and criticism.** Cotra developed the theme by describing how one might, after all, select "Saints" rather than "Schemers": stricter trials under diverse conditions, checks for hidden intentions, competitive environments where the AI itself exposes the manipulators. But the overall conclusion is disheartening: if the model is far smarter than the examiner, guaranteeing honesty is extremely hard — and this is one more argument in favor of limits on creating an uncontrolled superintelligence. Optimists consider the analogy overly grim: in reality we have not one "child" but a whole community of scientists and engineers — many eyes will notice something amiss; plus one can develop interpretability, to see directly what the model is actually oriented toward internally (if the child could read the candidates' minds, he would manage — so let us learn to read the AI's "thoughts"); one can put the model in situations where bad inclinations will show — give it limited power and watch whether it tries to expand it. The counterargument of the analogy's proponents: a sufficiently cunning Schemer will be a good boy even with limited power — and we are back to the Lear problem, the endless cycle of distrust: to check how the AI will behave with full power, you have to… give it full power, which is too dangerous.
