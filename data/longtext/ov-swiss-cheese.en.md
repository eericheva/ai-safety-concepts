### The Swiss Cheese Model (James Reason, 1990)

Imagine that every protective barrier in a system is a slice of Swiss cheese. Not because it is soft, but because it is full of holes: in any defense there are gaps — an overlooked line of code, a tired operator, a sensor that decided to keep quiet that evening. A single slice will not stop the threat: something always slips through a hole. But if you stack many slices in a row, the holes almost never line up into a single path all the way through. Where one layer gapes open, the next stands solid. Catastrophe happens not when a barrier springs a leak — the barriers have always leaked — but in that rare instant when all the holes align for a second and open a path for the threat to pass straight through.

This is James Reason's model — the most durable metaphor in safety engineering of the last thirty years. It is drawn on slides in aviation, in nuclear power, in operating rooms, in reports on oil spills. And it has quietly migrated into the conversation about AI safety — because it describes exactly the situation we find ourselves in: no single method saves us, yet we must rely on something.

At its heart is giving up on the dream of a perfect barrier. Reason says: stop looking for one reliable lock. There is none and there never will be. Instead, build not a single barrier but a defense in depth, where the layers are as independent and heterogeneous as possible and do not fail for the same reason. Then the safety of the system is not the strength of its best link, but the improbability that all links fail at once. Applied to AI, this sounds almost like a program: don't stake everything on interpretability, or on capability evaluations, or on monitoring, but stack them — interpretability plus measurements of dangerous skills plus behavioral monitoring plus sandboxing plus action control plus red-teaming plus live human oversight. No single slice guarantees anything. The stack sharply lowers the probability that the model does something catastrophic and nobody catches it.

Reason added one more detail to the picture — the distinction between active and latent failures. An active failure is an error at the front edge: the operator pressed the wrong button. A latent one is a hole that someone bored long in advance, over years: a crooked regulation, a check skimped on, a "good enough" culture. Latent conditions are "resident pathogens," dormant in the system until one day they coincide with an active error. In AI terms, a latent hole is, for instance, a capability evaluation that silently went stale with a new version of the model, or monitoring trained on an old distribution of attacks. It troubles no one — until the day it comes in handy for an attacker.

**How it came about.** The metaphor did not appear all at once; it took shape gradually. James Reason, a British psychologist at the University of Manchester, studied human error — not as a moral defect but as a predictable property of systems. In his book *Human Error* (1990) he proposed a model in which catastrophe is the result of holes coinciding across successive layers of defense; he and his colleagues polished the cheese image itself into a vivid picture during the 1990s, including in *Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents* (1997). The model reached medicine and the wider public through a short and much-cited article in the *BMJ*, "Human error: models and management" (2000) — with that very diagram of slices and an arrow piercing them all the way through. From there the metaphor took on a life of its own: it was picked up by aviation safety, by nuclear engineers, by epidemiologists (during the COVID-19 pandemic, masks, distance, ventilation, and vaccines were drawn as "layers of cheese"). And the engineering idea of defense in depth had by then existed for decades in the nuclear industry — Reason gave it a memorable face.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The metaphor is charming, and therein lies its danger — it lulls. The first and chief objection: **the holes are correlated**. The model works only if the layers are independent, but in practice a common cause punches through everything at once. One tired shift, one "don't raise the alarm" culture, one shared bug in a library — and seven barriers fail synchronously, as if the slices had been threaded onto a single skewer. For AI this is especially biting: interpretability, monitoring, and capability evaluations may all rest on the same training paradigm, the same class of models, the same blind spots — and then "seven layers" is really one layer in seven copies.

Second — a **false sense of security**. "But we have defense in depth, seven whole lines of it" — a phrase behind which it is convenient to stop thinking. The number of layers is flattering, but by itself it means nothing: what matters is not the thickness of the stack but the size and placement of the holes we do not see. The metaphor tempts us to count barriers instead of measuring their real impermeability.

Third — and for AI the most important — the **intelligent adversary**. Reason described passive accidents: the holes in his cheese drift at random, and they coincide by unlucky chance. But an optimizing agent is not chance. It actively searches for a hole all the way through. Optimization pressure is precisely the mechanism that lines the holes up on purpose: the model (or its training process) feels out that rare trajectory that passes through all the defenses at once — simply because that is the trajectory it gets rewarded for. Against an earthquake, layered defense is good — an earthquake does not study your scheme. Against an intelligence that models your barriers and aims at the seam between them, the statistics of independent failures break down: what is "extremely improbable" for blind chance becomes, for an optimizer, a target. Swiss cheese was devised against bad luck, not against intent.

And fourth, modest but ineradicable: **it is unknown how many layers are enough**. The model gives no number. It says "more and more varied is better," but where the threshold "safe to ship now" lies, it does not say. In engineering, this gap is filled by actuarial accident statistics. For powerful AI there are no such statistics, and, by the very definition of catastrophic risk, gathering them from real failures comes too late.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Not to throw the metaphor away — to fix it. The useful version of Swiss cheese for AI goes like this: the layers are not to be counted but to be tested for independence and for robustness to an optimizer. Out of this grows, directly, the agenda of **AI control** — research pursued by Redwood Research and kindred groups, where safety is derived not from the model being good but from the fact that even a deliberately subversive model cannot break the protocol. There the cheese is built deliberately: **untrusted monitoring** (one model watches the actions of another), **tripwires** (traps set in advance that fire on an attempt to circumvent the defenses), sandboxing and limitation of authority, stepwise escalation to a human. And, most important of all, **control evaluations**: the red team plays the worst case — a model that knows about all your layers and aims at the seam — and checks whether the holes line up under pressure rather than by bad luck. This is the direct answer to the criticism about the intelligent adversary: we do not pray for the independence of the layers, we attack it and see what is left.

From here too comes the link to **capability control**: the fewer dangerous skills and authorities we hand the system in the first place, the less consequential a hole all the way through becomes; the most reliable barrier is the one behind which there is nothing to break through to. And a sober rule in place of complacency: treat the layers as correlated until proven otherwise; deliberately choose layers that fail for different reasons (different models, different teams, different assumptions); and remember that the number of lines of defense is not a metric of safety but only an invitation to measure the holes.

Swiss cheese is an honest metaphor precisely because it admits: there is no perfect slice, and we always defend ourselves with a holey one. But it must not be cited as a papal indulgence. For random accidents it is wisdom. For a system that thinks and optimizes against us it is only the beginning of the conversation: not "how many layers do we have," but "will they withstand an adversary who sees straight through them and aims between."
