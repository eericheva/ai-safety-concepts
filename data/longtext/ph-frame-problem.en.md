### The Frame Problem (John McCarthy & Patrick Hayes, 1969)

A robot stands before a table. It has been told to move the table to the window. It takes hold of the edge, pushes — and in that instant something must happen inside its logical core that is so obvious to a human that we do not even notice it as an event. The robot must *know* that the color of the walls has stayed the same. That night has not fallen outside the window. That the number of prime numbers has not changed. That the coffee in the cup across the room is still warm, and that the robot itself is still called what it was called a minute ago. The world after the push of the table coincides almost entirely with the world before the push — and it is that "almost entirely" that turns out to be hell.

Because how is the robot to know what exactly did *not* change? Naive logic forces it to write out so-called "frame axioms" — for every action and every fact a separate statement: "pushing the table does not change the color of the walls," "pushing the table does not turn off the light," "pushing the table does not turn the coffee to ice." Actions number in the thousands, facts about the world are infinite, and at their intersection a whole universe of statements swells up, each one to the effect that nothing has happened. This is the frame problem in its original, technical sense: how to represent in logic that an action changes exactly what it changes — without explicitly enumerating the boundless multitude of everything it does *not* touch.

The trouble is not that the robot is too lazy to write out a million axioms. The trouble is that, even having written them, it must at every step *check* them — run them through inference, making sure the consequences are consistent. And the consequences branch. It pushed the table — the table's shadow shifted — the illumination of the corner changed — but did the air warm from the friction? And what if a time bomb was lying on the table? A logical system that honestly enumerates the consequences of its action has no built-in way to stop at the relevant ones and discard the empty ones. It does not know what matters, because "matters" is not a logical category.

**How it came about.** The term was introduced by John McCarthy and Patrick Hayes in their 1969 paper "Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence" (Machine Intelligence 4). The problem takes its name from the "frame" itself — the fixed frame of background facts, presumed unchanged by default, around the little that an action does change. McCarthy and Hayes were logicists: they believed intelligence could be built on the predicate calculus and a formal representation of knowledge about actions (situation calculus). And it was from within that program, conscientiously pushed to its limit, that the monster crawled out — the technical impossibility of writing down the inertia of the world economically.

Fifteen years later Daniel Dennett, in his essay "Cognitive Wheels: The Frame Problem of AI" (1984), dragged the problem out of the logical cellar into the philosophical light — and gave it an immortal parable. Robot R1 must haul its spare battery out of a locked room; the battery sits on a cart, and on that same cart — unfortunately — sits a ticking bomb. R1 rolls the cart with the battery outside. Along with the battery, the bomb rolls out too. Explosion. R1 knew it was hauling out the battery, but did not infer that it was hauling out the bomb as well. The engineers build R1D1, which deduces *all* the side consequences of its actions. It freezes before the cart, honestly proving theorems: "hauling out the cart will not change the color of the walls," "the wheels of the cart will turn more revolutions than there are wheels on the cart"… — the bomb goes off while it enumerates the implications. They build R2D1, taught to divide consequences into relevant and irrelevant — and it freezes still earlier, because now it has to enumerate the endless list of *what it has decided not to consider*, tagging each item as "ignore." Dennett showed: neither diligence nor care can save the naive formalization of common sense. The problem is not the volume of computation, but that relevance cannot be mined by enumeration — one would already have to know what is relevant.

The two poles — the technical and the philosophical — should not be blurred into one. The engineering frame problem is in large part *solved*. Nonmonotonic logic (Reiter, McCarthy with his circumscription), the fluent calculus of Hölldobler and Schneeberger, developed further by Michael Thielscher, the event calculus formalism of Kowalski and Sergot, Reiter's neat "successor state" axioms — all of this makes it possible to write down the inertia of the world compactly: by default a fluent (a fact whose value can change from one situation to the next) keeps its value unless an action explicitly changes it. Murray Shanahan, in his book "Solving the Frame Problem" (1997), drew a line under this: as a *logical* task of representing change, the frame problem is more or less solved. But the philosophical core remained untouched.

**Criticism / arguments against.** Logicians are justifiably irritated when philosophers say "the frame problem is not solved": in the narrow sense for which McCarthy and Hayes posed it, solutions exist and work. Patrick Hayes, in a paper whose title says it all, "What the Frame Problem Is and Isn't" (1987), directly objected to the expansive reading: you, he in effect told the philosophers, have hung a whole heap of different difficulties on our technical term (the qualification problem, the ramification problem, the problem of induction) and called all of it by one name.

On the other side, philosophers led by Jerry Fodor answer: it is precisely in the broad sense that the problem is interesting — and invincible. Fodor, in "The Modularity of Mind" (1983) and later in "The Mind Doesn't Work That Way" (2000), reformulated the frame problem as a problem of *abduction* and *the holism of relevance*: which of my beliefs should I revise in the light of a new fact? In principle — any of them may turn out relevant to any other. Relevance is not local; it depends on the whole background of knowledge at once (holism), and so it cannot be computed modularly, "without looking at everything." And to "look at everything" is exactly what blows up Dennett's robot. The criticism of the criticism runs thus: perhaps we mistook the absence of a *formal* solution for the absence of a solution altogether — people do somehow manage, so there is a mechanism — it is just that it is not logical but, say, statistical or heuristic.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The frame problem is not a historical curiosity of symbolic AI but a point at which one can see what common sense actually demands. It is akin to the "Winograd schemas": in the sentence "the city councilmen refused the demonstrators a permit because *they* feared violence," one can grasp to whom "they" refers only by silently invoking a boundless background of knowledge about councils and demonstrators — that is, by solving a local problem of relevance. It is also the flip side of the symbol grounding problem (Harnad): to know what is relevant, symbols must be tied to the world, not only to one another.

Large language models are interesting in that they *bypass* the engineering frame problem without solving it head-on. They do not write out axioms of inertia and do not enumerate consequences — they have learned from terabytes of text the statistical shadow of human common sense: in the overwhelming majority of described situations the walls did not change color, and the model by default continues the world the way people continue it. Relevance here is not inferred but *reproduced* — as the averaged habit of the corpus. This explains why GPT-like systems seem endowed with common sense where symbolic systems spun their wheels for decades.

But the philosophical core of Dennett's argument has gone nowhere — it has merely moved up a floor. An LLM is superb at guessing the typical and catastrophically ignorant of *the boundaries of its own knowledge*: it does not distinguish "this is irrelevant" from "there was nothing about this in the training data." The problem of "knowing about not-knowing," the problem of reliable relevance in a new, unencountered situation, the problem that the model cannot *stop* and say "here my background is silent" — this is all the same cart with the bomb, only now the robot does not freeze but confidently rolls it outside, because statistically that was done more often. For alignment this is exactly the sore point: a system that bypasses the problem of relevance by averaging will err precisely where the situation is atypical and the cost of error is high. The frame problem, posed as the question "how does the system know what matters now," remains an unwritten page — and perhaps the main one.
