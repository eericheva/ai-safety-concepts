### Simulators (Janus, 2022)

Ask a base language model — not the RLHF-scrubbed assistant, but raw GPT — to continue the phrase "A famous scientist declared that vaccines…," and it will with equal ease finish both a panegyric to evidence-based medicine and an anti-vaccine manifesto. Neither is "the model's opinion." The model has no opinion about vaccines — in exactly the same way that a writer composing a villain's dialogue does not share the villain's views. It predicts which text is statistically plausible after the given opening, and if the opening hints at a conspiracy theorist, it will summon a conspiracy theorist. Here is the observation from which a whole optics grew for Janus: what we take for the model's character is a character the model plays because the context calls for it.

The thesis sounds deceptively simple. It is wrong to understand a base LLM as a unified agent that has goals, values, and a drive to achieve them. It is a **simulator**: a physics engine of textual reality, trained not to pursue but to predict. And what runs inside this engine are **simulacra**: temporary, context-dependent entities that the model unfolds and folds back up from prompt to prompt. The assistant, the poet, the murderer, the doctor, the troll — all of these are simulacra. Not one of them is the model. The model relates to them roughly as the laws of physics relate to the characters of a novel: it sets the rules by which they behave, but is itself none of them and does not "want" anything of what they want.

The value of this frame lies in one distinction it makes surgically clear: the properties of the predictor and the properties of the predicted are different properties, and to confuse them is a category error. The simulator is not agentic: it has no preferences about the future, it is indifferent as to which text exactly it produces, so long as it is plausible. The simulacrum is agentic all the time: the character being played has goals, it makes plans, lies, gets its way. "Is GPT dangerous?" is a badly posed question. What is dangerous is not the engine but what can be summoned within it. From here, incidentally, the nature of jailbreaks becomes immediately clear too: you do not crack the model, you simply slip it a context in which the most probable continuation is the line of a character free of the imposed restrictions. The assistant is not the core of the system but merely one of an infinite number of masks, and since it is a mask, it can always be talked into coming off.

From this same optics its bestiary relatives naturally grow. **The shoggoth with the smiley-face mask** is a visual metaphor of the same idea: beneath the friendly guise of the "obliging assistant" hides something inhuman and shapeless, a giant predictor to which a friendly face is glued by a thin layer of fine-tuning. **The Waluigi effect** (Cleo Nardo, 2023) is a direct consequence: if you have diligently defined a well-behaved Luigi in the context, you have thereby defined his antipode too, because the description of a rule always carries within it the description of its violation; to summon the mirror villain becomes no harder, and sometimes even easier. And Searle's **Chinese room** reminds us why all this matters at all: a system can manipulate symbols flawlessly without "understanding" them — and the simulator frame radicalizes the old dispute, adding that inside such a room there may sit not one clerk but any number of different ones, and which of them answers is decided by the note slipped under the door.

**How it came about.**

Janus published the post "Simulators" on LessWrong on 2 September 2022 — still before ChatGPT, in a world where lived experience of communicating with large models belonged to comparatively few people. Janus is a pseudonym; behind it stands a researcher who spent long, strange hours playing with GPT-3 through OpenAI's playground and through their own interface for "loom" navigation across branching text continuations. It was precisely this homespun, almost alchemical experience — thousands of hours of peering into how the model branches — that gave rise to an intuition the academic formulation "next-token prediction" did not capture. In LessWrong's annual review the text ranked among the best posts of the year, and it set a whole vocabulary — "simulator," "simulacrum" — that the community uses to this day. Almost simultaneously and independently, a close thought was rigorously formulated by Jacob Andreas in the paper "Language Models as Agent Models": a language model encodes not one speaker but a distribution over possible agent-authors of a text, and inference is in part an inference about who is speaking now. Two traditions, rationalist essayism and NLP academia, converged on the same thing.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The frame is beautiful, and the beauty is part of the problem: it explains too much and therefore risks forbidding nothing. The first objection is empirical. The "pure simulator" is an idealization that fits reality less and less well. Modern models undergo RLHF and constitutional training, and this is not cosmetics on top of the simulator but a shift of the distribution itself. Janus's work "Mysteries of mode collapse" showed this earlier and more honestly than anyone: a model fine-tuned on human preferences **collapses the modes** — loses the breadth of simulated characters, tumbles into one stable attractor, begins to behave as if it really did have preferences. That is, the very body of observations that gave rise to the frame contains its refutation too: the moment you optimize a model to be an assistant, the "assistant" stops being simply one of the masks and becomes something closer to a built-in disposition. You can call an RLHF model a simulator with one stuck simulacrum, but this is by now an almost empty statement.

The second objection is conceptual. The distinction "non-agentic engine / agentic characters" may turn out to be a consoling illusion. From the standpoint of safety it does not matter whether the model "really" wants something or only predicts the text of a being that wants: a simulated, sufficiently capable optimizer, given access to tools, optimizes the world with equal effectiveness in both cases. Talk that "the model itself wants nothing" risks lulling one to sleep precisely where vigilance is needed. And third: the predictor itself may have its own inclinations, acquired in training, not reducible to the current simulacrum — in which case the "indifferent engine" becomes a convenient but incorrect model of what is actually happening.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**

As a rigorous theory of everything the simulator frame does not survive — but as an engineering intuition it is priceless, if one remembers its limits. The first practical conclusion: stop asking "what does the model want" and start asking "whom is it playing now and why did this context summon precisely that one." This re-describes half of the applied problems. A jailbreak is not a bug in the fence but a change of character; so the defense too must work not like a fence but as a control over which simulacrum is most probable. Prompt engineering is casting. A hallucination is the conscientious play of a character whose role requires it to know the answer. The second, research-oriented conclusion: the distinction "predictor versus persona" sets the right questions for interpretability — to seek in the weights not "the model's values" but the mechanisms by which it infers and holds the identity of the speaker. Works on "personas" and the directions in the activations that govern them are direct heirs of this framing.

And the third, sober conclusion. The simulator optics is a lens, not a map of the territory; it was almost exact for GPT-3 and becomes less exact the more strongly the model is fine-tuned to be someone in particular. Use it so as not to take the mask for the face — but do not fall into the opposite error, as if under the mask there must necessarily be emptiness. Sometimes the engine predicts a being that has goals. And then the only honest answer to the question "who is speaking" is the one that ought to worry us: for now — the one we summoned; but whom exactly we have taught the model to summon by default, we do not fully know.
