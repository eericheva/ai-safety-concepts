### Multiple Realizability (Putnam / Fodor, 1967–1974)

An octopus jerks a tentacle away from the hot edge of the aquarium. It has no C-fibers — those thin unmyelinated nerves that in mammals conduct dull, aching pain. Its nervous system is built quite differently in general: two-thirds of its neurons are moved out of the brain into the tentacles, each arm partly "thinking" for itself. And yet few, watching that recoil, are prepared to say that the octopus feels nothing. And if so, then pain cannot be *the same thing* as the firing of C-fibers. For the octopus has none of them, yet the pain, it seems, is there.

That is the whole argument, in one mollusk. Multiple realizability is the thesis that one and the same mental state can be embodied in physically completely different systems: in a human brain, in an octopus's ganglia, in the silicon innards of a hypothetical Martian, and in the limit — in anything built *functionally* right. From this follows a conclusion deadly to one influential theory: a mental state cannot be identified with a particular physical type. The formula "pain = C-fiber firing" is false not because pain is something immaterial but because it ties pain too narrowly to one single physics. Pain is a role, and a role can have many performers.

The mechanism here is subtle, and it is worth spelling out carefully, so as not to topple into dualism. No one asserts that pain hovers above matter. What is asserted is more modest and more radical: the type of a mental state is determined not by *what* the system is made of but by *what that state does* — which inputs cause it, which outputs it produces, with which other states it is connected. Pain is that which is typically caused by injury, produces avoidance, a cry, a memory of danger, and is connected to a desire for it to stop. A causal role. And if the causal role can be played on silicon just as on carbon, then to identify the state with a particular protein or a particular neuron is to confuse the actor with the character. From here a straight road leads to functionalism: the mind as organization, not as substance. And from here, too — the autonomy of psychology: since psychological types "carve" the world differently than neural ones, the science of the mind is not reducible to neuroscience and does not dissolve into it.

**How it came about.** The thesis was born as a weapon against a specific opponent — the type identity theory, developed in the late 1950s by Ullin Place and John Smart. They asserted: mental states are literally identical to brain states, pain *is* a particular brain process, just as lightning *is* an electrical discharge. Hilary Putnam, in the paper "Psychological Predicates" (1967; later reprinted under the title "The Nature of Mental States"), struck with the question of diversity: pain is felt by mammals, reptiles, mollusks, and hypothetically by aliens too. To demand that all of them have one and the same physico-chemical correlate is "certainly an ambitious hypothesis." It follows that the identity theory loses empirically to functionalism, which makes no such demand. Putnam at the time formulated the mind through Turing machines: a mental state is a functional state of a probabilistic automaton.

Seven years later Jerry Fodor, in the paper "Special Sciences" (1974), raised the stakes, carrying the thesis from the philosophy of mind into the philosophy of science in general. His image is economics. The concept "money" is realized by shells, coins, banknotes, entries on a blockchain; no single physical law covers all exchanges. In just the same way the "special sciences" — psychology, biology, economics — work with types multiply realized in physics, and therefore do not reduce to it while remaining fully materialist. Multiple realizability became not a curiosity about octopuses but a load-bearing beam of the whole non-reductive picture of the world, where the floors of reality are stacked one above another but do not collapse into one.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first blow came from neuroscience — and it is serious. William Bechtel and Jennifer Mundale, in the paper "Multiple Realizability Revisited" (1999), noticed: the argument imperceptibly cheats with levels of description. When we say "pain," we take a coarse-grained, functional concept. When we say "C-fibers," a fine-grained, anatomical one. No wonder the coarse concept maps onto many different fine ones. But one must compare at a single level. And neuroscientists, by the way, identify brain regions across species precisely by function — the hippocampus of a mouse and of a human is called by one word not because of identical molecular innards but because of a shared role. If one applies a single scale of vision to both sides, brains turn out far *more alike* than the functionalist paints them. Realizability, say Bechtel and Mundale, is rhetorically exaggerated.

The second blow is metaphysical, and it was struck by Jaegwon Kim. In "Multiple Realization and the Metaphysics of Reduction" (1992) and later in the book *Mind in a Physical World* (1998) he turned the thesis against functionalism itself. If pain-in-a-human and pain-in-an-octopus have nothing physically in common, then what makes both of them *pain* at all? Only an external similarity of role. But then "pain in general" is not a genuine causal kind but a heterogeneous dump, and there can be no scientific laws about it: science will speak separately of pain-in-a-brain and pain-in-silicon. Kim turns the argument inside out: multiple realizability does not save the autonomy of psychology but threatens its unity. And it drags along the famous *problem of mental causation*: if every mental state is realized by a physical one, and the physical already has a complete physical cause, then what is left for the mental as such to do? Does the role not turn out to be an epiphenomenon, a powerless passenger on the physical locomotive?

The third, most stubborn question is *what "the same" function means* on different substrates. Lawrence Shapiro, in "Multiple Realizations" (2000), noticed: two realizations either differ only in causally irrelevant details (then it is, in essence, the same realization — the color of a corkscrew does not matter for uncorking) or differ in relevant ones (then they are, perhaps, *different* functions, and one can no longer speak of "the same pain"). Between these blades there is much less room for genuine multiple realization than it seems. And here lies a trap for AI enthusiasts: "the same" is all too easily declared on the basis of coarse behavioral similarity, without asking whether the difference of substrate matters for the very nature of the state.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For alignment, multiple realizability is not a museum exhibit but a working premise, often unspoken. The thesis of the *substrate independence* of the mind, on which transhumanism rests, and talk of mind uploading, and the whole idea that a digital system can possess a full inner world — is a direct child of Putnam and Fodor. If the mind is organization, not substance, then in silicon it is in principle possible.

From this follow two conclusions, and both are uncomfortable. The first is about the moral status of AI. If pain really is realizable on a non-biological substrate, then one cannot wave away the question of AI suffering with the phrase "it's just a program, it has no nerves": the absence of nerves is exactly the objection that multiple realizability removes. The threshold beyond which we are obliged to reckon with another's suffering ceases to coincide with the threshold "made of meat." The second conclusion is cautionary, and it comes from the critics. From the fact that pain *in principle* can be in silicon it does not follow that it *is* in *this* model. Functional similarity of behavior is too cheap a coin: a language model says "it hurts" splendidly while having, perhaps, nothing behind the words. Ned Block's thought experiment about "the Chinese nation" (the China brain, "Troubles with Functionalism," 1978) — when a billion Chinese, passing signals by walkie-talkie, realize for an hour the functional organization of a brain — is honed precisely against naive functionalism: a system functionally isomorphic to a human intuitively seems devoid of experiences. The question "is a functional role alone enough for consciousness, or does the substrate matter after all" remains open — and it is in this crack that all the uncertainty of our attitude to the possible suffering of machines lives.

The practical conclusion is modest and honest: multiple realizability forbids us to *deny a priori* an AI's inner life on the basis of material, but gives no right to *assert it a priori* on the basis of behavior. It shifts the burden of proof, it does not abolish it. And it leaves us with a task we have barely approached — to learn to distinguish the realization of pain from its convincing imitation, having access neither to another's C-fibers, nor to another's silicon, nor to whether there is anyone in there at all for whom it hurts.
