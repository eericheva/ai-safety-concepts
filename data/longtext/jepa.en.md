### JEPA: a different architecture — a different shape of risk

Almost all present-day fears and hopes revolve around large language models — autoregressive predictors of the next token. But this is not the only path to a powerful AI, and Yann LeCun (one of the "godfathers" of deep learning) has long argued that it is not even the best one. In his 2022 position paper, "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence," he criticizes the pure generative paradigm: predicting everything down to the last pixel or token is wasteful and does not let you build a reliable model of the world. His alternative, JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture), predicts not raw data but abstract representations of it (embeddings). The model learns in a self-supervised way, guessing the hidden or future parts of a scene in semantic space rather than at the level of pixels — and thus builds up an internal model of the world.

Technically, JEPA rests on energy-based models: instead of generating a single "correct" answer, the system evaluates the compatibility of the representations of the input and the prediction. LeCun also sketches a whole architecture for an autonomous agent: a world model (predicts how the world will respond to actions), a "configurator," an actor (plans actions), perception, short-term memory, and — importantly for our conversation — a separate cost module that sets goals and constraints. Implementations of this line already exist: I-JEPA for images, V-JEPA for video. The ultimate aim is machines that plan, reason, and possess common sense by virtue of a world model, rather than just deftly shuffling text.

Why this belongs in the chapter about the frontier of capabilities, and what risk has to do with it. The very type of mind we build changes the shape of the danger. Curiously, LeCun is a well-known skeptic regarding the existential risks of AI: he considers the fears exaggerated and is confident that an "objective-driven" AI can be made safe by design — by setting explicit goals and hard constraints in the cost function (for example, "do not harm humans" as a mandatory term in the cost module). And there is a grain of truth in this: explicit, externalized goals can in principle be inspected and edited — unlike the murky "goals" of present-day LLMs, smeared across the weights.

But this is exactly where his opponents catch him. First, an explicit goal does not abolish the problem of its specification: if it is specified poorly — even if openly and explicitly — it still leads to the same paperclips, and instrumental convergence doesn't go away. Second — and this matters more — a competent agent with a world model, capable of planning and acting, is precisely that objective-driven intelligence at which all the classic alignment anxieties are aimed. A passive text predictor, which only answers a query, may in this sense be safer than an autonomous planner that itself builds multi-step schemes in a world model for the sake of a goal. That is, a JEPA-like AI may make some things safer (explicit, readable goals) and others more dangerous (genuine agency and planning). The dispute is unresolved.

The practical upshot is sobering: architecture is not a detail but a risk parameter. The threat profile of an autoregressive chatbot differs from that of a planning agent with a world model, and each calls for its own methods of evaluation and control. And since the field may well shift from LLMs to systems in the spirit of JEPA, it is worth preparing these methods in advance — while "world model + planner" has not yet become mainstream.
