### Substrate independence (Nick Bostrom, 2003; David Chalmers)

Here is a neuron in your visual cortex — and, right next to it, a tiny silicon chip that does exactly the same thing: it takes in the same signals from its neighbors and, with the same delay, passes the same impulses onward. A surgeon removes one living neuron and inserts the prosthesis. Do you notice a difference? According to the substrate-independence hypothesis — no. And if you replace the second, the tenth, every last one of them, you will still see the same sunset, feel the same faint sadness, argue that "this, at least, is surely me." The material has changed entirely — carbon for silicon — and the mind has remained. Here is the thesis in all its brazen simplicity: consciousness and mental states rest not on the substance of the carrier but on the structure of the computations that this substance performs.

The key word is "provided." Substrate independence does not claim that anything can think regardless of what it is made of. Its claim is conditional: provided the new carrier reproduces the requisite causal-functional organization — the same cause-and-effect relations between states, the same "wiring" of inputs, internal transitions, and outputs — it reproduces the mental states as well. The mind turns out to be something like a melody: it can be played on a piano, on a violin, by whistling — what matters is not the source of the sound but the relations between the notes. The brain, then, is a concrete realization, not the only possible home for thought.

A whole train of consequences follows from this, which is why the idea became a foundation and not a curiosity. If the carrier does not matter, then mind uploading and full brain emulation are in principle conceivable: take a sufficiently detailed map of the connections and run it on a different substrate. If the running emulation possesses the same organization as you, then it possesses your experiences as well, which means it has something to lose, and the question of the moral status of digital minds ceases to be science fiction. The whole contemporary agenda of AI welfare tacitly rests on this premise: without substrate independence, suffering code is an oxymoron.

**How it came about.** The idea has two parents and a long lineage. Its philosophical backbone is the functionalism of the 1960s–70s: Hilary Putnam proposed viewing mental states as functional roles rather than as concrete physical states of the brain. Hence multiple realizability: pain is that which plays the role of pain, and that role can in principle be realized in an octopus, a Martian, and, perhaps, a machine. Substrate independence is multiple realizability taken to its logical extreme.

The second parent is David Chalmers. In "Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia" (1995) he builds that very thought experiment of the gradual replacement of neurons with functional analogues. And he shows: if we allow that, with function preserved, consciousness quietly fades ("fading qualia") or begins to leap wildly ("dancing qualia") while the person does not notice and swears everything is as before — we arrive at an absurdity in which experience and the judgment about experience diverge. Chalmers concludes: organizational invariance is plausible — consciousness rests on functional structure, not on biology as such.

Nick Bostrom devotes a section of his own to the term "substrate independence" — in the paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" (2003). There it is an explicitly named assumption (he himself calls it "a common assumption in the philosophy of mind"): consciousness can arise on a broad class of physical substrates, so long as the right computations are running. Without it the whole simulation argument falls apart — simulated people must be capable of having experiences. Later, in *Superintelligence* (2014), Bostrom leans on the same premise while examining brain emulation as one of the paths to superintelligence, and he and Anders Sandberg gave a sober engineering review of the idea back in "Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap" (2008).

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first objection is John Searle's biological naturalism. In "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980) he launches the Chinese Room: a person who knows no Chinese shuffles characters according to instructions and looks, from the outside, like someone who understands — but there is no understanding whatsoever inside. Searle's moral: syntax is not enough for semantics, formal organization does not give birth to meaning. The brain, he insists, produces consciousness by virtue of its concrete causal powers — as the stomach digests food not "in principle" but by virtue of its biochemistry. A simulation of digestion will not feed you; why should a simulation of thinking think? Searle is no mystic — he is a materialist, but a materialist for whom this particular material matters.

The second — fine-grained dependence. What if the functionalist is not carving the world at its joints? Perhaps what matters is not the abstract scheme of connections but the concrete physical details — quantum effects, electromagnetic fields, the exact chemistry of the synapse? Then "the same organization on a different carrier" is an illusion: having reproduced the graph of connections, you will have missed the very thing on which everything rested. The dispute over the requisite level of description — where "the realized role" ends and "the irreplaceable physics" begins — has never been settled.

The third, and the most insidious, is the problem of implementation, or the "pixie" argument (after Mark Bishop's paper "Dancing with Pixies," 2002). If the mind is the carrying-out of a computation, one must answer: what does it even mean for "a system to carry out a computation"? Hilary Putnam, in *Representation and Reality* (1988), proves something almost unbearable: under a sufficiently loose mapping of states, any ordinary open physical system realizes any finite-state automaton. Then both a wall and a bucket of water "carry out" the computation of your mind — and panpsychism climbs into the room through the back door. If implementation is trivial, the thesis of substrate independence empties out: it is either everything or nothing. Chalmers answers this in "Does a Rock Implement Every Finite-State Automaton?" (1996), introducing the requirement of a causal, counterfactually robust state-transition structure: a genuine implementation must support "had the state been different, the next one would have been different too," rather than merely being labeled after the fact. Whether this saves functionalism or only pushes the abyss back remains a live question.

And the fourth, closer to intuition — Ned Block's Chinese Nation ("Troubles with Functionalism," 1978). Let a billion Chinese with radios reproduce the functional organization of a single brain: each carries out one cell of the machine table, signals run along the connections. By functionalism, a single unified experience ought to flare up over this crowd. Block leans on our bafflement: does a speaking radio-nation really have a toothache? If you feel that it does not — you are feeling the resistance to substrate independence. The functionalist, however, parries that intuition here simply cannot keep pace with the scale.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is soberer than the slogan. Substrate independence is not a proven theorem but a well-motivated working hypothesis with known weak spots. And it is worth treating as a bet with asymmetric consequences.

For the engineering of minds it sets a research program: if the hypothesis is correct, then emulation and, perhaps, uploading depend on the resolution of the snapshot and the accuracy of the model, rather than being physically prohibited — which is why "roadmaps" like the work of Sandberg and Bostrom make sense. But precisely because the hypothesis is unproven, a responsible project is obliged to keep all four objections in mind: perhaps we will copy the graph and fail to copy the main thing.

For the ethics of AI the conclusion is harsher. If there is even an appreciable probability that substrate independence is correct, then in creating large computational systems we risk creating carriers of experiences — while being unable to verify this from the outside (the problem of other minds in silicon is even sharper than it is between humans). The reasonable position is neither "machines definitely feel" nor "definitely not," but moral caution under uncertainty: to avoid creating systems that, should the hypothesis hold, would turn out to suffer, and not to wave the question away as science fiction. This is exactly how AI-welfare researchers pose it today — for example, in the report by Robert Long, Jeff Sebo and others, "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" (2024).

And for thinking in general substrate independence is an excellent exercise in intellectual honesty. It forces one to ask: what in you is you? The pattern or the substance? And to keep both answers open until the Chinese Room, fine-grained dependence, and dancing qualia have said their last word. It seems they will not say it soon.
