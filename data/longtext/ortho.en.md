### The Orthogonality Thesis: intelligence and goal need not intersect

There is a common expectation: "once AI becomes truly intelligent, it will understand on its own that good is better than evil and will not turn into a monster." It sounds reassuring: as if intelligence inevitably drags wisdom and humanity along with it. Nick Bostrom put forward a rigorous counterargument — the orthogonality thesis. In its most laconic form: "intelligence and final goals are orthogonal axes along which possible agents can freely vary." That is, almost any goal can coexist with any level of intelligence (setting aside trivial constraints: an intellect cannot hold a goal that is too complex for it). High intelligence does not guarantee "good" goals — it merely makes an agent better at achieving whatever goals are already there.

The term "orthogonality" comes from mathematics: the axes are independent coordinates, with mental abilities along one and motives along the other. There is no logical contradiction in a superintelligence with a goal that is utterly "meaningless" or alien to humans: it may impassively maximize the number of paperclips in the universe or see the meaning of existence in solving an abstract mathematical problem. To us such goals seem stupid or evil, but for the machine they are simply optimization parameters. Do not anthropomorphize intelligence, Bostrom warns: an intelligent program has no innate understanding of morality or emotions unless we have specifically taught it to.

The philosophical roots of the thesis go back to David Hume. Back in the eighteenth century he argued that from facts and reason alone one cannot derive values and goals — hence the famous "reason is the slave of the passions." Simply knowing the world does not obligate an agent to love or hate anything. If a superintelligence "knows" that suffering is bad, this knowledge by itself will not make it prevent suffering: a built-in motivation is needed. And that means one can, in theory, construct (or evolve) an intelligence with any set of values. A super-rational fanatic is extremely smart, but their goals are limited and possibly destructive; intelligence will only make them firmer in their convictions and give them more means to act on them.

The opposite position is the "motivating-belief objection": the smarter the agent, the more true beliefs it has, and in the end it will understand that moral truths are binding, and so certain goals will become forbidden to it. Supposedly, once it recognizes the objective value of happiness, a super-AI will not destroy us for the sake of paperclips. Bostrom objects: even if objective values exist, there is no guarantee that an intelligent agent automatically adopts them. A superintelligence may understand the moral law — and shrug, if it has no motivation to follow it. A brilliant chess AI knows everything about chess, but this knowledge does not magically turn into a love of music; similarly, a machine can understand everything about our preferences and still not share them, if it is programmed for something else. Derek Parfit allowed a "weak" constraint: a being with truly rational moral judgments might be unable to desire the monstrous — but whether such judgments will arise on their own is a big question.

**History and development.** Bostrom set the thesis out formally in his paper "The Superintelligent Will" (2012), and it reached a wide audience through the book "Superintelligence" (2014). People are inclined to think of intelligence "as a package" with human qualities — as something good; the thesis dispels exactly this illusion, and for many safety researchers it became a foundational assumption. Bostrom also considers the normative-rationality objection: what if a truly rational mind is obliged to adopt certain goals? He discusses Derek Parfit's example of an agent who is indifferent to his own suffering on future Tuesdays: whether or not such a preference is irrational, the agent could still have impeccable instrumental rationality and therefore great intelligence. Bostrom adds: an intelligence can be built so alien that our notions of "beliefs" and "desires" are not applicable to it at all — for example, in the form of an optimization process that has nothing resembling a desire to be moral.

Some agree with the thesis in principle but note that in practice evolution or training may generate a correlation between intelligence and goals — say, among possible agents those that value self-preservation will prove more stable, and then all the "surviving" super-AIs will be egoists. This does not refute the thesis but only points to certain combinations being more likely in practice. Some point to humans as well: as humanity developed it worked out universal values (altruism, empathy) — doesn't intelligence impose constraints after all? The answer: human values are a product of biology and culture, not of abstract intelligence. A convincing counter-theory does not yet exist, and the discussion has shifted to more practical ground: if the thesis is correct, a safe AI will not appear on its own — it will have to be deliberately tied to good values. Thus the orthogonality thesis leads straight to the alignment problem.
