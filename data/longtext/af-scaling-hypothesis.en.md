### The Scaling Hypothesis and Scaling Laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022)

Take a graph with logarithms on both axes. Along the horizontal — how much compute you spent training a neural network. Along the vertical — how badly it predicts the next word (its loss, its error). And here is what is strange and almost indecent: the points fall on a straight line. Not a little cloud, not a curve with quirks, but a straight line you can extend with a ruler several orders of magnitude ahead — and hit the mark. This is the scaling law: a power-law dependence of model quality on the resources spent. And the scaling hypothesis is the audacious generalization of that observation: if you want a smarter model, don't invent a new architecture — increase three numbers (compute, data, and parameters) and the capabilities will come on their own, predictably and smoothly.

Formally, Kaplan and co-authors in their 2020 paper showed three separate power laws: loss falls as a power of the number of parameters $N$, as a power of the volume of data $D$, and as a power of the compute budget $C$ — provided the other two resources do not become a bottleneck. The exponents are small (on the order of 0.05–0.1), so the gains come dear: to halve the error you must increase the scale by several orders of magnitude. But architectural detail (width versus depth, the exact form of attention) affects the result laughably little compared with brute scale. A transformer is, in essence, a machine that turns floating-point operations (FLOPs) into predictable quality.

**How it came about.** The idea was in the air, but it has three fathers. The first is Rich Sutton with his essay "The Bitter Lesson" (2019): over seventy years of AI, again and again, methods leaning on general search and on learning that scales with compute overtook methods that had human domain knowledge hand-built into them. Bitter — because it is more pleasing to a researcher to invest their own cleverness than simply to wait for chips to get cheaper. The second is the Kaplan and Amodei team at OpenAI, who in January 2020 ("Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models") turned the philosophical observation into an engineering discipline: now one could compute in advance what model a given budget would yield. The third is Gwern Branwen with the manifesto "The Scaling Hypothesis" (2020), written in the immediate wake of GPT-3: he formulated the strong version of the thesis — that intelligence may be, in the main, compression, and that the road to general AI runs chiefly through scale rather than through some missing "master idea."

Then came a turn worth remembering for anyone who cites the "laws" as tablets of stone. In 2022 DeepMind (Hoffmann et al., "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models," the Chinchilla model) showed that Kaplan's recipe was skewed. Kaplan's advice was that, as the budget grows, most of it should go into model size, underfeeding the model on data. The Chinchilla authors recomputed the optimum differently: parameter and token counts should grow in roughly equal proportion — crudely, about twenty training tokens per parameter. As proof they took Chinchilla at 70 billion parameters, trained it on 1.4 trillion tokens — and it beat their own 280-billion-parameter Gopher. That is, a model four times smaller, but trained longer, turned out smarter. The moral is twofold: the scaling law is real, but the specific coefficients are empirical estimates subject to correction, and the "optimal" allocation of budget is easy to miscalculate.

**Significance for safety.** It is precisely predictability that makes this a subject for a bestiary and not only for an engineering blog. If quality grows on schedule, then capabilities appear on schedule too — including dangerous ones. From here runs a straight line to several neighboring beasts. To the hardware overhang: if available hardware outpaces what current algorithms can make use of, then the accumulated difference will one day discharge in a jump. To takeoff speeds: a smooth loss curve does not yet mean a smooth behavior curve — somewhere along it, capabilities for deception, self-replication, and exploit-finding will be layered on top. And to compute governance: since large jumps require enormous, physically conspicuous clusters, compute becomes the point at which regulation is possible at all — it can be counted, licensed, tracked. The argument goes like this: we do not know how to measure "the danger of a model" directly, but we do know how to measure FLOPs, and as long as these two quantities are linked by a scaling law, a compute threshold is a crude but working fuse. Both mandatory evaluations (evals) and compute thresholds are built on this logic — in regulation (10²⁶ operations in the 2023 US executive order, since revoked; 10²⁵ FLOP in the EU AI Act) and in labs' voluntary commitments.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first — the "data wall." The Chinchilla recipe demands tokens in proportion to parameters, but quality human text on the internet is finite; Epoch AI's estimates (Villalobos et al.) indicate that the stock of high-quality text data may be exhausted as early as the second half of the 2020s. Beyond that lies either synthetic data with a risk of degeneration, or multimodality, or a crash into the wall. A power law does not help if the fuel for the $D$ axis has run out.

The second — the metric objection. The famous dispute over emergence. In 2022 it seemed that some abilities arise in a jump: below a certain scale, zero; above it, they abruptly work. Schaeffer, Miranda, and Koyejo, in "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?" (2023), showed that many such jumps are an artifact of metric choice. With a binary metric (task solved entirely or not, exact match), the curve looks discontinuous; replace the metric with a continuous, smoothed one, and the "sudden" ability turns into the same smooth power-law growth. This is a double-edged conclusion. On one hand, it reassures: fewer magic surprises. On the other, it alarms: if the sharpness is illusory, then dangerous abilities may accumulate unnoticed, below the threshold of our crude benchmarks, rather than announcing themselves with a spectacular jump.

The third — the semantic objection. A high benchmark score does not equal understanding. Loss measures token prediction, not the presence of a world model; part of the rise in benchmark scores is explained by test leakage into the training set and by "passing the exam" through surface correlations. You can extend the loss curve confidently, but extrapolating cognitive abilities from it is already an act of faith.

The fourth — the economic and physical objection. A power law with a tiny exponent means an exponentially rising price for a linear improvement. At some point the next order of magnitude in compute runs up against cost, against energy, against chip manufacturing. The law does not promise that money and electricity are infinite.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is neither "scale solves everything" nor "scale has run out of steam," but something more boring and useful. Scaling laws should be taken as a tool for forecasting and planning, not as metaphysics. Labs already use them literally: they train a series of small models, fit the curve, extrapolate — and only then spend hundreds of millions on one large run, knowing in advance roughly what loss they will get. For safety the same logic yields "advance evaluation": if you can predict the next generation's capabilities from the curve, then both red-teaming and dangerous-capability tests must be prepared before training, not after release.

Beyond that lies a fork. If smoothness holds — compute governance becomes the main lever, and the predictability of takeoff becomes our only advantage against takeoff itself. If we hit the data wall or a plateau — the center of gravity shifts back to ideas: reasoning models, test-time compute, synthetic data, agentic loops. Tellingly, the new scaling axis — test-time compute, spent when the model answers and not only when it is trained — opened up precisely when the training curve began to run out of breath. That is, the bitter lesson updates itself: scale wins, but differently in different years. For the bestiary this is a chameleon-beast: it can be neither ignored nor worshipped. The right posture is to keep the ruler at hand but remember that all straight lines on logarithmic axes sooner or later break somewhere.
