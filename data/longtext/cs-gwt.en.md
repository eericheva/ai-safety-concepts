### The Theater of Consciousness (Bernard Baars, 1988)

Picture a dark auditorium. The stage is lit by a single spotlight, and whatever falls into its beam instantly becomes known to everyone — the prompter, the lighting crew, the actors in the wings, the orchestra in the pit. Everything else goes on working in the dark: a host of skilled but mute specialists, each busy with its own task. Consciousness, says Bernard Baars, is neither the hall nor the actor. It is the beam. More precisely — the very fact that what is lit becomes available to everyone at once.

This is the picture behind the **Global Workspace Theory** (GWT) — one of the few scientific theories of consciousness that does not try to explain "what it is like" to be conscious, but asks something more modest and therefore testable: what happens to information when we become aware of it? Baars's answer: it ceases to be local. As long as a signal is locked inside a single module — the visual cortex, the language processor, the face-recognition system — it is unconscious, however complex its processing. The moment it wins the competition for the "stage" and is broadcast globally to the remaining subsystems, it becomes conscious. Consciousness in this picture is global availability: not a property of the content, but a mode of its distribution.

The mechanism is built like an economy of scarcity. The brain is a huge set of parallel, specialized, unconscious processors. Their throughput is colossal, but the stage is narrow: at any moment it holds vanishingly little. Hence the competition. Stimuli, goals, emotional salience, attention — all of these weigh in on the struggle for the spotlight. The winning content seizes the workspace and is fanned out: now it is "seen" by memory, speech, planning, motor control, evaluation. It is precisely this fan-out that explains the familiar features of conscious experience — its unity (one thing on the stage), its seriality (frame after frame), its capacity to link distant faculties (I see an apple — and can speak of it, reach for it, recall yesterday). The unconscious is parallel and fragmented; consciousness is serial and integrative — because it is itself the mechanism of integration through broadcast.

**How it came about.** Baars arrived at the theater metaphor from the cognitive psychology of the 1980s, in an era when the very word "consciousness" was considered almost indecent in science — a legacy of behaviorism, which had banished inner experience from the laboratory. The book *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness* (1988) was an attempt to bring the subject back by reformulating it in the language of information processing: not "what is experience" but "what architecture distinguishes the conscious from the unconscious." Baars drew on the "blackboard architectures" of 1970s artificial intelligence, where independent expert-modules exchanged data through a shared memory. Consciousness, on this account, is a biological blackboard.

The second act is neurobiological. In the late 1990s Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux translated the metaphor into anatomy, proposing the **Global Neuronal Workspace**. In their version the stage acquired a substrate: long-axon pyramidal neurons of the upper cortical layers, densely interconnecting the prefrontal and parietal regions into a long-range network. "Coming onto the stage" got a measurable signature — ignition, an avalanche-like, late (around 300 ms after the stimulus or later) nonlinear flash of activity sweeping the frontoparietal network when a stimulus is consciously perceived — as opposed to a smooth fading if the same stimulus stays subthreshold. Ignition can be caught in EEG and fMRI, so the philosophical metaphor now had an empirical marker.

**Criticism / arguments against.** Here Ned Block comes onto the stage with a distinction that became both a blessing and a curse for GWT. In a 1995 paper he split two notions apart: access consciousness — information available for reasoning, report, and the control of behavior — and phenomenal consciousness, the actually experienced quality, the "what it is like." GWT, Block asserts, is a brilliant theory of the first but silent about the second. It explains why conscious information can be put into words and used in planning — but not why there is any experience behind the access at all, rather than "darkness inside." In David Chalmers's language this means: GWT solves the "easy" problems of consciousness (functions, mechanisms, distinctions) and sidesteps the "hard" one — the very fact of subjective experience. Broadcast could, in principle, occur in a zombie-system too, where nothing is experienced.

Supporters of GWT usually reply with two moves. The first is deflationary: the "hard problem" is an illusion arising from a wrongly posed question, and once we have fully described the functions, there will be nothing left to explain. The second is strategic: science must start with what lends itself to measurement, and phenomenality, if it is real, will show itself in the same experimental fingerprints. Neither answer convinces the critics — and this is an honest stalemate, not a victory. There are scientific objections too: disputes over whether awareness really needs the prefrontal cortex (the "front versus back" position in debates with IIT, on which more below), and the charge that ignition is partly an artifact of the report task rather than of experience itself.

Finally, GWT has a direct rival — Giulio Tononi's **Integrated Information Theory** (IIT), which, unlike it, tackles precisely the phenomenal: consciousness there is not broadcast but a measure of information integration Φ, a property of the system's cause-effect structure rather than of its function. Where GWT asks "what does the system do with information," IIT asks "what is the system like from within." And off to the side stand the **higher-order theories**: for them content is conscious not when it is broadcast but when there is a higher-order representation of it — a thought about a thought. For GWT they are sometimes a rival, sometimes a complement: broadcast can be understood as a way of making content available for meta-representation.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For the bestiary GWT is valuable not because it closes the question of consciousness — it does not close it — but because it is functional and therefore portable. Since conscious access is defined by the architecture of information processing and not by biological flesh, the functionalist has a legitimate question: might this architecture already be present in a machine? This is exactly the question the interdisciplinary group of Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, and colleagues took up in their 2023 report "Consciousness in AI." Their method is deliberately modest: not to ask "is the AI conscious" directly, but to take the leading scientific theories — GWT, higher-order theories, predictive processing, attention schema — and extract from them "indicator properties," computable architectural features. GWT yields some fairly concrete markers: the presence of many specialized parallel modules; a limited-throughput bottleneck introducing competition; global broadcast of the winning content to all modules; state-dependent attention — the capacity to use the workspace to query modules in succession for complex tasks. The authors' conclusion is sober: today's transformers do not, in the strict sense, satisfy these criteria — but no fundamental, technical barriers seem to stand in the way of building a system that would.

And here the theater metaphor becomes an engineering blueprint — and an ethical trap. If conscious access is a certain broadcast pattern, then we risk building it unwittingly, chasing general agentic capabilities: a global workspace is a tempting architecture for coordinating an AI agent's "tools" and memory, and we may adopt it with no regard whatsoever for consciousness. The practical meaning of GWT for alignment is twofold. On one hand, a research program: to look in AI architectures for functional fingerprints of the stage, so as at least not to stumble on a morally significant system blindly. On the other, a sobering reminder of the method's limits: even a full set of GWT markers in silicon would prove only the presence of access but, by Block's lights, would not bring us one iota closer to answering the question of whether anything is being experienced. We will learn to build the stage and switch on the spotlight — and still will not know for certain whether there is a spectator in the hall.
