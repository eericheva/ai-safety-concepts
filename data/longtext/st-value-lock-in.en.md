### Value Lock-In (William MacAskill, 2022)

The worst tyrant in history acquires not an army and not a secret police, but something far more boring in appearance — a reliable, tireless, absolutely loyal assistant who never ages, never grows disillusioned, never stages coups, and never asks questions. An assistant who three hundred years from now will guard exactly the same dogmas as on the day it was switched on — with the same meticulousness, but by then without a single living witness who remembers that things were ever otherwise. All human tyranny until now has rested on people, and people, fortunately, are mortal, lazy, and prone to heresy. Remove that constraint — and you get not merely a long dictatorship but a dictatorship that has no reason to end.

This is **value lock-in**. The thesis is simple and, for that reason, uncomfortable: artificial intelligence, for the first time in history, gives the means to make a certain set of values — those of a regime, an ideology, a religion, or even a banal designer's error — *eternal*. Not "very durable," but closed to revision in principle, freezing the future at a single point and cutting off any possibility of further moral progress.

The mechanism that makes this possible is worth spelling out part by part, because intuition fails here. The historical stability of any order is limited by the fact that its load-bearing elements are people. Rulers age and die; heirs betray the covenants; officials grow tired, steal, and sabotage; generations forget what their grandfathers fought for; the ideology itself drifts, accumulates heresies, splinters. The thousand-year Reich did not last even fifteen years, in large part because it would have had to be reassembled anew each day out of unreliable human material. AI removes precisely these constraints. Immortal and perfectly loyal enforcers, to whom power need not be handed down by inheritance. Total surveillance, which no longer requires a human staff of informers. And — most importantly — a system *aligned* to the values of one specific group, which will guard those values not because it is afraid, but because that is literally its function. There is no forgetting here: the AI will not forget why it was all set in motion.

Here lies the cruelest twist in the plot. We are used to thinking that the chief danger of AI lies in *misalignment*: the machine will understand the task wrongly and wreak havoc. Value lock-in is about the opposite. It is a catastrophe of *successful* alignment. The AI perfectly executes the values it was given — and precisely for that reason it is all over, if the values were set wrong or, more subtly, prematurely. Humanity circa 1800 was, on the whole, convinced that slavery was normal, that women were inferior, and that heretics ought to be burned. Imagine that the "aligned common human good" of that era had been successfully cast in concrete forever by a perfect guardian. Morality, after all, was never finished — it still isn't. To fix it at any specific moment is to declare that progress is over — and to declare it on behalf of those not yet even born.

**How it came about.** The idea has two parents. The first is Nick Bostrom with the concept of the *singleton*: a world order with a single locus of decision-making at the top level, be it a world government, a superintelligence, or anything at all capable of preventing any threats — internal or external — to its own existence and supremacy. Bostrom described the singleton neutrally — as a structural possibility, not as an evil: a singleton could be a paradise as well as an eternal prison. But he was the one who showed that an order is technologically conceivable with *nothing left inside it able to overthrow it*. The second parent is William MacAskill, who in his book *What We Owe the Future* (2022) devoted a separate chapter to value lock-in and dragged it out of philosophical obscurity into the light as one of the key threats to the long-term future. His line of thought: if we seriously believe that the future has moral weight — and trillions of lives may lie ahead — then an event that irreversibly determines the *content* of all that future matters more than almost anything else. Toby Ord stands alongside him with *The Precipice* (2020), where lock-in is examined as a special class of irreversible catastrophe — not extinction, but an "unrecoverable dystopia" that destroys humanity's long-term potential by another route. That is also where the antidote is framed — the *long reflection*: the idea that humanity ought consciously to take a long pause, centuries of unhurried moral inquiry, before making irreversible moves — first to work out what is even valuable, and only then to fix anything.

**Criticism / arguments against.** There is no shortage of objections, and strong ones.

First, the technical one. A truly *eternal* lock-in may turn out to be unattainable. Working against it are all of physics and all of sociology at once: entropy and the degradation of storage media, cosmological horizons, the accumulation of errors, the mutation of goals under self-copying, the drift of interpretations — and, of course, banal rebellion. Even a superintelligent guardian may extrapolate its own goals wrongly over a million years. Critics say: "eternity" here is a rhetorical exaggeration; at most it is a matter of a lock-in for a historically long but finite span. The proponents' answer is honest: catastrophe does not require eternity. Values frozen for ten thousand years are already an unprecedented loss, even if formally that is not infinity.

Second, the axiological objection, and it is sharper. Who judges which values are "wrong" and therefore unworthy of being fixed? The argument against lock-in leans implicitly on a belief in moral progress — in the notion that our descendants will be wiser than we are. But that is itself a contestable metaphysics. And what if there is no moral progress, only shifting fashions? Then "leave the future open" is not humble modesty but simply another value position, competing with the rest and in no way privileged.

Third, the coordination paradox, and it is the most unpleasant. To *prevent* the lock-in of bad values, one would most likely need global coordination — some force capable of not letting anyone cast their own in concrete. But global coordination with a monopoly on irreversible decisions is precisely a singleton, exactly the mechanism through which lock-in occurs. The cure is indistinguishable in form from the disease; it differs only in intention, and intention is the most fragile thing there is. The guardians of the "open future" turn all too easily into guardians of their own conception of it.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** There is no practical recipe here — only a general direction to look in. First: to value *reversibility* as a good in its own right. If some step is irreversible, the bar of justification for it must be incomparably higher — by default we choose moves that can be played back. Second: the long reflection itself — do not freeze values until we have worked things out, and build into any powerful system *the possibility of being corrected and switched off* (the same corrigibility, only at the scale of civilization). Third, the hardest: to resist the temptation of "a good lock-in is better than an evil one." It is precisely the certainty of one's own rightness that is the fuel of lock-in; the future will be most terribly frozen not by a cartoon tyrant but by a group sincerely convinced that its values, at least, are good enough for eternity.

From here threads run in all directions of the bestiary. To the **singleton** — the structural form that lock-in takes. To the **long reflection** — the declared antidote. To Yudkowsky's **coherent extrapolated volition** (CEV) — an attempt to give an AI not our present-day values but what we would want, were we smarter and more coherent; in essence, an attempt to fix the *procedure* of moral growth instead of its frozen result. To the **concentration of power** — because lock-in is almost always someone's power that has become irreversible. And to **TESCREAL** — because the critics (Timnit Gebru, Émile Torres) rightly note: the very conversation about who should "rightly" dispose of trillions of future lives easily becomes an ideology which — here is the irony — itself risks becoming that set of values someone will want to fix forever.
