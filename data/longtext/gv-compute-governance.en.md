### The Silicon Bridle (Sastry, Heim et al., OpenAI/GovAI, 2024)

Suppose you want to ban explosives. Regulating gunpowder is pointless — the recipe has been known for a good thousand years and fits in three lines. Regulating the intent to blow something up is impossible — it lives inside someone's head. But regulating purified weapons-grade uranium works surprisingly well: its centrifuges hum in known places, enrichment is measurable in percentages, and the chain itself — from ore to cascade — passes through a handful of points on the planet. Not because a bomb matters more than gunpowder, but because uranium is a **governable node**.

Compute governance proceeds from the idea that artificial intelligence has such a node too, and that it is hardware, not algorithms or data. Accelerator chips, GPUs, data centers — what in the jargon is called "compute." The thesis, minted in the big 2024 OpenAI and GovAI (the Centre for the Governance of AI in Oxford) paper, is this: of the three ingredients of training — algorithms, data, compute — compute has four properties that make it an ideal fulcrum for policy levers.

It is **detectable**: a large AI supercomputer is not a flash drive in a pocket but a facility that can consume tens of megawatts, with its own substation and cooling towers, visible from a satellite and in the electricity bills. It is **excludable**: access to cutting-edge chips can be physically restricted — they can be withheld or not shipped, and a cloud provider's license can be revoked. It is **quantifiable**: compute is counted in FLOP — floating-point operations — and it is the only one of the three ingredients that has an honest ruler. And finally, its production is **monstrously concentrated**: the world's extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines are made essentially by a single company, ASML in the Netherlands; the best chips are printed essentially by a single company, TSMC in Taiwan; the top accelerators are designed essentially by a single company, NVIDIA. All the might of planetary AI passes through a bottleneck of a few companies on a few patches of the globe.

Compare with an algorithm. The idea of the transformer is set out in a public paper; it cannot be recalled or "not quite shipped." The weights of an open model, once posted, are copied endlessly and for free. Data is smeared across the whole internet. None of it can be detected, excluded, or properly measured. That is why, proponents of the approach say, if we want any handle at all on frontier AI — and not only good intentions — the handle is attached to the hardware.

From the four properties grow concrete levers. The first is **export control**: since October 2022 the United States has consistently restricted the supply of top accelerators and the equipment for their manufacture to China, taking advantage of this supply-chain concentration. The second is **training-volume thresholds**: the 2023 US executive order required developers to report on models trained beyond 10²⁶ operations; the European AI Act introduced a presumption of "systemic risk" for models above 10²⁵ FLOP. For the first time in history, the FLOP became a legal quantity. The third and fourth are on the way: **chip tracking** (down to hardware verification mechanisms right in the silicon) and **KYC for clouds** — "know your customer," so that a giant training run cannot be quietly rented through a front company.

**How it came about.** The thought that hardware is a lever ripened at the junction of strategic studies and the economics of scaling. Georgetown's CSET think tank had already laid out, in a 2020 report by Saif Khan and Alexander Mann, *what* AI chips are and why their supply chain is a strategic asset and not just a market. In parallel, scaling laws made compute not a backdrop but the main axis of progress: a model's capabilities grow predictably with the compute invested. Once compute became a proxy for capability, people wanted to govern it. Lennart Heim at GovAI turned the intuition into a research program. Yonadav Shavit showed in 2023 how one could in principle *verify* compliance with training rules through compute monitoring, without cracking open the models themselves — a work with the telling title "What Does It Take to Catch a Chinchilla?" The culmination was the 2024 synthesis "Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence" — nearly two dozen authors from OpenAI, GovAI, Cambridge, Georgetown, with even Yoshua Bengio among the signatories — where the four properties were brought together and canonized. Around the same time, compute governance migrated from preprints into the texts of laws.

**Criticism / arguments against.** First, **smuggling and circumvention**: chips are a compact, golden commodity, and after each round of controls gray channels surface: re-export through third countries, data centers "abroad but ours." The bottleneck is narrow but not airtight. Second, and this is a blow to the very heart — **algorithmic efficiency erodes the thresholds**. Research by Epoch AI on algorithmic progress in language models shows that ever less compute is required to reach a fixed quality — by their estimates, the compute needed for that quality falls roughly by half every eight months. That means what today requires 10²⁶ operations could eventually be done with 10²⁵, falling below a fixed reporting threshold and potentially becoming harder to detect. A threshold nailed fast to a FLOP count rusts right there on the wall.

Third — **concentration of power in the regulator's hands**: a tool convenient for choking others' training runs turns just as conveniently into a valve at which one player sits — a state or a coalition — and decides who in the world is allowed to build strong AI at all. The cure for the race risks becoming its prize. Fourth — **dual use**: the same GPUs run weather forecasts, fold proteins, and render films; to hack away at the hardware is to strike at everything useful that lives on it too. And fifth — **damage to open research**: thresholds and licenses fall heaviest not on the giants with an army of lawyers but on universities, startups, and open-source researchers, who lack the resources to comply — and the picture of "safety through concentration" becomes self-fulfilling.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** A reasonable answer to "the thresholds rust" is not to throw away the ruler but to make it living: index the threshold to algorithmic progress, supplement gross compute with dangerous capability evals, so that what is regulated is not an abstract FLOP but what the model *can do*. Here the bridle joins with **scaling policies** (RSPs — responsible scaling policies — and if-then commitments): "if a model at such-and-such compute shows such-and-such capability — then such-and-such measures." Compute governance gives these rules what they desperately lack — a measurable and verifiable trigger, rather than a lab's word of honor. To "concentration of power" the answer is multilaterality and technical guarantees: not a single valve in one pair of hands, but verifiable hardware mechanisms to check compliance, combined with political limits on who controls access to compute.

On the Bestiary's map the "Silicon Bridle" is a rare beast that is not about a model's innards but about the physical world around it. It softens the **AI race** (slowing the dash toward the cliff) and reduces **structural risk** (giving a point at which one can intervene at all), yet it itself gives rise to the threat of **power concentration**. Compute is neither a silver bullet nor a cure-all for alignment; it cannot align values inside a model at all. It does one thing, but an important one: it turns AI governance from an argument about the invisible into work with the tangible. As long as progress has a narrow throat of silicon, humanity has something to hold on to. The only question is whether we will hold the bridle — or it will hold us.
