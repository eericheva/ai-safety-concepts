### Counterfactual mugging (Vladimir Nesov, 2009)

Imagine that Omega approaches you — a being that is never wrong in its predictions, and you know this for certain. Omega tells you it has just tossed a fair coin. It came up tails. And here is its request: "Give me a hundred dollars." In return — nothing. Nothing at all. What is more, Omega honestly explains the terms of the game you have already lost: had it come up heads, it would have handed you ten thousand dollars — but only if it had predicted in advance that, on tails, you would give it a hundred. Heads did not come up. The ten thousand is not in the cards and never was in this particular branch of reality. The coin lies tails up. Omega holds out its hand.

Do you pay up?

The first, healthy reaction: what on earth for? There is nothing to pay for. The reward was played out in a world that does not exist — the one where heads came up. And in this world I am simply parting with a hundred in exchange for air. This is exactly what the two most venerable theories of decision-making say. Causal decision theory looks at the cause-and-effect arrows: my payment now influences nothing, the coin fell long ago, it generates no future winnings — so I do not pay. Evidential decision theory looks at what my decision is evidence of: by paying, I learn nothing good about myself regarding the already-settled draw — the reward in the heads branch does not depend on my present gesture and is useless as evidence. Both say: keep your wallet shut.

And now the uncomfortable part. Imagine that a second *before* the coin toss you had been asked: "Do you want to be the type of agent who, on tails, pays?" Of course, yes. Such an agent, on average — that is, across half heads and half tails — walks away with an expected gain of 0.5 × 10000 − 0.5 × 100 = 4,950 dollars. An agent who does not pay walks away with zero: Omega predicts its refusal and does not hand out the reward in the heads branch. Before the toss the preference is unambiguous: I want to be a payer. But you cannot merely pretend to be a payer — Omega sees straight through you. To be a payer means actually giving the hundred at the very moment tails came up and paying became "pointless." That's the whole trick: what's reasonable before you observe and what's reasonable after have split sharply.

The counterfactual mugging is not a puzzle about greed. It is a test of *updatelessness*: the readiness to hold to a policy chosen before you saw anything, even after what you saw has made that policy locally disadvantageous. Updateless Decision Theory (UDT) answers "pay" — because it evaluates not the individual act from the vantage of "already tails" but the whole policy from the vantage of "before the toss," and then carries out that predetermined-optimal policy literally, without recomputing it after the fact in light of the fresh news about the coin.

**How it came about.** The term and the thought experiment itself were introduced by Vladimir Nesov in a post on LessWrong in March 2009 — a short note that instantly became a touchstone for a whole community. The context was heated. Not long before, Eliezer Yudkowsky had been analyzing Newcomb's paradox and insisting that "rational agents should WIN," rather than cling to a ritual fidelity to causal reasoning that stably leaves its bearer with less money. In that same 2009, Wei Dai posted a sketch of Updateless Decision Theory — a theory that precisely refuses to "update" on observations when choosing an action, and instead optimizes the mapping "observations → actions" as a whole. The counterfactual mugging turned out to be the ideal stress test for UDT: it separates it from all predecessor theories more cleanly than Newcomb itself. In classic Newcomb the "correct" move (take one box) is rewarded in the very world where you make it. Here the reward lies in a counterfactual branch that will no longer be — and so theories tied either to causality or to evidence about the current world are obliged to balk, while UDT is obliged to pay. Thus the mugging took its place in the menagerie alongside Newcomb's paradox, Parfit's hitchhiker, and the "transparent" Newcomb, where both boxes are glass and you see the contents in advance.

**Criticism / arguments against.** There are plenty of objections, and they are not stupid. The first — "dynamic inconsistency in reverse": UDT asks me, at the moment of tails, to perform an act that right now, by everything I know, only harms me. The causal camp's critics say: rationality evaluates an action in the circumstances in which it is performed, not in hypothetical ones; to bind yourself with a promise given to a nonexistent past is not rationality but a fetishization of commitments. The second objection strikes at realism: the whole experiment rests on an infallible predictor who is, moreover, honest and symmetric. Real "Omegas" err, lie, and play in their own favor; let in a single drop of uncertainty into the prediction and the elegant "pay" dissolves into a dull calculation of probabilities. The third, a subtle one: the problem of the privileged reference point. UDT counts the policy optimal "before the toss," but why is that temporal point the canonical one? Rewind farther still — to before you even knew of Omega's existence — and situations appear (for example, where Omega mugs only those who are in principle willing to pay) in which it is predetermined-optimal to be a *non-payer*. Where to set the zero mark of the prior is a question to which the theory has no clean answer, and this is not pedantry: what the theory prescribes depends on it. Finally, the existential one: the intuition of many philosophers simply refuses to call the voluntary handing over of a hundred for nothing a "win," however beautifully the expectation over the ensemble of worlds comes out.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is not to train yourself to hand money to imaginary entities. It is that the counterfactual mugging exposes a mechanism without which credible commitment does not work — and credible commitment underlies everything, from nuclear deterrence to bargaining to AI safety. An agent whose future selves always revise yesterday's commitments the moment fulfilling them becomes disadvantageous is an agent whose promise cannot be trusted, which means an agent with whom you cannot strike a beneficial deal. Parfit's hitchhiker dies in the desert precisely because the telepathic driver sees that the rescued one will not pay. The ability to be a payer on tails is the very ability that lets you reach the city alive. For AI builders there is a double signal here. On one hand, updateless steadfastness is a trait we may *want* to see in systems, so that they keep their word and do not yield to the blackmail of "it's too late now, pay." On the other, the same mechanism makes a system vulnerable to mugging and, in general, to anyone who can present convincing counterfactual threats; hence a live line of research on how to tell "healthy" commitments from capitulation to an extortionist. Technically, the mugging became one of the seeds from which grew the Functional Decision Theory (FDT) of Yudkowsky and Soares — an attempt to describe formally an agent that chooses neither an action nor a policy-over-time, but the output of its own decision function at once in all the places where it is instantiated.

The practical meaning for the reader is simpler and harsher. Every time you break a promise because "the situation has changed and keeping it is no longer worthwhile," you are telling all future Omegas — partners, employers, opponents — what type of agent you are. And they remember it before they toss their coins.
