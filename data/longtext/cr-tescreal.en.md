### TESCREAL (Timnit Gebru and Émile P. Torres, 2023–2024)

The acronym is read as "tescreal" and unfolds into seven names: **T**ranshumanism, **E**xtropianism, **S**ingularitarianism, **C**osmism, **R**ationalism, **E**ffective **A**ltruism, **L**ongtermism. Seven currents that Timnit Gebru and Émile Torres propose to regard not as a random assortment but as a single "bundle" — an interwoven tangle of ideas with shared people, shared money, and, as the authors claim, a shared genealogy.

The critics' thesis is blunt and deliberately provocative. TESCREAL is, in their words, a secular eschatology: a system of beliefs about a coming salvation — except that instead of the Kingdom of Heaven, what awaits is the colonization of the galaxy, digital immortality, and trillions of happy digital minds in the distant future. This faith, say Gebru and Torres, is the real engine of the race for AGI. It gives the major labs a moral justification to build a "god out of the machine" at any cost — because at stake, supposedly, is an infinite utopia, and next to infinity any present-day harm zeroes out. And, crucially for the authors, they trace the genealogy of this bundle to the eugenics of the twentieth century: to the idea that humanity can and must be "improved" by weeding out the undesirable.

**Significance for the discourse.** Before TESCREAL, AI critics had no common word for the sense that behind various signboards — foundations, forums, manifestos — one face was showing through. The acronym gave them that word. It turned diffuse irritation into a recognizable label, around which camps instantly formed. For some, "tescreal" became a precise diagnosis of Silicon Valley ideology; for others, a lazy insult, a conspiratorial tangle into which incompatible things had been dumped. The term works as a litmus test: how someone reacts to it shows at once where they stand in the debate over the future of AI.

**How it came about.** The term's roots lie in Émile Torres's personal trajectory. A philosopher who for years wrote from within the longtermist tradition, Torres gradually grew disillusioned with it and became one of its most caustic critics. Torres introduced the abbreviation "TESCREAL" in 2023 — by their own account, in a then-unpublished paper co-written with Timnit Gebru — to name in a single word the family of worldviews they had once studied from the inside. Gebru — a researcher fired from Google after a conflict over a paper on the risks of large language models, and founder of the DAIR institute — brought to that frame her own line of criticism: attention to the real, present-day harm of algorithms. Their joint work crystallized into the paper **"The TESCREAL bundle: Eugenics and the promise of utopia through artificial general intelligence,"** published in the peer-reviewed journal *First Monday* in April 2024. By that point the term was already alive on Twitter, in talks, and in Gebru's lectures; the paper merely gave it academic form and an extended argument.

On the authors' account, the bundle unwinds chronologically like this. Twentieth-century transhumanism (with its dream of overcoming biological limits) gives rise to the Californian extropianism of the 1990s — an optimistic faith in boundless progress. From the idea of a technological singularity grows singularitarianism. Cosmism adds a galactic scale. The rationalist community around LessWrong turns all this into a style of thinking; effective altruism makes it a practice of handing out money and career advice; longtermism supplies an ethical justification: future generations are so numerous that it is precisely their interests that should determine our actions today.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

First, uniting seven different currents into a single "bundle" coarsens the picture. Between an effective altruist handing out anti-malaria nets in Africa and an e/acc accelerationist demanding that AGI be built as fast as possible there is a gulf — they are often outright opponents. Many effective altruists regard AI risk with alarm rather than delight, and call for slowing down rather than speeding up. To fold them into a single acronym with accelerationist transhumanists is to lose precisely those distinctions that matter for understanding the dispute. The bundle glues together allies and enemies on the basis of family resemblance, and resemblance is not identity.

Second, the frame has a built-in logical weakness — **the genetic argument**. To point out that an idea grew historically from a dubious root (eugenics) is not the same as showing that the idea is false. The claim "caring about distant future generations is morally important" does not become untrue because some of its proponents held unpleasant views on "human improvement." The origin of an idea and its truth are different questions; conflating them is what logic calls the genetic fallacy. In an abstract for a UCL talk, [Connor McGlynn](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/mathematical-physical-sciences/events/2026/mar/democracy-technocracy-and-ai-safety) raised this objection to the TESCREAL account, arguing that it treats origins as determining truth. But Gebru and Torres also argue from contemporary practices and harms, so their case cannot be reduced to genealogy alone.

Third, **the eugenics thesis itself is contestable**. Yes, between early transhumanism and the eugenic ideas of the twentieth century there are historical threads, and individual figures really did write things that today sound repellent. But to apply the label "eugenics" to the whole field — from the alignment researcher to the altruist volunteer — is to ascribe to millions of people views they do not hold and that most would flatly reject. The accusation is strong, emotionally charged, and precisely for that reason demands more targeted evidence than a demonstration of common ancestors.

Fourth, "TESCREAL" is too convenient as a **rhetorical label**. A single word with which to disqualify an opponent without engaging their concrete argument: call them a "tescreal," and you need not answer on the merits. This mirrors the very danger the critics see in the bundle itself. The label economizes on thinking precisely where thinking is needed.

And yet the frame retains a solid core that the objections do not cancel. The social and financial ties among these communities are real and well documented: the same foundations, the same people on boards of directors, the same circuit of conferences. Another observation keeps its force whatever the outcome of the disputes over eugenics: the rhetoric of "saving humanity in the distant future" conveniently diverts attention and resources from quite measurable harm here and now — from model bias, the exploitation of data labelers, the concentration of power. The best thing in TESCREAL is not the genealogy but the question: who benefits from our looking a thousand years ahead rather than at our own feet?

**Legacy and connections.** TESCREAL quickly became a reference point for the critical wing in the conversation about AI and ties logically into neighboring entries of the Bestiary.

With **longtermism** the connection is direct: it is the letter L, and the criticism strikes it hardest. Even before the *First Monday* paper, Torres, in the essay "Against Longtermism" (*Aeon*, 2021), called longtermism "quite possibly the most dangerous secular belief system in the world today" — TESCREAL extended that thrust from a single current to the whole family.

With the **stochastic parrots** the bundle is linked through Gebru herself. The famous "parrots" paper and the TESCREAL frame are two sides of one program: don't let grand promises about AGI obscure what the models are already doing to real people.

With **Roko's basilisk** the connection is that of a symptom. The thought experiment, born on LessWrong (the letter R, rationalism), is for TESCREAL's critics a perfect illustration of how eschatological thinking gives rise to almost theological constructions: a future superintelligence as a punishing deity.

With **decolonial ethics** the bundle resonates directly: critics read the language of "cosmic expansion" and the colonization of the galaxy as a transfer of the imperial logic of resource seizure onto a cosmic scale.

Finally, TESCREAL maps curiously onto the axis of **e/acc versus doomers**. At first glance the effective accelerationists (build AGI faster) and the doomers (AGI will kill us) are sworn enemies. But Gebru and Torres's frame offers a provocation: both camps believe in one and the same grand future and in the decisive role of superintelligence — they argue only over the sign, whether it is salvation or ruin. And this cosmic wager itself, Gebru and Torres say, distracts from earthly problems. The utopian and the apocalyptic turn out to be two variants of one faith.
