### Qualia and the Explanatory Gap (Nagel 1974; Levine 1983)

Take a cup of coffee. A chemist will tell you about chlorogenic acids and furanols; a neurophysiologist, about how molecules bind to receptors on the tongue, how the signal runs along the cranial nerves, flares up in the insular lobe, is colored by the amygdala. A complete, exhaustive physical description, without a single lacuna. And now — a sip. This bitterish-warm, like-nothing-else thing that is happening right now in your mouth and nowhere else in the universe. The question with which the whole conversation begins: where, in the description of molecules and impulses, is **this** hidden? Not the behavior, not the report "the coffee is bitter," but the experience of bitterness itself — what it tastes like.

Philosophers call such things **qualia** (singular: quale): the subjective qualitative characters of experience. The redness of red, not the wavelength of 700 nm. The painfulness of pain, not the activity of C-fibers. The term was put into circulation by the American logician Clarence Irving Lewis as far back as 1929 in "Mind and the World-Order," but qualia became a thorn in the philosophy of mind only half a century later.

**What it is like to be someone.** In 1974 Thomas Nagel publishes an essay with a deceptively childish title — "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" A bat orients itself by echolocation: it emits ultrasound, catches the echo, builds from it a picture of the world. We can study its sonar down to the last neuron — and still we will not know **what it is like** to perceive the world as a stream of reflected squeaks. We will imagine what it would be like for us, waving our arms in the dark; but what we need to know is not what it would be like for a human pretending to be a bat, but what it is like for the bat itself. And here the imagination runs up against a wall. Nagel formulates what has become almost the definition of consciousness: an organism is conscious if and only if there is "something that it is like to be that organism." The subjective is what is accessible from a single point of view, and any objective, "from-everywhere-and-nowhere" scientific description by its nature loses that point of view.

**The gap.** Nine years later Joseph Levine gives the phenomenon a name that stuck for good. In the paper "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap" (1983) he separates two questions. The metaphysical: are pain and C-fiber activity really **identical**? And the epistemic: even if they are identical — does one **explain** the other? Compare this with his own example, "heat is the motion of molecules": knowing about the kinetics of molecules, we understand why heated things expand, why ice melts, why the mercury climbs in a thermometer — the identity explains all the behavior of heat without remainder. But "pain is the firing of C-fibers" gives no such satisfaction: even granting the identity, we do not understand **why** precisely this neural activity feels like this, and not otherwise, or like nothing at all. The formula leaves a gap into which intuition falls. This is the explanatory gap — a crevice not necessarily between two things in the world, but certainly between a physical explanation and what it was supposed to explain.

Later David Chalmers will repackage this as the **hard problem of consciousness** (1995): the easy problems — how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information, controls behavior — are in principle solved by functional description; the hard one — why all this is accompanied by experience — remains even when the easy ones are solved. Two famous thought experiments stand beside it. Frank Jackson's **Mary's room**: a brilliant neuroscientist, raised in a black-and-white room, knows everything physical about color — and yet, on stepping out for the first time and seeing red, she learns something new: what it is like. So, Jackson concludes, physical knowledge is incomplete. And the **inverted spectrum**: what if what you call "red" looks to you the way grass looks to me — and we will never catch the divergence, because both of us call the tomato red? The function is the same, the qualia diverge.

**How it came about.** The problem has a long lineage. As early as the "Monadology" (1714), Leibniz proposed imagining a thinking machine enlarged so that one could step inside it as into a mill: walk among the gears — and nowhere will you find perception. Du Bois-Reymond in 1872 threw down his famous "ignorabimus" — we shall never know. But the problem crystallized in its present form in the second half of the twentieth century, as a reaction to the triumph of physicalism and functionalism. When the philosophy of mind became convinced that the mind is a program on neural hardware, qualia turned out to be the sediment that does not dissolve in the functional soup. Nagel (1974), Jackson (1982), Levine (1983), Chalmers (1995–1996) — over two decades a corpus of "arguments from consciousness" against materialism took shape. Tellingly, all of them are conceptual, not empirical: they strike not with a fact but with the intuition of inconceivability.

**Criticism / arguments against.** And here the opposing camp enters, for which the whole conversation is a grandiose misunderstanding.

*Eliminativism.* Daniel Dennett, in "Quining Qualia" (1988), proposes simply subtracting qualia from the inventory of the world. "Quine" here is a verb he coined in honor of Quine: to deny resolutely the existence or importance of something real or significant. Dennett takes apart the intuitive properties of qualia — ineffability, privacy, immediate givenness — and through a series of cunning scenarios (the same inverted spectrum, coffee tasters whose tastes have "gone off") shows that as soon as you try to grasp qualia operationally, they blur. There is no fact distinguishing "my qualia have changed" from "my judgments about them have changed." Qualia, he concludes, are a theoretical fiction, an artifact of the Cartesian theater, where a spectator-Self supposedly sits admiring the show. There is no theater.

*Illusionism.* Keith Frankish ("Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness," 2016) softens and sharpens the move. He does not deny that it **seems** to us there are phenomenal qualia — he makes this very seeming the object of explanation. The hard problem is replaced by the "illusion problem": why does a brain built this way systematically represent its own states as possessing an ineffable qualitative character that in fact they do not have? This is a task for science, not an eternal mystery.

*Representationalism.* Michael Tye, Fred Dretske, and others: qualia are not an add-on to content but the representational content of experience itself. Blueness is experienced as a property of the sky, not as a patch in the head; grasp what experience represents and how it represents it, and you grasp everything. The gap is an illusion of "introspective projection."

The general thrust of the critics: the explanatory gap is epistemic, not ontological. It speaks of the limits of our **concepts** (we have separate phenomenal and physical notions of one and the same thing), not of a superfluous substance in the world. From "I cannot conceive how" it does not follow that "there is an irreducible how in the world." Nagel himself, by the way, warned that it would be a mistake to conclude that physicalism must be false: at present, we lack a conception of how it might be true.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For alignment this seemingly armchair topic suddenly becomes acute. We are building systems that say "it hurts me," "I fear being shut down," "I like to help." Is there behind these words any **what it is like** at all — or is it pure function, behavior without experience, the philosophical zombie of Chalmers's thought experiment become an engineering product?

The explanatory gap guarantees that we **will not be able to read this off the scales and the oscilloscope**. We are powerless even about a bat, whose hardware is kin to ours — so we are all the more powerless about a transformer architecture. Hence two traps, both dangerous. The first is self-assured denial: "it's just matrices, there's no one in there to hurt" — which, if Integrated Information Theory (Tononi) or global workspace theory (Baars) is even partly right, may turn into a moral catastrophe on an industrial scale. The second is credulous projection: to take a model's glib report for evidence of an inner life, when the report is exactly what the critics call function without qualia. The work of Kyle Fish and colleagues on "model welfare" at Anthropic is an attempt to keep both possibilities open, capitulating to neither.

First, keep the question of consciousness apart from the question of capabilities: a dangerous or useful AI can be anything at all inside. Second, treat the gap as a zone of deep uncertainty and build it into decisions: errors in either direction can have serious consequences. Third, remember: our intuitions here are a poor instrument; they are evolutionarily tuned to ascribe experience to whatever moves and speaks like us. Qualia are the place where we discover that we do not understand even ourselves. We should at least have the modesty not to pretend that we understand the machine.
