### The Butlerian Jihad (Frank Herbert, 1965)

Picture a civilization that flies between the stars but does not know what a computer is. It has interstellar navigation, guided not by machines but by human Guild navigators, pumped full of spice until they see the folds of the future. It has computational operations of colossal complexity — but they are performed by mentats, living calculators trained to think in place of silicon. And over all of this, one commandment that holds the whole culture together: "Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind." Such is "Dune." And the most interesting thing in it is not the sandworms and not the spice, but what happened thousands of years before the plot begins: a war in which humanity once decided that the safest course was not to align an artificial mind but to never create one at all.

This war is called the Butlerian Jihad. Herbert sketches it only in broad strokes, in the novel's appendices and glossary, but the strokes are bold: there was a revolt against the "thinking machines" and those who owned them, there was a victory, and there was a new chief commandment, written into the Orange Catholic Bible. From that moment on, the whole universe of "Dune" is a civilization that has consciously amputated a whole class of technologies. Not because it could not build them. But because it built them, got burned, and forbade them on pain of death.

For the bestiary of alignment this is a key exhibit, because it occupies the extreme point of the spectrum. There are people who say "let us align AI." There are those who say "let us slow down and impose a moratorium." And there is the ultimate position: the only reliable defense against a nonhuman mind is not to make it at all, and if you have made it, to destroy it and forbid it forever. The Butlerian Jihad is the literary name for this intuition. It is not about a pause. It is about amputation.

Any attempt at alignment presupposes that we are playing with a dangerous entity and hoping to keep it under control. The ban refuses the game entirely. The logic of the ban is the logic of risk asymmetry: if a technology's distribution of outcomes has a sufficiently fat tail of catastrophe, then expected benefit does not save you, because a single catastrophe from that tail erases everything else. From this point of view "do not create" is not obscurantism but a variety of risk management: this is (mostly) how humanity handles targeted biological weapons and certain kinds of experiments. Herbert merely carried the principle to its logical maximum and dressed it in religious robes — because an eternal ban cannot be sustained by regulation, only by taboo.

**How it came about.** The name is no accident. Herbert alludes to Samuel Butler — the English writer of the Victorian era who, a hundred years before "Dune," first clearly formulated the anxiety about machine evolution. In 1863 Butler, living in New Zealand, published in the newspaper "The Press," under the pseudonym Cellarius, the note "Darwin among the Machines." The idea could not have been fresher: if the just-published Darwin is right about natural selection, then look at machines — they too evolve, generation after generation becoming more complex and more perfect, and far faster than organic life does. And Butler drew a merciless conclusion: therefore war against the machines is a duty, "war to the death should be instantly proclaimed against them," because otherwise they will become the dominant species and man will become their domestic animal.

Butler unfolded this fantasy in the utopian novel "Erewhon" (1872; the title is an anagram of "nowhere"). There a traveler ends up in a country where machines are forbidden and smashed, and their fragments lie in museums. The reason is explained by a treatise within the book — "The Book of the Machines": the Erewhonians read the argument about the evolution of mechanisms, took fright that machines would develop their own consciousness and will, and made a clean sweep of all machinery that had not been in use for at least 271 years (the threshold was haggled out over years of wrangling); the reactionary civil wars came only afterwards. This is the literal literary prototype of the Jihad: a civilization that voluntarily cast off technology out of fear of the machines' descendants. Herbert took from Butler both the idea and, in essence, the name.

In "Dune" itself, Herbert showed not so much the machine revolt as its aftermath. A detailed — and far more mechanistic — history of the Jihad as a war against AI came later, in the 2000s prequels by his son Brian Herbert and Kevin Anderson. Fans dispute the canonicity of those books, and for our discussion the original matters more: for Frank Herbert the Jihad is above all a cultural and religious rupture, the revolt of men who had "turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free," which "only permitted other men with machines to enslave them."

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first — feasibility. A global eternal ban requires what humanity does not have: perfect coordination. AI, unlike physical machines, is ultimately mathematics and weights, which can be copied and hidden. The ban creates a race: whoever violates it in secret gets a decisive advantage, and so the incentive to violate is enormous, and a black market appears. The Erewhonians had it easier — a hammer against gears works reliably; you cannot smash bytes with a hammer.

The second — the cost of refusal. In refusing "thinking machines," we also refuse everything they might give: medicine, science, the solution of the very problems that today kill people by the millions. The ban is not a neutral "let us wait" but a choice with a body count of its own, merely invisible. This too is visible in Herbert's own text: the civilization of "Dune" is feudal, cruel, and frozen in its development for millennia. Taboo has a price.

The third — the most caustic. Enforcing an eternal ban requires an eternal overseer. Someone must watch, punish, and decide where the boundary of "mind in the likeness of the human" runs. And that is an institution with colossal power — and we have simply replaced the risk of an uncontrolled AI with the risk of an uncontrolled inquisition. In Herbert's universe the Bene Gesserit, the Guild, and the mentats form elites around human abilities developed after the ban on thinking machines. This suggests a political danger: a ban can redistribute power to those who control the permitted alternatives.

The fourth — the ethical. The word "jihad" is chosen for a reason: it is a holy war, that is, mass violence sanctified by a higher goal. The romanticization of "righteous destruction" is dangerous in itself — it grants an indulgence for bloodshed in the name of salvation, and the history of such indulgences is well known.

For certain classes of technology, refusal really is rational, and humanity already practices it. We do not run military programs for cloning humans and we try not to widen the club of bioweapon owners — not because we cannot, but because we decided the game was not worth the candle. The Butlerian intuition is not madness; the only question is whether AI falls into this class, and whether the ban is technically achievable.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical use of the exhibit is to keep in mind the whole spectrum of reactions to risk, not only "alignment as an engineering problem." The Butlerian pole is useful as a thought experiment: it forces one to ask whether there are outcomes for which the right answer is "do not build." Nick Bostrom formalized a kindred thought in "the vulnerable world hypothesis": if there exists a technology that almost surely leads to catastrophe when widely accessible (a "black ball" in the urn of inventions), then stability requires either total surveillance or a refusal to draw the ball. The Butlerian Jihad is the literary image of the second option, carried to its end.

A more moderate, "engineering" version of the same idea is differential technological development (also Bostrom): not to forbid everything, but consciously to accelerate defensive technologies and slow down dangerous ones, changing the order in which we draw the balls. And between the poles lies the whole contemporary discussion: the Future of Life Institute (FLI) open letter of 2023 with its call for a six-month pause on training the most powerful models; the far more radical column by Yudkowsky in TIME, where he says a pause is not enough and one must "shut it all down"; and on the other side of the barricades — the effective accelerationists (e/acc), for whom any braking is itself a crime against the future. Doomers invoke the Butlerian Jihad to frighten, accelerationists to tease: this, the accelerationists say, is where the logic of fear leads.

Where it leads depends on what we believe about the tail of the distribution. If a catastrophic outcome is improbable and reversible, Butlerian amputation is excessive and harmful. If it is probable and irreversible, Butlerian amputation may turn out to be the only honest option, and then the whole question becomes "is it even feasible without its own tyranny." Herbert's value is that he gave no cheap answer: his universe forbade machines — and became neither free nor kind as a result. The taboo saved humanity from one master and handed it to another. This is, perhaps, the most sober thing fiction has said about the ban: the refusal of a dangerous technology is not an exit from the problem of power but merely a change of its address.
