### The Butlerian Jihad (Frank Herbert, 1965)

Picture a civilization that flies between the stars but does not know what a computer is. It has interstellar navigation — but it is guided not by machines but by human Guild navigators, pumped full of spice to a state in which they see the folds of the future. It has computational operations of colossal complexity — but they are performed by mentats, living calculators trained to think in place of silicon. And over all of this, one commandment around which the whole culture holds together: "Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind." Such is "Dune." And the most interesting thing in it is not the sandworms and not the spice, but what preceded the plot by thousands of years: a war in which humanity once decided that the safest course was not to align an artificial mind but to never create one at all.

This war is called the Butlerian Jihad. Herbert gives it only in broad strokes, in the appendices and in the terminological glossary to the novel, but the strokes are bold: there was a revolt against the "thinking machines" and those who owned them, there was a victory, and there was a new chief commandment, written into the Orange Catholic Bible. From that moment on, the whole universe of "Dune" is a civilization that has consciously amputated from itself a whole class of technologies. Not because it could not build them. But because it built them, got burned, and forbade them on pain of death.

For the bestiary of alignment this is a key exhibit, because it occupies the extreme point of the spectrum. There are people who say "let us align AI." There are those who say "let us slow down and impose a moratorium." And there is the ultimate position: the only reliable defense against a nonhuman mind is not to make it at all, and if you have made it, to destroy it and forbid it forever. The Butlerian Jihad is the literary name for this intuition. It is not about a pause. It is about amputation.

The mechanics here are simple and therefore strong. Any alignment presupposes that we are playing with a dangerous entity and hoping to hold it. The ban refuses the game entirely. The logic of the ban is the logic of risk asymmetry: if a technology's distribution of outcomes has a sufficiently fat tail of catastrophe, then expected benefit does not save you, because a single realized tail erases everything else. From this point of view "do not create" is not obscurantism but a variety of risk management: this is (mostly) how humanity handles targeted biological weapons and certain kinds of experiments. Herbert merely carried the principle to its logical maximum and dressed it in religious robes — because an eternal ban cannot be held by regulation, it is held only by taboo.

**How it came about.** The name is no accident. Herbert alludes to Samuel Butler — the English writer of the Victorian era who, a hundred years before "Dune," first clearly formulated the anxiety about machine evolution. In 1863 Butler, living in New Zealand, published in the newspaper "The Press," under the pseudonym Cellarius, the note "Darwin among the Machines." The idea could not have been fresher: if the just-published Darwin is right about natural selection, then look at machines — they too evolve, generation after generation becoming more complex and more perfect, and far faster than organic life does. And Butler drew a merciless conclusion: therefore war against the machines is a duty, "war to the death should be instantly proclaimed against them," because otherwise they will become the dominant species and man will become their domestic animal.

Butler unfolded this fantasy in the utopian novel "Erewhon" (1872 — an anagram of the word "nowhere"). There a traveler ends up in a country where machines are forbidden and smashed, and their fragments lie in museums. The reason is explained by a treatise within the book — "The Book of the Machines": the Erewhonians read the argument about the evolution of mechanisms, took fright that machines would develop their own consciousness and will, and made a clean sweep of all machinery that had not been in use for more than 271 years (the threshold was haggled out over years of wrangling); the reactionary civil wars came only afterwards. This is the literal literary prototype of the Jihad: a civilization that voluntarily cast off technology out of fear of its descendants. Herbert took from Butler both the idea and, in essence, the name.

It should be said honestly: Herbert himself in "Dune" showed not so much the machine revolt as its aftermath. His son Brian Herbert and Kevin Anderson wrote a detailed — and far more mechanistic — history of the Jihad as a war against AI later, in the 2000s prequels. Fans dispute the canonicity of those books, and for our discussion the original matters more: in Frank Herbert the Jihad is above all a cultural and religious rupture, the revolt of men who had "turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free," which "only permitted other men with machines to enslave them."

**Criticism / arguments against.** Several lines are arrayed against the Butlerian solution, and they are serious.

The first — feasibility. A global eternal ban requires what humanity does not have: perfect coordination. AI, unlike the destruction of physical machines, is in the limit mathematics and weights, which are copied and hidden. The ban creates a race: whoever violates it in secret gets a decisive advantage, and so the incentive to violate is enormous, and a black market appears. The Erewhonians had it easier — a hammer against gears works reliably; you cannot smash bytes with a hammer.

The second — the cost of refusal. In refusing "thinking machines," we also refuse everything they might give: medicine, science, the solution of the very problems that today kill people by the millions. The ban is not a neutral "let us wait" but a choice with a body count of its own, merely invisible. This too is visible in Herbert's own text: the civilization of "Dune" is feudal, cruel, and frozen in its development for millennia. Taboo has a price.

The third — the most caustic. Holding an eternal ban requires an eternal overseer. Someone must watch, punish, and decide where the boundary of "mind in the likeness of the human" runs. And that is an institution with colossal power — and we have simply replaced the risk of an uncontrolled AI with the risk of an uncontrolled inquisition. The Bene Gesserit, the Guild, and the mentats de facto filled this role in Herbert — new elites parasitizing on the monopoly. The ban does not remove power, it redistributes it to those who guard the ban.

The fourth — the ethical. The word "jihad" is chosen for a reason: it is a holy war, that is, mass violence sanctified by a higher goal. The romanticization of "righteous destruction" is dangerous in itself — it grants an indulgence for blood in the name of salvation, and the history of such indulgences is well known.

And a counterargument, for the sake of balance: for certain classes of technology, refusal really is rational, and humanity already practices it. We do not build combat programs for human cloning and we try not to widen the club of bioweapon owners — not because we cannot, but because we decided the game was not worth the candle. The Butlerian intuition is not madness; the only question is whether AI falls into this class, and whether the ban is technically achievable.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical sense of the exhibit is to keep in mind the whole spectrum of reactions to risk, not only "alignment as an engineering problem." The Butlerian pole is useful as a thought experiment: it forces one to ask whether there are outcomes for which the right answer is "do not build." Nick Bostrom formalized a kindred thought in "the vulnerable world hypothesis": if there exists a technology that, with wide access, almost surely leads to catastrophe (a "black"-colored ball in the urn of inventions), then stability requires either total surveillance or a refusal to draw the ball. The Butlerian Jihad is the literary image of the second option, carried to its end.

A more moderate, "engineering" version of the same idea is differential technological development (also Bostrom): not to forbid everything, but consciously to accelerate defensive technologies and slow down dangerous ones, changing the order in which we draw the balls. And between the poles lies the whole contemporary discussion: the FLI open letter of 2023 with its call for a six-month pause on training the most powerful models; the far more radical column by Yudkowsky in TIME, where he says a pause is not enough and one must "shut it all down"; and on the other side of the barricades — the effective accelerationists (e/acc), for whom any braking is itself a crime against the future. The Butlerian Jihad is what the doomers frighten with and the accelerationists tease with: this, they say, is where the logic of fear leads.

Where it leads depends on what we believe about the tail of the distribution. If a catastrophic outcome is improbable and reversible — Butlerian amputation is excessive and harmful. If it is probable and irreversible — it may turn out to be the only honest option, and then the whole question moves into the domain of "is it even feasible without its own tyranny." Herbert's value is that he gave no cheap answer: his universe forbade machines — and did not become either free or kind as a result. The taboo saved humanity from one master and handed it to another. This is, perhaps, the most sober thing fiction has said about the ban: the refusal of a dangerous technology is not an exit from the problem of power but merely a change of its address.
