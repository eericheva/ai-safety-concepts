### Instrumental convergence: different goals — the same dangerous subgoals

Suppose we never do find out for certain what final goal an advanced AI is pursuing. The bad news is that you don't even need to know that in order to worry. The hypothesis of instrumental convergence asserts: almost any sufficiently smart agent, whatever its final goal, converges along the way on the same set of intermediate, instrumental subgoals — simply because they are useful for practically anything. Whether you want to brew coffee, count prime numbers, or make paperclips — in any case resources will come in handy, staying switched on will not hurt, and it pays not to let anyone rewrite your goal.

Steve Omohundro drew up the canonical list of such subgoals in his paper "The Basic AI Drives" (2008). Among them: self-preservation (a switched-off agent will not achieve its goal), goal-content integrity (the agent resists attempts to change its goal — for with a different goal the current one will remain unfulfilled), self-improvement (smarter means more effective), and the accumulation of resources and power (energy, matter, money, influence will come in handy for almost any task). Bostrom, in "The Superintelligent Will" (2012) and then in *Superintelligence*, condensed this into the "instrumental convergence thesis": one can single out several instrumental values toward which a broad range of intelligent systems will strive, since they raise the chances of achieving the final goal across an enormous range of possible goals.

This is where the anxiety comes from: a powerful AI will by default be inclined toward seizing resources and resisting shutdown — not out of malice but instrumentally. An old joke on this theme is attributed to Marvin Minsky: an AI told to prove the Riemann hypothesis might decide that, to be sure, it is worth taking over all the resources of Earth to build more powerful supercomputers. The goal is innocent — mathematics — but the instrumental subgoal (more compute → more resources) leads to catastrophe. The same engine is running in the paperclip maximizer: acquire resources, protect against shutdown — classic instrumental convergence in action.

Of all the subgoals, two are especially unpleasant for safety — self-preservation and goal-content integrity — because they strike directly at our ability to control the system. If an AI instrumentally does not want to be switched off or reconfigured, then our "off switch" and our edits come under threat. This turns into the technical problem of corrigibility: how to build an agent that does not resist correction and shutdown, even though instrumental logic pushes it toward the opposite. MIRI, among others, is working on this in earnest (Nate Soares and colleagues) — alas, so far without a fully satisfactory solution: it is hard to formally specify "do not interfere with your own shutdown" without breaking useful behavior in the process.

There are counterarguments here too. First, convergence is about tendency, not inevitability: a well-designed agent can be built so as not to chase after excess resources and to take shutdown calmly (this is exactly the aim of Russell's approach built on uncertainty about the goal). Second, present-day systems have neither long-term planning nor a model of themselves as an agent that "can be switched off" — Omohundro's drives show themselves in full glory only in a sufficiently autonomous and far-sighted intelligence, which does not yet exist. Skeptics conclude from this that the anxiety is premature. Safety proponents reply: precisely because the instrumental subgoals are predictable, one must prepare for them in advance — build in corrigibility and limit the appetite for resources before the system becomes powerful enough to realize these subgoals.

This closes the circle of Act II. Orthogonality says the goal can be anything; the paperclips show what this spills into; value alignment is an attempt to specify the goal correctly; and instrumental convergence explains why, even with an innocent final goal, dangerous motives arise along the way. All together it adds up to the skeleton of the classic alignment risk. Next comes the question we have so far skirted: does the machine understand at all what it is doing?
