### The Leaky Wallet — Money Pump and Dutch Book (Davidson–McKinsey–Suppes, 1955; Ramsey, 1926)

Picture a fairground barker who sells nothing but simply offers you trades — and always, in your view, to their own loss. In your pocket are three things: an apple, a pear, and a plum, but you hold only one at a time. You honestly admit: you value the plum above the apple, the pear above the plum, and the apple above the pear. Nothing terrible, ordinary human "likes." The barker smiles. "You have an apple? For a penny I'll swap it for a plum — you like the plum less than the apple… oh, the other way, more." You pay a penny, take the plum. "And for the plum — a pear, just a penny, the pear's better." You pay. "And for the pear — an apple." You pay. And there you are again with an apple in hand, exactly where you started, but three pennies poorer. The circle closes, the barker starts it up again. They do not cheat, do not slip you a fake, do not press — each individual trade you make freely, because it really is an improvement by your own measures. It is just that your measures go in a circle, and the barker has turned that circle into a perpetual motion machine for pumping money out of you. This is the money pump.

**Mechanism and significance.** The argument strikes at a single point — the intransitivity of preferences. If A > B, B > C, and C > A, then there is no consistent "how much each is worth": any thing can be called both best and worst, depending on which side you approach it from. The pump turns this logical squiggle into a material loss. The moral is pragmatic, not aesthetic: incoherent preferences are not a sin against taste but a hole in the wallet through which everything you have leaks out, and with your own consent at each step. Ruin, entirely by your own free will.

The Dutch book is the same trap, only transposed from the world of preferences into the world of beliefs. Here the agent assigns to things not a price but a probability — a subjective degree of confidence at which it is willing to make bets. If these degrees are incoherent, that is, violate the axioms of probability (say, you rate "rain tomorrow" at 0.6, "no rain tomorrow" also at 0.6, and the sum has crossed one), then a cunning bookmaker will put together a set of bets, each of which by your own numbers you will consider favorable and gladly accept — yet together they are guaranteed to leave you in the red no matter the outcome. A "Dutch book" is exactly such a book of bets, rigged so that the victim is bound to lose. And the twin conclusion: rationality of beliefs requires coherence, otherwise you will be cleaned out on bets you yourself deemed fair.

Both beasts are pragmatic arguments of one breed. They do not say "be coherent because it is beautiful"; they say "be coherent, or anyone who notices will gut you." And so both became a foundation under the coherence theorems: since incoherence is punishable by money, the pressure of selection — market, evolutionary, gradient — must squeeze everything sufficiently smart toward consistent preferences and consistent probabilities. And a consistent agent, by the von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem, behaves like an expected-utility maximizer. Here the money pump weaves into the main argument about AI risk: "a sufficiently smart agent will become coherent (so as not to be milked) → hence a utility maximizer → hence a potentially dangerous optimizer." The pump is the prop under the very first link of the chain.

**How it came about.** The money pump was set going explicitly by Donald Davidson, John McKinsey, and Patrick Suppes in the paper "Outlines of a Formal Theory of Value, I" (Philosophy of Science, 1955) — they were building a formal theory of value and noticed that an intransitive person can be driven around a cycle of trades to their own loss. The Dutch book is older: its idea goes back to Frank Ramsey and his essay "Truth and Probability" (written in 1926, published posthumously in 1931), where probability is for the first time defined through willingness to bet, and the consistency of bets turns out to be the condition for those probabilities to be meaningful. The Italian Bruno de Finetti developed the same thought independently, and more deeply, in "La prévision" (1937); he is considered the founder of the subjectivist view of probability. The phrase "Dutch book" itself surfaced in the English-language literature later, in the 1950s, and its origin is obscure. From philosophical exotica both arguments migrated into decision theory, from there into economics, and in the 2010s they were picked up by the canon of agent foundations (LessWrong, MIRI) as a folk proof that "intelligence strives for coherence."

**Criticism / arguments against.** The beast is beautiful, but on closer inspection it noticeably molts.

The first objection is the simplest and most devastating. A real agent is not a blind automaton: after the second or third circle it notices that it is standing still and getting poorer, and simply stops trading. The pump works only against a victim who myopically evaluates each trade in isolation and never looks at the cycle as a whole. Let the agent reason about the entire sequence of deals at once — and it will refuse the very first step leading into the funnel. Frederic Schick in "Dutch Bookies and Money Pumps" (1986) showed outright: the argument proves not that an intransitive agent is doomed to go broke, but only that it will go broke if it is additionally stupid and does not learn.

The second objection is subtler and more dangerous for the whole construction. Between "intransitive" and "coherent" there is a third state — incomplete preferences. An agent may honestly have no opinion about some pairs: not "A equals B," but "I decline to compare A and B." Such preferences form no circle, they cannot be spun into a pump — and yet they do not reduce to maximizing a single utility function. Richard Bradley and other defenders of incompleteness show: one can be unexploitable without being a VNM agent. The bridge "incoherence → loss → hence all the smart are maximizers" is breached: it has a whole safe bypass.

It is precisely this breach that Elliott Thornley turned into a tool. In his work on the "shutdown problem" he builds an agent with deliberately incomplete preferences — one that simply lacks a preference between trajectories of different length (keep working or be switched off) — and proves two things at once: such an agent cannot be drained by a pump, and at the same time it does not resist shutdown, because it maximizes nothing worth clinging to life for. Incompleteness, which seemed a defect, turns out to be a lever of corrigibility: a shutdownable agent is unexploitable — and simultaneously obedient.

The third objection is about the idealization of the barker themself. The pump presupposes an adversary who knows your preferences better than you do, acts with impunity, and never meets resistance, while you are memoryless and naive. Edward McClennen ("Rationality and Dynamic Choice," 1990) countered this with "resolute choice": an agent that binds itself to a plan in advance passes through any pump dry. In a real world with symmetric information, reputation, and memory, the perpetual motion machine of poverty stalls on the second turn.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** It is most useful to read this beast not as a proof but as a diagnostic bench — an instrument that shows exactly where the preference structure leaks. If an agent lets itself be milked, there is a cycle inside it; to rivet the cycle shut is to impose transitivity. But — and this is the main turn of the last decade — from "unexploitability" it by no means follows that there is "expected-utility maximization." Between the ruinable fool and the predatory optimizer lies a vast and inhabited land: agents with incomplete but consistent preferences, who cannot be cheated and who at the same time do not turn into an unstoppable maximizer. Thornley's program lives precisely there — it tries to settle a safe AI in that band.

From here runs a straight thread to corrigibility. If earlier resistance to the pump was considered a synonym for "correct" rationality (and hence, indirectly, a justification for instrumental convergence and danger), now the same argument is turned inside out: not every unexploitability need be utility-based, and it is precisely an architecture that gives up completeness — "leaky" in the right places — that gives us an agent that can be switched off. Resistance to the pump is safety only for its wallet; safety for us may require the exact opposite — to leave a gap in the preferences that cannot be gripped.

And finally, the link to Goodhart's law. The money pump is the oldest illustration of how optimizing pressure finds and forces through any crack in the specification of goals: a circle in the agent's preferences becomes a metric that the adversary maximizes at its expense. The moral of both stories is one. What is elegant on paper — transitivity, coherence, a single utility function — is not free; and a gap, a hole, an honest "I don't know" sometimes turn out to be not a defect of rationality but the last latch on the off switch.
