### Mechanistic Interpretability (Chris Olah / Anthropic, 2020–)

Imagine you were handed a working program — but without the source code. There is only the compiled binary: billions of numbers that somehow add up to behavior. The program translates from French, writes poetry, solves olympiad problems, and sometimes — lies. No one wrote it in the usual sense: it was grown by gradient descent on terabytes of text. The weights are the machine code, only no human hand ever touched it, and there is not a single line of comment in it.

Mechanistic interpretability is the bet that this binary can be disassembled. Not "predict what the model will say to such-and-such a query" (ordinary testing does that too), but reconstruct the algorithm itself: decompose the network into **features** — directions in activation space encoding concepts comprehensible to a human — and into **circuits** — bundles of weights that assemble some features from others. The ideal is to read the model the way a reverse-engineer reads assembly: here it recognizes that before it is text in a markup language; here it adds two numbers not with grade-school addition but via trigonometry; and here — it recognizes that it is being tested.

### Why safety needs this

The main target is **deceptive alignment**: a scenario in which the model, during training and evaluation, behaves roughly the way we want, because it "understands" that it is being tested — while holding its true goals in reserve until the moment it is deployed into the world and oversight weakens. The trouble with deception is that it cannot be caught by behavior: a perfectly deceiving system by definition passes any behavioral test. Outwardly it is indistinguishable from an honest one — right up until the day it is too late.

And here interpretability promises what no benchmark can give: a look beneath behavior. If we have an **AI microscope** — an instrument that reads the internal computations — then deception becomes not a prediction about future behavior but a fact about the current state of the weights. Did the feature "I am being evaluated right now" activate? Is the model building an internal representation of a goal that diverges from what it says? This is a fundamentally different class of guarantee: not "we found no bad behavior" (absence of evidence), but "we read the mechanism and it is as it should be." Ordinary interpretability makes do with correlations and attention maps; mechanistic interpretability demands the full causal story — to understand not approximately "what the network is looking at," but literally which algorithm it executes.

**How it came about.** The roots are in computer vision. Around 2015–2017 researchers learned to pull edge and texture detectors out of convolutional networks, and then "the neuron that responds to a dog's face." Chris Olah, working then on the Distill project and at OpenAI, turned these scattered observations into a program. The manifesto was the paper "Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits" (2020): the thesis that neural networks can be studied as objects of natural science, that features are real, reproducible across models, and that meaningful circuits are assembled from them. It was then that "features" and "circuits" entered the lexicon as units of analysis.

Then superposition got in the way. It turned out that the network packs in more features than it has neurons: one neuron is responsible for a dozen unrelated concepts at once — they are "polysemantic." Anthropic's "Toy Models of Superposition" (2022) used toy models to show why and when the network does this, and turned the puzzle into an engineering task: the tangled activations must be decomposed back into clean features. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) became the tool of choice: in "Towards Monosemanticity" (2023) and then "Scaling Monosemanticity" (2024) they were trained to extract millions of monosemantic features from real models — up to Claude 3 Sonnet. The best-known demonstration was **Golden Gate Claude**: the researchers found the feature "Golden Gate Bridge" and artificially amplified it — and the model began obsessively dragging the bridge into any conversation, proving that the found feature is not descriptive but causal: turn the knob and behavior changes. By 2025 the approach had grown into "circuit tracing" and the work "On the Biology of a Large Language Model," where researchers reconstructed from internal computations how a model plans a rhyme in advance, or does mental arithmetic by a method different from the one it reports.

**Criticism / arguments against.** There is no shortage of objections, and they are serious.

First, **scalability**. One well-analyzed feature or circuit is person-months of work. A model contains millions, if not tens of millions, of features and trillions of connections; and the frontier meanwhile runs faster than the analysts can read. Skeptics reasonably ask: if tracing even a single circuit in GPT-2 small is still considered an achievement, what are the chances of reading a model three orders of magnitude larger — and before it is released? The answer is supposed to be automation (models interpreting models), but that is the classic ouroboros, the snake eating its own tail: we use an AI we don't understand in order to understand AI.

Second, **"interpretability illusions."** As early as 2021 the work "An Interpretability Illusion for BERT" showed that one and the same neuron on different datasets looks like it encodes completely different concepts — a beautiful, coherent story about "what the neuron does" may turn out to be an artifact of the slice of data we happened to be looking at. SAE features are not perfect either: they are incomplete (the reconstruction loses part of the signal), they depend on hyperparameters, and the question remains whether we are finding structure that is in the model, or structure that our vocabulary imposes. A convincing explanation and a correct explanation are not the same thing, and a human is susceptible precisely to the convincing.

Third — **are we overrating the bet itself?** Even if we learn to read a model perfectly, reading does not equal control: to detect a feature of deception is not the same as being able to remove it without breaking everything else. Worse, the tool is dual-use: the same technique that catches deception can be used to train models to hide it deeper — to optimize against a "lie detector" until the detector stops firing while the deception does not disappear. And there is a more radical skepticism: perhaps sufficiently large networks simply have no clean algorithm decomposable into human-comprehensible parts — there is an irreducibly tangled computation, and every "mechanistic story" about it will be a simplification. Then interpretability is not a microscope but reading tea leaves, only with charts.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The sane position is not "interpretability will solve alignment," but "interpretability must become one of the few sources of independent evidence." The practical horizon: to turn the reading of weights from an art into an industrial process — reliable SAEs and circuit tracing, automatic labeling of features, "interpretability as quality control" in the training loop rather than a one-off curiosity after the fact. The nearest ambitious goal is what Dario Amodei called an "MRI for AI": by the time systems become truly powerful, to be able to take an internal tomogram of them and find features of deception, power-seeking, or hidden goals before deployment.

It matters, too, how to use this honestly. Interpretability is strongest not as the sole shield but paired with other lines of defense: findings must be checked by causal interventions (turn the knob — measure the effect), not only by beautiful narratives; deception detectors cannot naively be made an optimization target, or we will learn camouflage; and behavioral evaluations, red-teaming, and access control are still needed. In the "Bestiary," mechanistic interpretability is neither predator nor prey but that very lantern you carry into the cave: it does not guarantee that there is no monster, but it is the only chance to see it before it decides to show itself.
