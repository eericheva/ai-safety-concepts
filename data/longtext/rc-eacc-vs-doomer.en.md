### e/acc versus doomers / d/acc (Beff Jezos, Yudkowsky, Buterin; 2022–2023)

The whole debate about humanity's future can be compressed to a single button with two positions: "floor it" and "emergency brake." That button got handed to tens of thousands of people on Twitter, with an invitation to pick a side by putting a little flag in their handle — "e/acc" or a drop of irony over "doomers." Thus was born one of the most conspicuous culture wars around AI. And, like almost any war, it is interesting not for what it started over, but for how quickly the substance of the dispute drowned under the flags.

We are talking about an ideological spectrum of attitudes toward **speed**. At one pole — effective accelerationism, e/acc: accelerate technology and AI as fast as physics allows. At the other — the "doomers," proponents of caution: slow down, introduce pauses, treat superintelligence as an existential threat. And somewhere in the middle Vitalik Buterin tried to drive in a stake reading d/acc — and proposed arguing not about pace, but about direction.

**What, exactly, is being claimed.** e/acc in its early, half-serious formulation is not so much a program as a metaphysics. Technological progress, the growth of computational power, increasing complexity — are presented as a continuation of a cosmic process: the universe supposedly "wants" to dissipate energy and build up complexity, and intelligent life (including AI) is an instrument of this thermodynamic teleology. Hence the moral: to slow down is to go against the very nature of being; regulators, ethics committees, and talk of safety are friction, braking the inevitable and the good. Optimism is built in axiomatically here: growth is almost by definition good, and problems generated by technology will be solved by the next technologies.

The "doomer" position is mirror-like in structure but opposite in sign. If we create a system smarter than a human before we learn to control it, we may not get a second try. So it is reasonable to slow down: introduce a moratorium on training the most powerful models, buy time for alignment research, and in the radical version — halt the race altogether by international agreement. The bet here is not on the teleology of the universe but on the asymmetry of risks: the price of a mistake is not "GDP grows more slowly" but an irreversible catastrophe.

**The mechanism behind the turn to war.** Both positions individually are theses one can argue with on the merits. But in the Twitter/X environment they quickly curled up into *identities*. The flag in the handle stopped denoting an argument and came to denote a tribe. From there the usual dynamics of tribalism kicked in: one's own are read charitably, others in the stupidest possible interpretation. e/acc as depicted by its opponents is a bunch of irresponsible techbros ready to stake the biosphere for the sake of rising stock prices. Doomers as depicted by the accelerationists are cowardly Luddites secretly craving the power of regulators, an "end-of-the-world sect" that would strangle the cure for cancer in bureaucracy. Each side argues not with the strongest version of the other but with a caricature — and, tellingly, the caricature is more convenient: it is more pleasant to defeat.

**How it came about.** The term e/acc surfaced in 2022 in anonymous accounts, the chief of which was "Beff Jezos." At the end of 2023 a Forbes investigation (Emily Baker-White) unmasked the person behind it: Guillaume Verdon — a physicist and startup founder who had earlier worked in Google's quantum division. The word itself is an ironic mutation of "effective altruism" (EA): if EA calculates how to maximize good cautiously and measurably, then e/acc proposes to maximize *acceleration* — and snickers at the whole culture of rationalists and "longtermists."

The confrontation became electrified in the spring of 2023. In March the Future of Life Institute (FLI) published an open letter, "Pause Giant AI Experiments," calling for a six-month pause on training systems more powerful than GPT-4; it was signed by thousands, including well-known researchers and entrepreneurs. Next, on 29 March, TIME ran a column by Eliezer Yudkowsky with a telling thesis: a pause is not enough — "Shut it all down," switch everything off. The sharpness of the formulations gave the accelerationists an ideal target, and "doomer" turned from the self-designation of a small group into a label for anyone who utters the word "risk."

In the autumn of 2023 the dispute took shape in manifestos. Marc Andreessen put out "The Techno-Optimist Manifesto" — a hymn to progress, where caution is named outright among the "enemies." And on 27 November Vitalik Buterin replied with the essay "My techno-optimism," where he agreed with optimism in spirit but refused the binary of "gas/brake" and proposed a third vector — **d/acc**. The letter "d" is deliberately multivalent: defense, decentralization, democracy, differential. The idea: accelerate not everything indiscriminately and not brake everything indiscriminately, but *selectively* develop technologies that strengthen defense rather than offense, and that distribute power rather than concentrate it. Not a pedal and not an emergency brake, but a steering wheel.

**Criticism / arguments against.** First and foremost: both extremes build straw men — and therefore, paradoxically, need each other. e/acc holds on to a caricatured doomer in order to look like the voice of life against the voice of fear; doomer discourse holds on to a caricatured accelerationist in order to look like the voice of reason against recklessness. The flag in the handle economizes on thought: it announces in advance what to think and spares one the labor of reading the opponent.

Second: the subject of the dispute itself is displaced. It seems that they are arguing about **pace** — faster or slower. But what really matters is the **vector**. "Fast" is meaningless without an answer to "fast *where*": the same years can be spent on growing offensive biotech or on growing means of biodefense, on centralizing control over models or on tools for auditing them. The "speed" axis hides the "direction" axis — and this is exactly why d/acc, however one relates to it, poses a more meaningful question than either pole.

Third, addressed to e/acc specifically: the appeal to thermodynamics as a source of *values* is a naturalistic fallacy in pure form. From the fact that the universe dissipates energy, not one iota follows about what is *good*; "complexity grows" is a description, not a commandment. And the built-in optimism ("every problem will be solved by the next technology") is not a conclusion but an assumption, precisely what needs to be proven.

Fourth, addressed to the doomer extreme: "shut it all down" runs up against the problem of enforceability and coordination. A global moratorium requires trust between competitors and states that does not exist; a unilateral pause may simply hand leadership to the least cautious. Confident, high estimates of p(doom) often rest on long chains of assumptions, presented as near-certainty — and this rhetorical rigidity itself feeds the caricature.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is boring and therefore rarely gathers likes: take off the flag and return to a substantive conversation. It is more useful to ask not "are you for acceleration or for a pause," but "which *exactly* technologies, in what order, with what protective measures." This is the logic of **differential technological development** (Nick Bostrom): not "faster/slower in general," but changing the *relative* speeds — outracing risky capabilities with their own means of defense and control. d/acc is a popularized version of exactly this idea, and its value is not that it is "more right" than the poles but that it moves the conversation from the axis of pace to the axis of direction.

It is also useful to bear in mind that this dispute is a special case of larger ones. Technological **solutionism** ("a new technology will solve the problems of the previous one") is the optimistic axiom of e/acc, and it is worth checking rather than accepting. Bostrom's **vulnerable world hypothesis** is an argument that under some configurations blind acceleration really is capable of drawing a "black ball"; so direction matters more than speed not out of cowardice but out of arithmetic. And the **AI race** explains why calls for a pause so often shatter against reality: as long as the winner is the one who does not brake, individual caution is punishable — and the problem is solved not by a slogan but by coordination.

If one thought is worth taking away from this whole war of flags, here it is: a dispute about speed is almost always a disguised dispute about direction, and whoever first takes off the flag and asks "where exactly are we going" usually turns out to be smarter than both poles at once.
