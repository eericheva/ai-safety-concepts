### Longtermism (Nick Beckstead)

Longtermism is an ethical position according to which the well-being of distant future generations counts exactly as much as our own. Given that, influencing the distant future becomes one of the chief moral priorities. In AI ethics this means that, in assessing the consequences of technologies, we must think not only of the coming decades and not only of those now living, but of millennia ahead — of the billions of potential lives whose fate depends on our decisions today.

Nick Beckstead, one of the longtermist movement's early ideologues, puts it bluntly in his dissertation: shaping the trajectory of the distant future is incredibly important; overall, the most important thing of all is to make sure humanity develops along the best possible path over millions, billions, and trillions of years. If we do not go extinct, then the overwhelming majority of people will live not now but later — perhaps in space, in colonies around distant stars. And then even a tiny improvement in the odds of a happy future for that colossal number of people outweighs almost anything we can do here and now.

Longtermism and AI safety are joined at the hip. For a longtermist, artificial intelligence is the chief factor determining the fate of the distant future: build a safe superintelligence, and we may secure the flourishing of civilization for millennia; fail, and we risk a long night of extinction. Hence the conclusion of Bostrom, MacAskill, Beckstead, and company: reducing existential risks from AI is a moral duty toward the trillions of our potential descendants. Bostrom adds his notion of "astronomical waste" (2003): if humanity misses its cosmic potential — goes extinct before it manages to settle the galaxy — the number of never-realized happy lives will turn out to be literally astronomical. And that means any risk capable of finishing us off ahead of time puts not merely our lives but a whole galactic civilization that does not yet exist at stake.

**Arguments against.** For all the appeal of "caring for our descendants," longtermism has no shortage of critics. The first objection is speculativeness: building an ethics on events that may or may not happen a thousand years from now is unreliable. We err even in predictions ten years ahead, let alone about eternity. It is easy to be carried away by phantom probabilities and to overlook the real ills of today — climate, inequality. The second is that longtermism has a way of justifying dubious means for the sake of a grand hypothetical end. The philosopher Émile Torres called it "quite possibly the most dangerous secular belief system in the world today": when an abstract "potential of humanity" is placed above the needs of living people, tragedy is not far off. If someone decides that for the sake of colonizing the galaxy it is permissible to sacrifice a million present-day lives — after all, there will later be a trillion happy ones — that's already a nightmare, and shame on anyone who'd think that way. The third is a question of trust: who, exactly, decides which future is "good"? When we strive toward a utopia for our descendants, it's easy to end up imposing our own dubious ideas on them. Modern longtermists, for example, often proceed from the assumption that more living people is a good thing (let us settle the galaxy with trillions). But not all philosophers agree that population growth is good in itself; here a whole field of population ethics opens up, with its "repugnant conclusion" (maximize the quantity of lives at the price of their quality).

In sum, the critics see a whiff of "fanaticism with tiny probabilities" in longtermism: a minuscule chance of a gigantic payoff — and we are already prepared to neglect modest but reliable good deeds today. This is Pascal's wager, only worse — a Pascal's mugging (shout-out to Roko's basilisk). Beckstead himself acknowledges the problem: his thesis implies that even a vanishing chance of an infinitely good outcome outweighs any finite one — he calls this fanaticism and honestly says that living with such a paradox is hard.

In AI ethics all of this boils down to an argument over priorities: how much to invest in addressing long-term risks versus today's problems? Longtermists say a substantial share, since the very existence of the future is at stake. Their opponents say: don't overdo it, because present-day abuses of AI (surveillance, algorithmic discrimination) harm living people right now.

Lately even people within the effective altruism community admit that too narrow a focus on "universes a billion years hence" makes the argument less persuasive — society finds it hard to accept such abstract arguments. Neel Nanda and Scott Alexander therefore argue that the case for reducing existential risk does not require longtermism: measures to reduce existential risks are useful in the near term as well (the same methods of controlling AI make today's systems more reliable too). But as a moral lens longtermism remains valuable — it lends work on AI safety a grandiose scale and meaning. As someone once joked on a forum: we are saving not the world but millions of worlds — a dream job for a superhero. And the critics, for their part, will remind us that before you take on infinity, maybe start by cleaning your own room.
