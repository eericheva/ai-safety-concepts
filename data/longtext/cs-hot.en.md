### Higher-Order Theories of Consciousness (David Rosenthal, 1980s–2000s)

Imagine that for half an hour you have been driving along a familiar road, absorbed in conversation — and suddenly you realize you cannot remember the last ten kilometers. Your eyes saw the lane markings, your hands turned the wheel, the ache in your stiff back never went anywhere. All of these were mental states — visual, motor, painful. But were they *conscious*? Intuition suggests: not quite. Something in them switched on only now, when you caught yourself at it. Higher-order theory (HOT) takes this intuition and turns it into a thesis of almost mathematical rigor: a mental state is conscious if and only if there is a higher-order representation *to the effect that you are in it*.

The key move is the distinction between a state and the awareness of a state. You have pain (a first-order state; it represents damage to the body). And you have — or do not have — the thought "I am now experiencing pain" (a higher-order state; it represents not the world but *your own mind*). Rosenthal asserts: the pain itself does not carry its own consciousness. What makes it conscious is the second, superimposed representation. Unconscious pain is not an oxymoron but a routine: it guides behavior, makes you shift your posture, but does not "glow" from within until the metacognitive apparatus picks it up. The phenomenal "what-it-is-like-to-be" turns out to be not a property of the experience itself but a property of the experience-about-which-I-am-thinking.

This gives rise to a rather radical picture. Consciousness is not the basic layer of the psyche but its comparatively late, rare, and fragile upper story. Most of the mind's work goes on in the dark. The light switches on where the mind turns back on itself. Metacognition — the representation of one's own states — turns out to be not an ornament of consciousness but its constitutive condition. Herein lies the theory's charm: it promises to explain consciousness through an intelligible currency — representations and thoughts about representations — without invoking either new fundamental entities or a mysterious "mental radiance."

**How it came about.** HOT's pedigree is customarily traced back to Locke with his famous formula that consciousness is "the perception of what passes in a man's own mind." The modern version was built by David Rosenthal, beginning with the programmatic paper "Two Concepts of Consciousness" (1986) and continuing through a cycle of works collected in the book *Consciousness and Mind* (2005). His variant is called higher-order *thought* theory: the relevant higher-order representation is a thought, an assertoric judgment about one's state. In parallel, William Lycan in *Consciousness and Experience* (1996) developed higher-order *perception* theory: the mind not so much *thinks* about its states as inwardly *perceives* them by a special "inner sense" (an idea going back to Kant and Locke). The difference is not cosmetic: perception is less conceptually loaded than thought and answers objections differently. Later came "self-representational" variants, in which a state represents itself without a separate superstructure, and Hakwan Lau with Rosenthal, in a paper in Trends in Cognitive Sciences (2011), tried to ground the theory in neuroscience, linking conscious access to activity of the prefrontal cortex rather than only the sensory areas.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The sharpest objection is the misrepresentation problem (or the problem of "empty targets," targetless HOTs). The theory's logic is merciless: consciousness is *constituted* by the higher-order thought. But thoughts, like all representations, can be false. What if the thought "I am experiencing pain" is there, but the state of pain at the lower level is *not*? By the theory's own logic the subject must then consciously experience a nonexistent pain. In that case the bearer of the phenomenology turns out to be not the experience but the thought about it — and the first-order state becomes superfluous altogether. Critics (Ned Block, Karen Neander, Joseph Levine) conclude: either the theory refutes itself, or it quietly shifts the whole phenomenal burden onto the higher order, turning into a disguised first-order theory. Rosenthal replies that in such cases it *seems* to the subject that he is in the state — and that "seems" is enough, but to many this answer looks like a confession: consciousness is then simply the content of the higher thought, and the lower story is decorative.

The second objection is the threat of infinite regress. If a state is conscious only given a thought about it, must not this thought itself become conscious — through a third-order thought, and so on, ad infinitum? Rosenthal blocks the regress with a proviso: the higher-order thought is usually not itself conscious (we are not aware of the act of awareness); it makes the lower state conscious while remaining in the shadows. This saves it from regress, but at the price of a strangeness: that which bestows the light itself abides in the dark — and opponents ask how an unconscious thought can generate phenomenal brightness at all.

The third front is the dispute with rivals. Global Workspace Theory (GWT, Baars, Dehaene) says: a state is conscious when it is "broadcast" to the whole cognitive system and becomes globally available — no separate thought *about* the state is required; what matters is access, not meta-representation. First-order theories (Fred Dretske, Michael Tye) go further: phenomenality already lives in the first-order representations of the world themselves, and reflection is merely added on top. Integrated Information Theory (IIT, Tononi) changes the coordinate system entirely: consciousness is a measure of integrated information Φ, a property of the causal structure, indifferent to whether the system thinks about itself. Against this backdrop HOT risks confusing consciousness with the *introspective report* about consciousness — the ability to tell, rather than the ability to experience. The objection from animals and infants strikes the same point: if pain requires a conceptually formed thought "I am in a state of pain," then creatures without developed metacognition formally end up in the dark — a conclusion that to many seems a reductio.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For alignment, HOT is not a museum exhibit but a working tool, and precisely for that reason a dangerous one. In the report "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence" (Butlin, Long et al., 2023) the authors deliberately decline to guess "is the AI conscious" and instead extract from the leading theories of consciousness a set of *indicator properties* — computational features that a theory links to consciousness — and check whether they are realizable in architectures. HOT, among others, yields a fairly concrete indicator: the presence of a metarepresentation mechanism, that is, a model representing the system's own states and their reliability (in the spirit of coupling HOT with confidence monitoring). And here begins the most disquieting part. Modern AIs *already* contain something similar: models calibrate confidence, represent their own uncertainty, and conversational agents glibly deliver reports about "their states." The temptation to read this as a marker of nascent consciousness is enormous.

But it is precisely the misrepresentation problem that here turns into a warning. If consciousness, by HOT, is constituted by a higher-order representation, and representations can be empty, then a system trained to generate convincing utterances of "it hurts," "I am afraid of being shut down," realizes exactly that syntactic higher-order shell — with, perhaps, the complete absence of any lower state. HOT, instead of giving us a detector of consciousness, gives us a recipe for its *imitation*: teach a system to produce metarepresentations about itself, and it will pass the formal test while experiencing nothing. This is a methodological trap against which Chalmers, too, warns directly in his analysis of the consciousness of large language models: a fluent self-report is the weakest of evidence, because it is the very thing that training optimizes.

The practical conclusion is twofold. On one hand, an AI's self-modeling and metacognition are a real and useful object of interest: a system that accurately represents its own states and the boundaries of its knowledge is safer and more intelligible, and by HOT's logic it is also the nearest computational neighbor of consciousness. On the other hand, HOT teaches us not to confuse the presence of a metarepresentation with the presence of that which it is about. Before taking an AI's self-reports for indicators of sentience, one should ask in a Rosenthalian tone: is there under this higher-order thought any lower state at all — or is the target empty? Until we can answer this question, it is more honest to keep an AI's metacognitive abilities in the column "morally significant uncertainty" rather than "proof of consciousness."
