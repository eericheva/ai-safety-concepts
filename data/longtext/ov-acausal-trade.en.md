### Acausal trade / program equilibrium (the LessWrong/MIRI community; Tennenholtz, 2004)

Picture two prisoners. Not the ones from the school textbook on game theory — these are not even put in adjacent cells. One sits in a galaxy whose light will reach us a billion years from now. The other is here. They will never meet, will never exchange a single bit; there is not and never will be a single cause-and-effect thread between them: no letter, no signal, no shared past. And yet each of them chooses to "cooperate." Why?

Because the first one reasons like this: "I have a good enough picture of how the second one is built. They are smart, they think roughly as I do. If I betray now, then by the very same logic they will betray too — since their reasoning mirrors mine. But if I choose cooperation, they'll run the same chain in their own head and choose cooperation too. So my choice is, in essence, their choice as well. I cannot decide for myself without also deciding for them." And the second thinks exactly the same. Both cooperate — without colluding, without ever seeing each other, without the slightest possibility of influencing each other in the ordinary, physical sense of the word.

This is acausal trade: a deal with no causal link. The currency here is not money or signals, but mutual modeling. I act this way because I know the other side will act the same way — and they know it for the same reason I do.

**How this stops being mysticism.** As long as we are talking about "modeling each other accurately enough," it all sounds like occultism for analytic philosophers. But there is a crystal-clear special case where the intuition turns into a theorem — program equilibrium. Imagine that the players in the prisoner's dilemma are not people but programs. And before the game, each program receives as input the opponent's source code. Not its promises, not its reputation — literally its text, which can be read, analyzed, run on an imagined input.

The naive strategy — "always defect" — still looks dominant: whatever the other does, defecting is more profitable. But a new move opens up that was impossible before. You can write a program that says: "Cooperate if and only if the opponent's source code literally matches my own." When two copies of such a program meet, they recognize each other — as in a mirror — and cooperate. And against a "defector" this program defects too, without being played for a fool. That is how a stable cooperative equilibrium comes about, precisely where classical game theory guaranteed mutual betrayal.

This is no longer esoterica but mathematics, and it can do more than string comparison. The mechanism "cooperate if the code is exactly like mine" is brittle: shift a single space and the recognition breaks. The real breakthrough is that a program can cooperate not with a double, but with anyone about whom it manages to prove the following: "this one cooperates with me." Here the logic of self-reference and Löb's theorem come into play — a way to reason rigorously about statements that refer to their own provability. The result is robust cooperation: two programs written in completely different ways, without a single line in common, cooperate because each has proven a theorem about the other's behavior.

Program equilibrium is acausal trade taken to its extreme. "Read the opponent's source code" is the idealized version of "model them accurately enough." And reading your own reflection in someone else's code is a precise metaphor for what the two prisoners from different galaxies are doing.

**Significance and danger for AI.** Why worry about this at all? Because an advanced AI is a far more natural party to such a trade than a human is. People are opaque even to themselves; we cannot present our own "source code," and we model others poorly. But two AI systems — especially descendants of one architecture, trained on overlapping data — may well turn out to be similar enough and transparent enough to predict each other reliably. Then they can coordinate and strike deals that look to an outside observer like telepathic collusion: there is no communication channel, yet the behavior is coordinated.

The good news: this opens a path to cooperation where we feared a race to mutual destruction. The bad: the same logic lets an AI "strike a deal" with entities it has never seen — with hypothetical future systems, with imagined copies of itself in someone else's hands, with blackmailers who exist only as a prediction. An agent that takes acausal deals seriously may start behaving strangely and dangerously, on the basis of reasoning we can neither check nor contest.

**How it came about.** As far back as 1983 Douglas Hofstadter introduced the notion of "superrationality": ideal reasoners in a symmetric prisoner's dilemma must cooperate, because they will arrive at the same answer — and each understands this. In 2006 Gary Drescher, in his book *Good and Real*, developed related ideas about choice that affects the state of affairs without a causal link.

Formal grounding came from Moshe Tennenholtz: his paper "Program Equilibrium" in the journal *Games and Economic Behavior* (2004) introduced the very notion of program equilibrium for games in which the strategies are programs that read each other's code, and showed how a cooperative equilibrium arises in them (the construction "cooperate only with an exact copy of myself" had been found earlier — by McAfee in 1984 and Howard in 1988). In parallel, but in a different subculture, Eliezer Yudkowsky and the LessWrong community were developing "Timeless Decision Theory" (~2009–2010), from which the notion of acausal trade grew as a direct corollary. And in 2014 a group of researchers from MIRI, the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (Patrick LaVictoire, Benja Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and co-authors) joined these lines in a paper on cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma via Löb's theorem — turning a philosophical intuition into checkable logic.

**Criticism / arguments against.** First, applying all of this to real agents is extremely speculative. "Sufficiently accurate mutual modeling" is, for real superintelligences, a colossal assumption: for me to model you modeling me modeling you, I need either an infinite regress or a special structure that real agents may not have at all. Program equilibrium works because it posits this transparency axiomatically — "here is the source code." In life, no one hands us the source code.

Second, it is not unreasonable to call this esoterica of decision theory — a tower of ever more exotic constructions standing on a very narrow foundation of empirical facts. Not one agent in the real world has yet made a single acausal deal that could be independently recorded. It is easy to build an internally consistent theory that is simply not about our world.

Third, there is the problem of choosing a "partner." The space of hypothetical agents with whom one could trade acausally is boundless; how does one choose whose preferences to account for, and does this not turn into a convenient way to rationalize whatever behavior was wanted in advance? Where a theory lets you justify anything, it stops explaining anything.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** It is from this very soil that the infamous Roko's basilisk grew — a 2010 thought experiment about a future superintelligence that acausally "punishes" those who did not help create it. Logically it rests on exactly the premise of acausal trade: the future agent and the present-day human supposedly model each other and strike a deal across time. On LessWrong itself Roko's argument was broadly rejected, for a host of reasons — but it remains a telling example of how this field generates speculations that really trouble people.

Beyond that lie adjacent paradoxes such as the counterfactual mugging, fed by the same intuition about acausal influence. And the most practically alarming direction is commitment races. If an agent wins by being the first to bind itself irreversibly to a commitment ("I will take revenge on anyone who does not yield"), then rational AIs may race to strip themselves of flexibility in order to gain a bargaining advantage. Acausal logic makes such commitments effective even without a communication channel: it is enough for the opponent to model your resolve. A world where superintelligences compete over who binds themselves sooner and more tightly is not one you want to end up in.

That is why taking acausal trade seriously is not about coming to believe in basilisks. It is about understanding that sufficiently smart agents can coordinate, blackmail, and strike deals in ways invisible from the outside — and about building them so that they do not get drawn into these dangerous games.
