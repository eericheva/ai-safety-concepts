### The Linear Representation Hypothesis (Park, Choe, Veitch, 2023–24)

Take a trained language model, catch it in the middle of a line of reasoning, and look inside — at the moment when the context has already turned into a column of several thousand numbers, the activation vector at some middle layer. The linear representation hypothesis asserts something almost indecently simple about this column: high-level concepts — "truth," "gender," "the language of the text," "emotional tone," "I am about to refuse to answer" — are written into it as **directions**. Not as cunning nonlinear patterns, not as ensembles of a thousand interacting neurons, but as straight lines in activation space. "Male→female" has a vector. "True→false" has a vector. And — here is the main promise — along these vectors one can not only *read* but also *write*: add the direction of "refusal" and the model digs in its heels; subtract it and it becomes compliant.

The classic forerunner is known even to those who have never opened a language model: the arithmetic of word2vec. `king − man + woman ≈ queen`. `Paris − France + Italy ≈ Rome`. The vector connecting "man" and "woman" turned out to be roughly the same as the one connecting "king" and "queen" — that is, "gender" lived in the embeddings as a stable offset, the same across the whole space. If this works for words, why should gender, tense, and truth not live as the same kind of directions in a transformer's deep activations too?

#### What exactly is claimed — and why it is so tempting

A careful formulation matters, because "linearity" here is treacherously many-valued. What is meant is that for a binary concept there exists a direction v such that the projection of the activation onto v monotonically encodes the concept's value, and a shift of the activation along v causally changes it without touching the rest. From this come twin instruments. The first — **linear probing**: we train the simplest linear classifier on top of the activations and see whether the concept reads out; if it reads out linearly — the direction is found. The second — **activation steering**: we add the same direction to the activations during generation and steer behavior without any fine-tuning.

The temptation is plain. If the hypothesis is true, interpretability ceases to be hermeneutics and becomes **geometry**. There is no need to decipher "what the model is thinking" by peering into myriads of weights — linear algebra suffices: dot products, projections, orthogonal complements. Control, too, becomes geometry: safety is not only RLHF but also, perhaps, the subtraction of a "toxicity" vector at inference. Beautiful to the point of suspicion.

**How it came about.** The trail runs from distributional semantics. Mikolov and co-authors in 2013 showed regularities in word2vec — those very parallelogram analogies. It then emerged that the trick is not limited to lexical embeddings. Alain and Bengio (2016) proposed linear probes as a way of seeing what a network's intermediate layers "know." Then interpretability sensed linear structures in the most unexpected places: Nanda and colleagues (2023) discovered that a model trained merely to predict moves in Othello holds inside a linearly readable board state — and in the coordinates of "my piece / opponent's piece," not "black / white." Marks and Tegmark (2023), in "The Geometry of Truth," found a direction of truth: along it true and false statements separate linearly, and a shift along it changes what the model takes to be true. Turner and co-authors (2023) turned this into an engineering device — Activation Addition, steering without optimization. And then Park, Choe, and Veitch (2023–24) gave this scatter of observations a theoretical foundation: they carefully teased apart three different senses of the word "representation" (subspace, measurement by a probe, intervention), introduced the notion of the **causal inner product** — a metric in which causally separable concepts turn out orthogonal — and showed under what conditions a "direction" is a correct object and not an artifact of the chosen coordinate system. From folklore ("king minus man") the hypothesis became a formulable statement.

**Criticism / arguments against.** Here the interesting part begins, and the bestiary loves exactly this part.

First, **not everything is linear**. Engels, Liao, Michaud, Gurnee, and Tegmark (2024) titled their work bluntly "Not All Language Model Features Are Linear" and showed *multi-dimensional* features that no straight line can catch. The canonical example is cyclicity: the days of the week and the months the model lays out not along a ruler but along a **circle**. And logically so — for "Monday" and "Sunday" the neighborhood is cyclic, not "maximum minus minimum," and the honest geometric representation of such a concept is a circle, a two-dimensional feature, not a one-dimensional axis. Adding a "vector" to it is meaningless: along a circle there is no global direction "forward."

Second, **linearity may be partial and approximate**. The word2vec analogies do not always work and work worse on rare words; a truth direction found on one set of facts sometimes does not transfer to another — that is, we may be catching not "truth in general" but a correlate like "sounds plausible" or a peculiarity of the dataset. This is the flip side of **superposition** (Elhage et al., "Toy Models of Superposition," 2022): the model has more features than dimensions, they are packed non-orthogonally, and a naively extracted direction is almost always a mixture of several concepts. Tug at "politeness," and "formality" will move too — and so will "response length."

Third, the subtlest risk — **illusory interpretability**. A linear probe is trainable and greedy: give it enough dimensions and it will find a separating hyperplane for almost anything, even if the model itself does *not* causally use that direction. High probe accuracy proves that the information is linearly *present* in the activations, but not that the model *relies* on it. Hence a methodological discipline: probing is checked by intervention (shift along the direction — did behavior change predictably?), and steering results are checked against "placebo directions" and side effects.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is neither "the hypothesis is true" nor "the hypothesis is false," but "linearity is a strong but leaky working model, and one must know its limits." A whole industry already stands on it: linear detector-probes (is the model lying? about to refuse? hallucinating?), activation steering as a cheap alternative to fine-tuning, representation engineering as a top-down approach to transparency. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs), the current hit of mechanistic interpretability, are the direct heirs of the hypothesis: they try to *decompose* superposition back into monosemantic linear features, in essence taking linearity as a working postulate and fighting its chief enemy — the entanglement of directions.

Where does this lead for safety? To cautious optimism with two caveats. The first caveat: do not take a found direction for a concept until you have checked it causally and for transferability; a beautiful parallelogram is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis. The second caveat: keep in mind the nonlinear and multi-dimensional "tail" — cyclic, hierarchical, branching features that a one-dimensional axis simply does not see; it is there, rather than in the convenient linear part, that whatever we end up missing is more likely to be hiding. The linear hypothesis gave alignment the dream that a machine's thoughts can be read with a ruler and edited by addition. The dream half came true — and the second half is precisely the map of where our rulers lie.
