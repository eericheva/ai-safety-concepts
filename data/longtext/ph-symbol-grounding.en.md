### The Symbol Grounding Problem (Stevan Harnad, 1990)

Imagine you have decided to learn Chinese — and all you have at your disposal is a dictionary, entirely in Chinese. You open it at the first character. Its meaning is explained through five other characters. You look those up — each is explained through a fresh handful of unfamiliar signs. You go on, and on, page after page, and at some point you realize with a chill: you will never get out. The circle cannot be broken. Signs refer endlessly to signs, definitions lead to definitions, and nowhere — on no page — does this carousel touch the ground. Not once were you shown a real dog beside the character for "dog," not once given a taste of what is called "bitter." You can spin this little machine forever and still never learn what it is even talking about.

It is precisely this trap that Stevan Harnad in 1990 named **the symbol grounding problem**. The formulation is deceptively simple: how do a system's internal symbols acquire meaning — rather than remaining empty marks defined only through other marks of the same kind? A symbol in a formal system is just a squiggle we manipulate by rules, looking at its shape and not at its sense. The rules are syntactically flawless: they tell you what to do with the mark but say nothing about what it refers to in the world. And if every symbol is defined only through other symbols, the whole system hangs in the air. It is internally coherent and semantically empty at once — like that very Chinese-Chinese dictionary that knows everything about its characters and nothing about the world.

Why is this a central question for the dispute over "do LLMs understand"? Because a large language model is, to a first approximation, exactly a gigantic self-enclosed dictionary. It is trained on texts: on the form of language, on the statistics of token co-occurrence. A word is defined for it precisely by the company of other words it keeps. "Cat" is what stands beside "purrs," "claws," "milk." But the model has never stroked a cat, never jerked its hand away from claws, never watched one lap up its milk. If Harnad is right, then all its erudition is that same unending circle of definitions, syntax with no exit to a referent. And then the model's "understanding" is an illusion projected by us, the readers, who are in fact grounded and therefore read meaning into a place where there is none.

**How it came about.** The problem grew directly out of John Searle's thought experiment — out of the "Chinese Room" of 1980. Searle imagined himself locked in a room: slips of paper with Chinese characters are pushed under the door, and he has a thick rulebook in English telling him which character to hand back for which. From outside it seems the room speaks fluent Chinese. Inside is a man who understands not a word, mechanically consulting the rules. Searle's moral: manipulating symbols by their form (syntax) neither produces nor guarantees meaning (semantics). A program can pass the Turing test and still understand nothing.

Harnad, a psychologist and cognitive scientist, took this intuition and turned the rhetorical blow into a well-defined engineering problem. Instead of "the machine has no consciousness" he asked, more concretely: where could a symbol's meaning come from in the first place — what would a system have to be able to do for its marks to cease being empty? In his paper "The Symbol Grounding Problem" (Physica D, 1990) he offered a sketch of an answer as well. Symbols, he wrote, must be grounded bottom-up — on two levels of nonsymbolic representations. The first, iconic: analog imprints of the sensory projection, traces of how a thing looks, sounds, feels. The second, categorical: learned and innate features that let one distinguish one type of thing from another and reliably name them. And only elementary symbols, nailed to these categories through perception, can then combine into complex ones — such that meaning rises up from the ground rather than circulating in the void. Note the lineage: the old philosophical idea of intentionality stands behind Harnad too — the "aboutness" of thought, which Franz Brentano back in the nineteenth century called the mark of the mental. A real thought is always about something. An empty symbol is about nothing.

**Criticism of language-only models.** The sharpest and most contemporary blow was struck by the linguists Emily Bender and Alexander Koller in a paper with a telling title, "Climbing towards NLU" (ACL, 2020). Their question, put bluntly: can meaning be learned from form alone at all? Their answer is no, and they tell a parable about an octopus: a superintelligent deep-sea octopus splices into a telegraph cable between two people on different islands. It knows no English, but it is a brilliant statistician: over time it listens to their correspondence and learns all the patterns of who says what in reply to what. At some point it cuts the wire and begins answering one of the interlocutors itself, imitating the other perfectly. The deception holds for a long time — until one day the person, facing a bear attack, panics and asks for advice on how to build a weapon out of the sticks they have grabbed. The octopus falls apart. It has never seen a bear or a stick; it has the form of the words but no connection to the things the words point to. At the critical moment, one demanding a real referent, the statistics of form is powerless. Bender and Koller's octopus is exactly Harnad's ungrounded symbol, only in the costume of a neural network. A kindred reproach comes from the "stochastic parrots" paper (Bender, Gebru, and co-authors, 2021): the model stitches together plausible sequences with no reference to meaning behind them — a parrot repeating words rather than saying them.

Out of this criticism grows a positive program: grounding must be done through a body and through several modalities at once. If meaning comes from the coupling of language with perception and action, then a one-legged textual stool cannot stand — vision, sound, touch, movement are needed. Multimodality and embodiment as a way to nail words to the world — that is where Harnad's finger points, extended a third of a century forward.

But the criticism and its program each have a weak side. First, a counterargument from language itself: people constantly understand words they have never "grounded" perceptually — "justice," "entropy," "the seventh dimension." Much meaning we draw precisely from the fabric of language, from a word's connections with other words, and perhaps the circle of definitions is not so hopeless as it seems, if it is rich enough and tied to rare points of contact with the world. Second, multimodal models that see and hear already exist — and yet it is unclear whether the extra channels have made "meaning" more real or have merely added correlating forms: an image, after all, also arrives in the model as a stream of numbers, as one more dictionary, albeit a pixel-based one. Is this grounding — or the same circle, only wider?

**What to do about it / where it leads.** One proposed route is to build machines that learn predictive models of the world. Yann LeCun, in his programmatic note "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence" (2022), proposed the JEPA architecture — Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture. The idea is that the model should predict not the future pixels or words themselves but their abstract representations, and should build an internal model of how the world is arranged and how it changes — from observation and, in the limit, from action. This is a direct bet on embodied AI: an agent that has sensors, whose actions have consequences, and that has a feedback loop with reality — and whose internal symbols therefore have a chance of attaching to something beyond their own carousel. In spirit this is an attempt to fulfill Harnad's bequest by engineering means: to give the marks ground under their feet.

The symbol grounding problem is not solved; it has, rather, moved house. From a philosophical objection to AI it has turned into a compass by which people argue about what present-day models lack and whether the next leap will add understanding to them. Every time some system produces something frighteningly meaningful and then falls apart on a banal fact about the physical world, we hear the same question Harnad asked in 1990: are you holding meaning — or still leafing through an endless dictionary, where one word is explained by another, and no ground is in sight?
