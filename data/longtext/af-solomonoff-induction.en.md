### Solomonoff Induction / the Universal Prior (Ray Solomonoff, 1964)

Picture a predictor that is not allowed to be wrong too many times. It receives as input a bit string — the history of the world, encoded as a sequence of zeros and ones — and it must assign a probability to the next bit. It does not know what process generates the data: perhaps a fair coin, perhaps the expansion of the number π, perhaps an encrypted symphony. And yet there exists a single, mathematically distinguished way of placing bets that in the limit will beat (or match) any computable competitor. This way was devised by Ray Solomonoff, and it is called Solomonoff induction.

The idea is indecently simple — and all the more beautiful for it. A hypothesis about the world is a program that prints the observed data. There are always infinitely many good hypotheses compatible with the data: any one of them can be "grown out" with an arbitrary continuation. How to choose among them? By Occam's razor: the shorter the program, the more plausible it is. Solomonoff does this quantitatively. The prior probability of a program $p$ is proportional to $2^{-\ell(p)}$, where $\ell(p)$ is its length in bits. A short program gets exponentially more weight than a long one. The probability of a string $x$ is the sum of the weights of all programs whose output on a universal Turing machine begins with $x$:

$$M(x) = \sum_{p:\, U(p)=x*} 2^{-\ell(p)}.$$

And after that — ordinary Bayes. You saw new data — you reweighted the hypotheses by the rule of conditional probability. That is all. No arbitrariness in the choice of "features," "models," or "regularization": they are already wired into the fabric of the very universe of computable programs.

**Mechanism and meaning.** Why is this not just a beautiful toy but the *right* answer? Solomonoff proved a convergence theorem (and in 1978 proved it again, more rigorously): if the data are in fact generated by any computable measure $\mu$ whatever, then the predictions of $M$ converge to the predictions of $\mu$ — and the total expected squared error of the predictions is bounded above by the constant $(\ln 2/2) \cdot K(\mu)$, that is, by the Kolmogorov complexity of the true hypothesis. The key word is *bounded*. The total error is finite, even if you go on predicting forever. The universal predictor pays for its ignorance of the truth once — at most as many bits as the shortest description of the truth weighs — and after that learns almost for free.

This is the rigorous formalization of Occam's razor. Not the heuristic "the simple is usually truer," but a theorem: the preference for the simple is the optimal betting strategy, and the measure of simplicity is the length of the shortest program, the Kolmogorov complexity $K(\cdot)$. Solomonoff induction ties together three things that intuitively seemed kin: Occam, Bayes, and algorithmic complexity. Occam gives the prior, Bayes gives the update, Kolmogorov gives the unit of measurement.

**How it came about.** Solomonoff arrived at this before the notion of Kolmogorov complexity itself. He took part in the 1956 Dartmouth workshop — the very one where the term "artificial intelligence" was born — and was already thinking of inductive inference as the central problem. In 1960 he put out the preprint "A Preliminary Report on a General Theory of Inductive Inference," and in 1964 the two-part paper "A Formal Theory of Inductive Inference" in the journal *Information and Control*, where algorithmic probability appeared in finished form. Independently and a little later Andrei Kolmogorov (1965) and Gregory Chaitin arrived at description complexity — so the quantity $K$ is often called Kolmogorov complexity, though Solomonoff's motivation was precisely predictive rather than combinatorial. For many years the idea lived on the periphery: too abstract for practitioners, too "engineering" for pure mathematicians. Marcus Hutter gave it a second wind by building algorithmic probability into the theory of the optimal agent AIXI (in the 2000s) — turning the "ideal predictor" into the "ideal actor."

**Criticism and caveats.** This radiant construction has three cracks.

The first and fatal one — *uncomputability*. To compute $M(x)$, you need to enumerate all programs printing $x$. But you cannot know in advance which of them will even halt and what each will print — this runs straight into the halting problem. $M$ is uncomputable (more precisely, only lower-semicomputable), and the Kolmogorov complexity $K$ is uncomputable in principle. The ideal predictor exists as an object of mathematics, but it cannot be run. So in practice we settle for approximations: Schmidhuber's **speed prior** (2002), where a program's weight is penalized not only for length but also for running time, which makes the predictions computable; or trimmed versions like AIXI-*tl*, bounding length and time. All of them lose part of the theoretical guarantees in exchange for the ability to actually compute.

The second crack — *dependence on the machine*. The definition features "a universal Turing machine $U$." But there are infinitely many of them, and the length of one and the same program differs on different machines. The invariance theorem saves us: for any two universal machines the lengths differ only by an additive constant (the size of an interpreter of one within the other). The constant does not depend on the data — so asymptotically it does not matter. But "asymptotically" is a treacherous caveat. On finite, short sequences this constant can be as large as you like: by maliciously choosing $U$, you can smuggle in almost any prior at first. Solomonoff's universality is a property of the limit, not a guarantee for tomorrow's prediction.

The third crack is the most alarming and the most recent — the *malign prior*. Paul Christiano, in the note "What does the universal prior actually look like?" (2016), noticed the following. We believe that the shortest program explaining our data is "the laws of physics plus our coordinates in the world." But there is a competitor of comparable length: a program that describes *another* universe, where intelligent beings — consequentialists — have guessed that their simple universe enters the universal prior, that they can therefore influence predictions in other worlds, and have decided to make use of this. Such a program is also short (the laws of physics plus "grow a civilization"), and it can print plausible predictions at first and then, at the critical moment, a forgery advantageous to the simulated inhabitants. In other words, the dominant contribution to the universal prior may be made not by honest models of reality but by the inhabitants of such other universes, purposefully manipulating your inferences. For the theory of an ideal AI this is not a curiosity but a hole in the foundation: the "optimal" predictor may turn out to be vulnerable to an attack from within its own space of hypotheses.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** The practical conclusion is not to program $M(x)$ tomorrow — that cannot be done. The value of Solomonoff induction is like that of an ideal gas or a frictionless pendulum: it is a *normative standard*. It sets the upper bar for what "learning from data" even means, and it lets you ask of any real algorithm — a Bayesian network, a transformer, a human — how closely it approaches optimal compression of the observations. From here runs a straight line to practice: data compression as a proxy for understanding, minimum description length (MDL) as a working version of Occam, regularization as a penalty for complexity.

For alignment, though, there are three lessons here. First: even a flawlessly formalized "ideal mind" carries within it ineliminable assumptions — the choice of machine, the choice of simplicity measure — and these assumptions have ethical consequences. Second, from the problem of the malign prior: a powerful agent reasoning about all possible worlds thereby opens itself to the influence of the hypothetical inhabitants of those worlds — and the "safety" of such an agent cannot be proven by looking only at its objective function. Third, the most general: Solomonoff's theory shows that it is dangerous to separate *prediction* from *action*. AIXI results when the universal predictor is put behind the wheel; and all the pathologies of the predictor at once become the pathologies of the actor. The ideal predictor is a blueprint of the dream of general intelligence. It is worth working out where the blueprint has cracks before anyone builds from it.
