### Accident versus Misuse (Zwetsloot & Dafoe, 2019)

Picture three ways a dam can kill a town downstream. First: there was a crack in the concrete, the engineers overlooked it, the water found a way — nobody wanted disaster, the disaster happened on its own. Second: an enemy planted a charge and blew the sluice — the harm was someone's goal. And the third — the most uncomfortable — is this: the dam was built too high and too fast, because the neighboring state was building its own, and to yield in the race meant losing water, investors, elections. Nobody planted a charge. Nobody overlooked a crack. Everyone acted sensibly — and that is precisely why the result was a catastrophe. This third dam is what almost always drops out of the conversation about AI.

The classic framing splits the risks of artificial intelligence in two. **Accident** — the system causes harm that nobody wanted: an alignment failure, a wrongly specified goal, a side effect of optimization, a hallucination in a medical chatbot. Almost the entire technical alignment agenda falls here: goal specification, robustness, interpretability, reward hacking. **Misuse** — people deliberately apply AI for ill: disinformation campaigns, autonomous weapons, automated cyberattacks, total surveillance, deepfakes. Here the question is not "will the model break" but "in whose hands is it."

Remco Zwetsloot and Allan Dafoe, in an article for *Lawfare*, said something simple but uncomfortable: this dichotomy is not enough. It leaves a huge blind spot — risks that can be blamed neither on a bug nor on a villain. They called this **structural risk**: harm born of the systemic dynamics into which AI is embedded as an accelerant and multiplier. A race to the bottom on safety between labs and between great powers. The erosion of employment and the concentration of power in the hands of those who own the compute. The destabilization of the strategic balance when AI compresses the time to decide on a nuclear response. Competitive pressure that systematically undermines caution — not because anyone is evil, but because the cautious one loses.

The value of the framing is that it is not a philosophical classification but a map of how effort is distributed. Each category has its own "instrument." An accident is cured by technical alignment: make the model do what we meant. Misuse — by access control, structured release, defense, attribution, norms. And structural risk is remedied by neither the first nor the second — it demands governance: institutions, international coordination, treaties, mechanisms that change the very incentives of the players. Three diagnoses — three medicines. And whoever gets the diagnosis wrong treats the wrong disease.

**How it came about.** The two-part framing grew out of engineering culture. In 2016 Dario Amodei and co-authors released "Concrete Problems in AI Safety" — a manifesto that set the vocabulary of "accidents": negative side effects, reward hacking, safe exploration, distributional shift. This was accident-centrism in pure form: risk as a technical malfunction that can be formalized and fixed. In 2018 came the collective report "The Malicious Use of Artificial Intelligence" — twenty-six authors from the Future of Humanity Institute, OpenAI, and Cambridge outlined the second half: AI as a weapon in the hands of states, criminals, terrorists. The two poles — accident and misuse — took shape and drifted into different communities: the techies fixed models, the security specialists caught bad actors.

Zwetsloot and Dafoe, then affiliated with Oxford's Center for the Governance of AI, noticed the gap between the poles. Their 2019 article grew out of Dafoe's broader governance agenda (his "AI Governance: A Research Agenda" had come out a year earlier) and was a direct response to the fact that both dominant framings — "fix the model" and "catch the villain" — are methodologically individualist. Both look for a culprit: either a faulty agent or a malicious one. Yet the most serious historical catastrophes — arms races, climate, financial crises — often have no culprit at all. They are structural. That is how the third category got its name.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and most serious objection: the boundaries are blurred, and in a real catastrophe all three types are mixed. A bad actor exploits an alignment bug during a race that forced a lab to release a model early — where in all this is the pure accident, the pure misuse, the pure structure? The categories are not compartments but lenses; the same incident looks different through different lenses, and trying to slap a single label on an event coarsens the picture. The authors' own framing points the same way: structure often describes not a separate cause but the conditions under which accidents and misuse become more likely and more dangerous.

The second objection is sharper, and it is an ethical one. The language of "accident" has a way of stripping away responsibility. "It was an accident," "the system behaved unexpectedly," "no one could have known" — these are formulations with which corporations and states have for centuries laundered entirely preventable harm. To call an emission, a catastrophe, or an algorithm's discrimination an "accident" is tacitly to bracket out those who chose not to test, not to slow down, not to look. Many "AI accidents" are in fact structural irresponsibility, dressed up as technical inevitability.

The third is practical and therefore the most bitter. Structural risk is the hardest of all to operationalize, measure, and fund. Under "fix alignment" you can set a benchmark, hand out a grant, defend a dissertation. Under "catch misuse" you can write an access policy. But under "rebuild the incentives of international competition so that caution stops being a losing strategy" — where do you start? There is no metric, no owner of the problem, no quarterly report. And so structural risk, while perhaps the most important, remains a chronically underfunded orphan — even though it is precisely what determines the kind of world in which the first two medicines are applied at all.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The framing is useful above all as a detector of one's own blind spots. If all your safety work is about model alignment, you are an accident-centrist and do not see that a perfectly aligned AI used in a race still leads to catastrophe. If all the work is about access control and misuse, you are a misuse-centrist and miss coordination failures, where there is no culprit but there is a corpse. The practical move is simple: take any harm scenario and run it through all three questions. What broke in the system? Who used it, and why? And — what dynamics made system failure likely and misuse profitable?

It is precisely the third question that leads straight to Moloch — Scott Alexander's metaphor for coordination failures, where each participant, acting rationally, sacrifices the common good to competition, and no one can stop alone. Structural risk is Moloch armed with AI: not an evil demon and not a stupid machine, but the impersonal logic of the race, which burns caution as fuel. And the cure for it is not better code and not a better firewall, but institutions, treaties, norms, mechanisms of mutual verification; all that boring governance housekeeping that turns "to yield is to lose" into "to yield safely, because everyone yields."

For the researcher, the framing leads to an audit of the portfolio: are all the eggs in the accident basket? For the lab — to the recognition that its own speed is a structural contribution to risk, not merely a source of products. For the regulator — to a shift of focus from "we'll punish the culprit after the fact" to "we'll change the incentives beforehand." Zwetsloot and Dafoe's framing gives no answers — it redistributes attention. And its main lesson is that the most likely way to lose the future with AI is not an evil genius and not a fatal bug, but a room full of not-stupid people, each of whom acts sensibly, and all of whom together drive off a cliff, because to brake first is too expensive.
