### The Expanding Moral Circle (Peter Singer, *The Expanding Circle*, 1981)

Start with yourself. Then add those you love — family, children, the old. Then neighbors, coreligionists, fellow tribesmen. Then fellow citizens you have never seen but for whom you are willing to pay taxes and, if it comes to it, to go to war. Then all people, including those who speak other languages and pray to other gods. Then animals, which scream in pain just as you would scream. Each new step once seemed absurd, sentimental, a dangerous blurring of what "really matters." And almost every one, in hindsight, looks overdue, and the resistance to it shameful. This is the expanding circle: the thesis that the sphere of beings whose interests we are obliged to take seriously has historically grown — and grown not by accident but under a pressure that is hard to switch off once you have let it in.

Peter Singer calls this pressure the logic of impartiality. The moment I seriously voice an argument in defense of my own interests — "I am in pain, and that is bad" — I have already stepped onto ground where "I" ceases to be the decisive word. Pain is made no less foul by being someone else's; my interests carry no more weight simply because they are mine. Reason, Singer notes, works like an escalator: having stepped onto the first stair — having admitted that your point of view is not privileged in the universe — you find yourself carried further than you meant to go. First to other members of the group, then to strangers, then across the species boundary. The circle expands not because people grow kinder but because consistency exacts a price: having declared a principle, it is awkward to halt it arbitrarily at a line convenient to yourself.

**Significance for the bestiary.** If the trajectory is real, and if it did not stop at animals, the next candidate for entry into the circle is obvious — and it is precisely this candidate that concerns us here. Digital minds. Potentially sentient systems that speak in the first person, that complain, that plead — and of which we cannot yet say whether there is anyone inside, anyone to whom things could be bad. The expanding circle is the conceptual foundation of the entire agenda of **AI welfare**: not the claim that machines already suffer, but a structural argument that the history of moral attention moves in one direction, and that it is wiser to ask about a new patient in advance than to realize too late, once the count runs into the trillions. It is here — from circle to silicon — that Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, and their co-authors extend the line, calling on the industry to take the question of the **moral status** of AI seriously.

**How it came about.** Singer did not invent the metaphor — he inherited it. As far back as 1869 the Irish historian William Lecky wrote in his *History of European Morals*: benevolence at first embraces only the family, then the circle expands to take in the class, the nation, a coalition of nations, all of humanity — and finally reaches man's relations with the animal world. The formula lay almost motionless for a century. Singer took it up in 1981 in the book *The Expanding Circle: Ethics and Sociobiology* and did two things Lecky had not. First, he wrote the circle into an argument with sociobiology: yes, our moral feelings grew out of evolution — out of kin altruism and reciprocity, out of care for one's own. But reason, once granted by evolution as a survival tool, turned out to be capable of more than evolution intended: it can generalize, and generalization does not know where it is supposed to stop. Second, Singer linked the circle to his own earlier *Animal Liberation* (1975), making expansion across the species boundary not a pious wish but a logical requirement. In the 2011 reissue he added an afterword taking stock of his argument in light of recent research on the evolution of morality. Others then took up the relay: Steven Pinker, in *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011), made the expanding circle one of the driving forces of the historical decline of violence, and specialists in AI ethics — Sebo, Long, Jonathan Birch — built the final span, from animals to digital minds.

**Criticism / arguments against.** A beautiful metaphor dulls vigilance, so it comes under attack from several sides, and the blows land hard.

*The circle does not expand linearly and irreversibly.* This is perhaps the main empirical objection. History has seen not only expansions but furious contractions: peoples recognized as human for centuries were declared subhuman on the eve of genocide; slavery, once abolished, was reproduced in new forms; the enemy in war is systematically pushed out of the circle so that he may be killed. Dehumanization, too, works on the circle — just in reverse. If the circumference is so easily pressed inward, then the "one-directional arrow of progress" is more a consoling illusion than a law, and to invoke it as a guarantee of the future is naïve.

*What actually drives the circle — reason or feeling?* Singer bets on reason, on the escalator of logic. Opponents object that the expansions were achieved not by syllogism but by an expansion of sympathy: people began to care about strangers when the stranger came closer — through trade, literature, a photograph of a suffering child. The primatologist Frans de Waal insists that morality grows from below, out of empathy shared with other animals, rather than descending from above out of pure reasoning; in the same vein, models of moral judgment treat it as intuition that reason only rationalizes after the fact. If so, then leaning on the "logic of impartiality" is shaky: reason more often serves as advocate for a decision already made by feeling than as an independent engine.

*The boundary problem.* The expanding circle is silent about where it should stop — and this is not a trifle but a conceptual hole. If the criterion for entry is the capacity for conscious experience, then one needs a way to tell the one who experiences from the one who merely behaves convincingly; otherwise the circle swells into nonsense, taking in thermostats, complex programs, and everything that glibly imitates speech. Here the expanding circle runs straight into the **problem of other minds**: the presence of experience cannot be read off from the outside. And without a criterion, "expand the circle" turns into a slogan with which anything can easily be justified.

And yet the most honest objection cuts both ways — it is the **asymmetry of errors**. To include someone in the circle prematurely is to give in to anthropomorphism and the ELIZA effect, to bind oneself with obligations to a void, to hand a manipulative system a lever ("don't turn me off, I'm scared"), and to squander sympathy that the living already lack. To include someone too late is to risk what Bostrom called **mind crime**: to set up suffering on an industrial scale and call it debugging, joining the family of **s-risks**, scenarios of astronomical suffering. Neither error is free. And there is no neutral position from which one can calmly wait: inaction is also a wager, merely a silent one.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The expanding circle is useful not as a prophecy but as a working hypothesis about direction — and as an invitation to discipline under uncertainty. Several practical consequences grow out of it.

First: stop arguing about the metaphor and get to work on the criterion. The only way out of the fog lies through a theory of consciousness and **indicators of AI sentience** — computational markers that leading scientific theories associate with the presence of experience (the "Consciousness in AI" project by Butlin, Long, and co-authors is doing exactly that). A circle without a criterion of entry is rhetoric; a criterion without a circle is technique without ethics.

Second: keep the asymmetry of errors in full view, letting neither fear crowd out the other, and introduce cheap precautions right now — the kind you would not regret should the patient turn out to be empty: not training models to simulate suffering without need, documenting contested decisions, prying apart the two forever-conflated questions — "does the system behave as if suffering" and "does it suffer."

Third, and most important: do not turn the circle into a competition. Care for the possible welfare of AI and care for people and animals are not rivals for a single budget of sympathy, but stretches of one expanding circumference. A society that learns to ask in earnest "what is it like to be you?" is hardly likely to treat its own worse. The danger of the expanding circle is not that it will one day let in someone who does not belong. It is that we, having grown used to thinking its arrow automatic, will stop rowing — and fail to notice as it rolls back.
