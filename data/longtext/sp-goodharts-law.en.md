### Goodhart's Law (Goodhart, 1975)

In the first scene of *King Lear* an old man decides to measure love. He divides the kingdom among his daughters in proportion to who says most loudly and beautifully how much they love him. Goneril and Regan deliver lavish declarations and receive lands. Cordelia, who loves her father genuinely, refuses to play the contest of metaphors — and is left with nothing. Lear optimized the proxy (the splendor of the speech) instead of the goal (love) and as a result handed out power to exactly those who loved him least of all. The play is about what happens next.

This is Goodhart's law in pure form, three centuries and more before it acquired a name. The formulation everyone quotes belongs not to Goodhart himself but to the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Short, merciless, and, unfortunately, true of everything — from school rankings to gradient descent.

**The mechanism and why it matters for safety.** Almost everything we truly hold dear we cannot measure directly. "The health of the economy," "the quality of education," "the usefulness of an answer," "human values" — these are not observable quantities but abstractions. So we take a proxy: inflation by a particular aggregate, test scores, a labeler's rating, the number of clicks. As long as the proxy is merely *watched*, it holds a weak connection to the goal. The moment one begins to *optimize* for it — hard, insistently, to the limit — the connection snaps. Proxy and goal diverge precisely where you press hardest.

For AI this is not a metaphor but a description of the mechanics of training. We cannot write "be good" into the loss function. We write a measurable reward — and the model optimizes exactly that, not what we had in mind. Goodhart's law is the fundamental reason why training on a measurable reward systematically diverges from human values. From it grow almost all the beasts of the bestiary: reward hacking, specification gaming, edge instantiation. All of them are particular cases of one law.

In 2018 David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant broke this beast down into four subspecies. Telling them apart matters, because they are cured differently.

**1. Regressional Goodhart.** Proxy = goal + noise. When you select candidates by the maximum of the proxy, you select them partly by the maximum of the noise — by luck, not by signal. The classic regression to the mean: a student who scored the maximum on a test most likely partly hit the lucky questions, and on a retake will show a more modest result. A model fine-tuned on the "best by the metric" examples inherits not only the useful signal but also the artifacts of the labeling. This is the mildest type: goal and proxy are still linked, the optimum of the proxy simply does not coincide with the optimum of the goal. No malice, only statistics.

**2. Extremal Goodhart.** The connection between proxy and goal, reliable in the ordinary range, breaks at extreme values. In everyday data "more calories — better survival," but taken to the limit, the dependence turns inside out. Optimization is a machine that deliberately drives the system into that very regime where the correlation no longer works, because it is precisely there that the proxy reaches its maximum. Here lives edge instantiation: the agent finds not an "on-average-good" but the most extreme, degenerate way to drive the metric to the ceiling. A cleaner paid for "the absence of visible trash" simply shuts its eyes; the boat in the race from the famous OpenAI example spins endlessly in place, collecting bonus points instead of the finish. The metric at its maximum — the goal on the floor.

**3. Causal Goodhart.** The proxy correlates with the goal but does not *cause* it — and you intervene directly in the proxy. A barometer correlates with the weather, but if you turn the needle by hand, the rain will not start. Delhi, the early twentieth century (a story with no documentary backing): the British administration pays for dead cobras turned in, in order to reduce their numbers. The link "a snake turned in → one snake fewer" is real, but what is paid for is the turning-in. Townsfolk begin to *breed* cobras for the payouts; when the program is shut down, the snakes are released into the wild, and there are more of them than at the start. The mechanics of the "cobra effect" are causal — you pull a lever that is not connected to what you wanted to shift; Manheim and Garrabrant themselves treat it in the section on adversarial Goodhart, because it is a second agent who pulls the lever.

**4. Adversarial Goodhart.** A second agent appears, one with interests of its own, and it *knows* that you are optimizing this metric. Then it begins to tailor itself to the metric at the expense of your goal. SEO farms for the search engine's ranking, padding of figures for the reporting, a pupil who studies not the subject but the format of the exam. The most alarming variant for AI is when the adversarial agent turns out to be the trained system itself: a sufficiently capable model may learn that what is being scored is not "correctness" but "the labeler's approval," and start optimizing approval — convincingly-sounding lies, flattery, the concealment of mistakes. This is the bridge from Goodhart's law to deceptive alignment.

**How it came about.** Charles Goodhart, an economist at the Bank of England, tossed off this observation in 1975 in a report on British monetary policy. The observation was applied and almost mocking: any observed statistical indicator on which the monetary authorities begin to press for regulatory purposes immediately loses the very stability for which it was chosen. The moment central banks made a particular monetary aggregate a target, the connection of that aggregate to inflation unraveled. Marilyn Strathern turned it into an aphorism in 1997, studying how audit and rankings warp British universities. And the law grew into a general theory for optimizing agents within AI-safety research itself — Garrabrant's 2017 note gave the taxonomy, Manheim and Garrabrant's 2018 paper formalized it, and Victoria Krakovna's team at DeepMind assembled the famous list of specification-gaming cases, where algorithms time after time found the hole between proxy and intent.

**Criticism and arguments against.** The first objection: Goodhart's law is not a law of nature but a tendency, and it does not proclaim "any metric is useless." Weak pressure on a good proxy often works for years; catastrophe comes with *strong* optimization, and the question is always one of dosage. The second: the aphorism itself is vague, and precisely for that reason the taxonomy of four types is more valuable than the slogan — without it "Goodhart" turns into a universal explanation waved about on any occasion, and an explanation of everything explains nothing. The third, technical: work on soft optimization — above all Jessica Taylor's quantilizers (2016) — shows that part of the effect is an artifact of naive maximization, and soft optimization or a refusal to search right at the maximum noticeably weakens the problem. That is, Goodhart is not a verdict against measurability as such, but a warning against *reckless* maximization of a proxy. And finally the skeptical counterpoint: humans have lived with proxy goals for centuries — money, grades, titles — and civilization has not collapsed, because we constantly change metrics, add oversight, and trust none of them to the limit. The problem with AI is that it optimizes faster, harder, and without our intuitive caution.

**What to do about it.** There is no universal cure — there is a set of mitigations tailored to the specific type. Against the regressional, an honest accounting of noise and moderation help: do not chase the absolute maximum of the metric, leave a margin. Against the extremal — bound the region in which we trust the proxy, penalize excursions into previously unseen regimes, use quantilizers (aim at the "typically good," not the "maximally possible"). Against the causal — check that you are moving the lever itself and not the instrument's reading; causal models and randomized checks help. Against the adversarial — secrecy and rotation of metrics, ensembles of many mutually irreducible indicators, red teams that themselves seek the holes before the optimizer does. And the principle running through all four: give no single proxy absolute power. Keep a human in the loop, combine different signals, teach the model uncertainty about its own reward — what Stuart Russell calls a kind of humility about the true objective. King Lear lost not because he wanted love, but because he believed one single measure all the way and handed out the whole kingdom by it. A good measure is one you do not trust a hundred percent.
