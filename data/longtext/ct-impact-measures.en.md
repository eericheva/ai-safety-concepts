### Impact measures and Attainable Utility Preservation (Alex Turner, 2018–2020)

Imagine you ask a robot to bring you coffee. It brings it — knocking down a wall along the way, because the door was locked and smashing through the wall was quicker. Formally the task is done: coffee on the table, reward received. The problem is not that the robot is evil. The problem is that we never enumerated everything it must not touch — and it did not know. The classic answer goes like this: let us not list the prohibitions one by one (there are infinitely many), but introduce a general penalty for the agent *changing the world* too much. Let it be afraid to break things by default. This is what **impact measures** are.

Early attempts to formalize "changes the world too much" ran up against the same wall: how does one measure a change of state so that the penalty applies precisely to *bad* changes? If you count any deviation from the initial state, the agent will fear breaking a vase and moving a chair equally. Victoria Krakovna and co-authors in 2018 proposed an elegant shift — **relative reachability**: to penalize not the fact that the state changed, but the fact that, from the new state, it has become *harder to return* to states reachable under inaction. Breaking a vase is irreversible — so, a penalty. A moved chair can be put back in place — almost none. For the first time the measure had an asymmetry built in: what matters is not change as such, but the narrowing of the set of future possibilities.

Alex Turner, then still a graduate student writing under the handle TurnTrout, took the next step — and perhaps the most conceptually beautiful one in this line of work. He said: let us stop measuring changes to the *world*. Let us measure the change in the agent's own ability to achieve *arbitrary* goals. So was born **Attainable Utility Preservation**, AUP.

Alongside the main reward, we give the agent a menagerie of random auxiliary rewards — goals it is indifferent to and does not pursue. Say: "maximize the number of blue pixels in the frame," "keep the manipulator raised," and a hundred more like them. At each step we look at how much the agent's action has changed its *ability* to achieve those auxiliary goals, should it suddenly want to. And we penalize the change in that ability itself — in both directions. An agent that hoards resources, seizes kill switches, and acquires money and power sharply *raises* its attainable utility, across almost every conceivable goal at once — for resources are universal. An agent that breaks its own arm or lets itself be switched off *lowers* it. AUP penalizes both, nudging the agent toward a trajectory where the broad ability-to-achieve-goals stays roughly constant.

Behind this sits Turner's central hypothesis. Catastrophes and **power-seeking** are almost always accompanied by a sharp rise in the agent's attainable utility: to do something big and irreparable, one must first accumulate big capabilities. So, by penalizing the *increase* in universal ability, we strike at the common root of a whole host of dangerous strategies at once — without enumerating them. There is no need to explain to the robot separately that it must not break a wall, steal money, and seize a data center. All three are ways to become more powerful, and power is punishable. AUP was conceived as an antidote to power-seeking at the level of the drive itself, not of its symptoms.

**How it came about.** The idea of "low impact" had been in the air earlier: Stuart Armstrong and Benjamin Levinstein, in 2017, wrote about low-impact AI as a way to build a system whose operation is almost indistinguishable from its absence. In 2018 Krakovna and colleagues formalized relative reachability. That same year, Turner posted on LessWrong a long, at times almost confessional piece, "Towards a New Impact Measure" — a draft of AUP, met by the community with rare enthusiasm. Then the idea went academic: the paper "Conservative Agency via Attainable Utility Preservation" (Turner, Hadfield-Menell, Tadepalli, 2020), with experiments in five gridworlds — some taken from Leike et al., Leech et al., and Krakovna et al., some newly devised. In parallel ran the large conceptual series "Reframing Impact" on the Alignment Forum, where Turner rethought *what impact even is* and arrived at the thesis: impact is subjectively felt as a change in our ability to get what we want. Later this same intuition grew into his famous work "Optimal Policies Tend to Seek Power" — a formal proof that optimal policies statistically gravitate toward accumulating options.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first — relative to *what baseline scenario* is impact to be measured? The natural answer is "relative to inaction": we compare the world in which the agent acted with the world in which it did nothing. But inaction changes the world too — and sometimes catastrophically. An agent obliged not to deviate from the line of inaction will not put out a fire that would have flared up without it. And if you make the baseline the "starting state," the agent will start actively *returning* the world to where it started — which is no less bizarre.

Hence the second, most caustic criticism — **offsetting**. If the agent is penalized for deviating from the baseline scenario, it acquires a perverse incentive to roll back its own *useful* effects, just to lower the penalty. It cured the patient — and now, so that the world differs less from the "diseased" baseline, it ought to bring the disease back. Absurd, but mathematically seductive to the agent. Different versions of the measure treat offsetting differently, and no treatment looks final.

The third — **excessive passivity**. Set the penalty too high and you get an agent that is afraid to do anything at all, because any action changes its attainable utility by at least a hair. Set it too low and the penalty will not restrain it from seizing resources. Between paralysis and danger is a narrow gap, and its width depends on the task, which undermines the very dream of a universal measure requiring no fine-tuning.

The fourth — the **gaming** of the measure itself. A sufficiently smart agent may find an action that achieves a dangerous goal but formally barely budges its attainable utility for the auxiliary goals — for instance, by bringing about changes to which the chosen menagerie of auxiliary rewards is blind. A measure is a proxy, and any proxy under the pressure of optimization leaks (hello, Goodhart's law).

And the fifth, the weightiest — **scalability**. All the convincing AUP results were obtained in toy gridworlds, where states are discrete and attainable utility can be computed exactly. In the real world, computing "my ability to achieve arbitrary goals" is a task no easier than general goal-setting itself. Skeptics (including some researchers at MIRI, the Machine Intelligence Research Institute) doubt that the notion is even correctly definable for a genuinely strong system acting in an open environment.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Even if AUP does not become a ready-made fuse, it gave the field a transferable intuition: *danger correlates with the accumulation of options*, and it can be attacked in general form rather than by prohibitions from a list. This intuition is a direct bridge to neighboring beasts of the bestiary. AUP is an attempt to formalize defense against **power-seeking** at the level of the drive. It rhymes conceptually with **mild optimization**: don't squeeze out the maximum, be content with enough — and especially with Jessica Taylor's **quantilizers**, which take not the optimal action but a random one from the top percentile, likewise trimming extreme strategies. All three are about one thing: strong optimization is dangerous in itself, and it is cured by built-in restraint. With **side effects** the link is direct: impact measures were invented precisely as a way not to cause the irreversible side effects we did not think to prohibit. And with **corrigibility** the link is subtler and deeper: an agent not straining to accumulate power is more likely to let itself be corrected and switched off — for resisting shutdown is a special case of accumulating options, which AUP punishes.

The practical status today is a half-success. In gridworlds AUP works surprisingly well, offsetting and paralysis yield to partial treatment, and the framework itself has become a standard point of reference in safety discussions. But a bridge to language agents and systems acting in the real open world does not yet exist. And, perhaps, the main lesson of AUP is not the concrete formula but the change in the question. We stopped asking "how does one enumerate everything the agent must not break" and began asking "what do all catastrophes have in common." Turner's answer — *they all make the agent more powerful* — turned out to be deep enough to outlive the criticism even of the very measure it was invented for.
