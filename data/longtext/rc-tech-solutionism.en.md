### Technological Solutionism (Evgeny Morozov, 2013)

There is a trash can that photographs what you have thrown out and posts the picture to a social network for your friends to see and score. There is a fork that vibrates when you eat too fast. There is an app that promises to "fix" democracy if voters rack up points for civic-mindedness, the way you rack up steps on a fitness tracker. Each of these things on its own is a charming absurdity. But if you look closely at their common grammar, a single gesture shows through: to take something tangled, contentious, human — overeating, laziness, political apathy, the very imperfection of the world — and declare it a *bug* awaiting a patch. It is precisely this gesture that Evgeny Morozov named technological solutionism: the morbid conviction that every problem is a badly posed engineering task, and that once you find the right algorithm, the conflict will vanish of its own accord.

The key word here is "redefine." Solutionism rarely lies about the solution. It lies about the problem. It takes a question that was by its nature political — that is, a place where incompatible values, interests, and notions of the good life collide — and rewrites it so that it acquires an *optimum*. And where there is an optimum, there is nothing left to discuss: all that remains is to reach it efficiently. The argument about goals gets swapped out for tuning the means. "What should we even do, and why" is quietly crowded out of the room, and in its place moves in "how do we optimize this." And most treacherous of all — this very substitution is presented as progress, as grown-up sobriety against chattering humanistic whining: enough philosophizing, let's finally *fix* things.

Morozov also notes the reverse oddity: in solutionism, solutions arrive before problems. First the technology appears — facial recognition, gamification, big data — and then people feverishly cast about for something to "solve" with it. The hammer is cocked, and now the whole world has to turn out to be a nail. And if the real problem resists, if it refuses to reduce to a metric — so much the worse for the problem: it will be neatly trimmed down to the part the tool knows how to grasp. Poverty becomes a matter of access to apps, health the sum of steps and calories, justice a question of a well-tuned rating. The remainder — that very undigitizable thing where the human lives — is declared inessential. Not because it is unimportant, but because it is inconvenient.

**How it came about.** The book *To Save Everything, Click Here: The Folly of Technological Solutionism* came out in 2013 from PublicAffairs. By that point Morozov — a researcher and polemicist of Belarusian origin — had already made his name with his first book, *The Net Delusion* (2011), which demolished the belief that the internet automatically brings freedom to dictatorships. With the second book he turned the mirror on the techno-optimists of Silicon Valley themselves. Morozov did not invent the word "solutionism": he honestly admits that he borrows it from architecture and urbanism, where it had long circulated as an insult: in the urbanist Michael Dobbins's usage it names design that presumes rather than investigates the problem it is trying to solve, reaching for the answer before the questions have been fully asked. Morozov merely transferred the diagnosis from concrete to code. Alongside it he put into circulation a second twin term — "internet-centrism": the habit of explaining and curing everything in the world through the magical entity "the Internet" with a capital I. Intellectually he stands on the shoulders of his elders: on Jacques Ellul's "autonomous technique" from *The Technological Society* (1954/1964), where la technique turns from an instrument into a self-propelling force with its own logic of efficiency, and on Ivan Illich's "conviviality" from *Tools for Conviviality* (1973), which distinguished tools that expand human autonomy from tools that replace it outright.

**Criticism / arguments against.** Morozov was hit from two sides, and largely deserved it. The first reproach: he is a brilliant demolisher and a hopeless builder. Hundreds of pages of elegant sarcasm about stupid gadgets — and almost nothing coherent about what doing it right would actually look like. The critique can easily curdle into a knee-jerk reflex where any technical improvement is met with an eye-roll: oh, of course, solutionism again. The second reproach — the tone. Morozov writes with such haughtiness and goes after his opponents so personally and so eagerly that even sympathizers wince; behind the fireworks of wit, the argument sometimes gets lost. The third, most serious: the boundary between a "real" and an "imaginary" problem keeps suspiciously shifting under his pen. After all, the vaccine, the sewer, the traffic light are also technical solutions to social ills, and thank goodness someone redefined cholera as an engineering task about clean water. Where does the line run between a benign solution and a solutionist sleight of hand? Morozov insists that the question is not the technologies themselves but whether, along with the problem, we amputate its moral and political tissue — but he never gives a clear criterion, and this leaves his diagnosis vulnerable: it is too easy to declare harmful anything you dislike.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For the "Bestiary," solutionism is not a small beast but a load-bearing structure to which half the rest of the menagerie is moored. It is a direct descendant of Ellul's autonomous technique and the evil twin of Illich's conviviality. It explains why it is more apt to speak of technologies in the logic of the pharmakon — a substance that is both medicine and poison at once, depending on dose and context: the solutionist sees only the medicine and refuses to notice that the same pill poisons a society's ability to negotiate its own goals. And it is also the secret engine behind the phrase that made all of this worth writing: "AI will solve alignment." Look closely — this is solutionism in its purest, most distilled form. The question "what values should a powerful intelligence serve, and who chooses those values" — a question ineradicably political, contentious, value-laden — is redefined into a technical optimization task: find the right function, the right RLHF, the right interpretable layer, and the normative conflict will dissolve into a benchmark. But alignment — *with whom*, *toward what*, *by whose decision* — is precisely the question "what should we do," and it cannot be delegated to gradient descent without committing exactly the sleight of hand Morozov described. Here solutionism converges with Scott Alexander's Moloch from "Meditations on Moloch" (2014): everyone locally "optimizes," and together they all rush toward a depopulated optimum that no one chose and no one needs — because along the way they imperceptibly lost the very forum in which one might have asked "but do we want this?"

The practical conclusion from all this is not Luddite and not "turn off the computers." It is humbler and harder. Before any "solution" — from the smart trash can to a plan for aligning superintelligence — one should ask three uncomfortable questions. First: is this really a problem, or is it merely friction, inefficiency, a roughness that actually carries some value — privacy, the freedom to err, the right to argue? Second: whose conflict of interest am I erasing right now by calling it a bug, and who benefits from the dispute being declared closed? Third: what exactly in this problem does not fit into the metric — and am I not throwing out the very most important thing simply because it does not count? Morozov gives no method for answering; he gives the habit of not ceasing to ask. For a discipline whose central task sounds like "bring the machine into agreement with human values," such an inoculation against solutionism is not a luxury but a condition for "agreement" to remain ours, rather than one that was optimized for us.
