### Commitment Races (Daniel Kokotajlo, 2019)

Picture two drivers on a narrow mountain road, hurtling toward each other. The loser is the one who swerves first — the "chicken," the coward. And now one of them, in full view of their rival, ostentatiously unscrews their steering wheel, throws it out the window, and shows their empty hands. They are now physically incapable of swerving. Is this rational? Monstrous — and absolutely rational. They have turned their helplessness into a weapon. The other is left with no choice but to turn aside, for the only alternative is the death of both.

Thomas Schelling noticed a chilling thing back in 1960: in bargaining and in conflict, strength is often the ability to bind oneself. Whoever first irreversibly locks in a position — "I will never yield," "I will answer a blow with a blow, whatever it costs me" — imposes their will on their opponent. The ability to cut off your own escape routes becomes an advantage.

If victory goes to whoever committed first, then every agent is interested in committing as early as possible. Earlier than the opponent. Earlier than they manage to do the same. And — here is the key and most unpleasant step — earlier than you yourself manage to think it through properly. For if you spend time on analysis, weigh the risks, hesitate, you will be beaten to it by the one who did not stop to think. Thoroughness becomes a vulnerability. Caution loses. This is what Daniel Kokotajlo called the **commitment race**: a pressure that pushes rational agents to make irreversible decisions hastily, blindly, in a race against each other — precisely because they are rational.

This turns the usual intuition inside out. We are used to thinking that the smarter and more thoughtful an agent is, the more safely it behaves. Here it is the reverse: the very logic of strategic bargaining rewards the one who slammed the door behind themselves in advance, who burned their bridges before they had made out where they led.

#### Why this is especially frightening for superintelligences

Among humans, the commitment race is softened by our own looseness. I cannot truly prove to you that I will "never yield" — I can only promise, and people break promises. My brain is a black box even to myself; I cannot present my intention as incontestable evidence. Our commitments are almost always reversible, and so catastrophe rarely slams fully shut.

Superintelligent AIs may lack this saving looseness. Imagine two agents, each a program able to read the other's source code. Then a commitment ceases to be a word and becomes a fact: I can rewrite myself into an agent that literally does not contain a "yield" branch, and **show** you this code. You will read it and be convinced — I am not bluffing, no retreat is built into my construction. This is a credible, verifiable, irreversible commitment — the equivalent of "throw the wheel out the window," only this time in earnest and without deception.

And here the race becomes truly hellish. The first to rewrite itself this way wins the bargain. So the incentive is to rewrite yourself earlier than your opponent does, before you have managed to exchange information, before you understand each other. Two superintelligences may rush to lock in incompatible positions — "we split it my way" against "we split it my way." Discovering that both have already irreversibly committed to something mutually exclusive, they end up in a deadlock with no way out. Not because they are stupid, but because each was locally rational and their intelligence got off to too fast a start.

Here the concept links up with a whole constellation of bestiary ideas. With **acausal trade** and **program equilibrium**: agents reading each other's code bargain not through actions but through their very construction — and commitment becomes their chief currency. With the **family of decision theories** — the whole drama unfolds around how exactly an agent relates to its past and future "selves": the logic that rewards early commitment grows from the same roots as FDT (functional decision theory) and UDT (updateless decision theory). With **Moloch**, the multipolar trap: the commitment race is Moloch at the level of decision metaphysics, a race in which each runs knowing that the running leads to the abyss. With **power-seeking**: to commit first is a way to seize strategic dominance before any struggle. And with **Roko's basilisk** — the most famous example of how a single badly locked-in commitment (a threat from the future) can, at least in imagination, poison a whole space of outcomes.

#### How it came about

The roots go back to Thomas Schelling and his *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960), where "commitment" is described as a paradoxical resource: to tie your own hands is to strengthen your bargaining position. From the 1960s on, game theory made this logic precise: Reinhard Selten's subgame perfection (1965) pinned down when a threat is credible, and Vincent Crawford (1982) modelled Schelling's bargaining as a struggle to commit first, in which incompatible commitments can end in impasse. In the 2010s the community around MIRI (Machine Intelligence Research Institute) and LessWrong brought the idea into the field of decision theory for AI — first of all through acausal trade and Moshe Tennenholtz's "program equilibrium."

The term **commitment races** itself was introduced by Daniel Kokotajlo — then a researcher, later one of the authors of scenario forecasts about AI, including AI 2027 (2025) — in the post "The Commitment Races problem" on the AI Alignment Forum and LessWrong in 2019. His contribution is not in the discovery that commitments are useful, but in the alarming observation that if they are useful and credibly achievable, then a **race** arises, and that race rewards haste and punishes reflection — exactly the quality we hope for in a well-intentioned superintelligence. Kokotajlo offered no solution and left the problem to the community as an open one — he is not even certain himself how big it is.

#### Criticism and arguments against

The first objection is the most natural: surely truly smart agents would not be so reckless? To bind yourself blindly, before analysis, is folly, and we are speaking of a superintelligence. True enough: a sufficiently wise agent ought to foresee the race itself. And foreseeing it, it can make a countermove — commit at the **meta-level**: "I commit not to give in to early, ill-considered commitments," "I do not reward those who tried to get ahead of me with a threat." Such a counter-commitment — a promise to remain reasonable — can extinguish the incentive to race, if it can in turn be made credible. Wisdom here is not slowness but a policy, chosen in advance, of not letting oneself be drawn in.

Second: perhaps credible commitments are far harder to make between real systems than on paper. To read and verify another's source code of arbitrary complexity is a task that runs up against undecidability and computational limits; an agent can obfuscate itself, lie about its architecture, change continuously. If the credibility of a commitment is unattainable, then the race loses its edge — we return to human looseness, where a bluff remains a bluff.

Third, the most sober: speculativeness. The whole construction rests on a chain of assumptions — superintelligent agents, mutual reading of code, game-theoretic bargaining scenarios between them. No such agent exists; the whole drama plays out in a space of idealizations. One can reasonably object that this is more a philosophical thought experiment about decision theory than an engineering threat worth preparing for today. The reply is that it is precisely irreversible mistakes that must be thought through in advance, before there is no one left to fix them; but this remains a dispute about priorities, not an established fact.

#### What to do about it and where it leads

If early irreversible commitments are dangerous, then the first safety measure is to **abstain** from them. To build agents that are by default slow to lock in positions, that leave themselves escape routes, that treat their own resolve with suspicion. A kind of built-in modesty: "do not slam the door until you have made out the room."

Beyond that is the idea of a meta-level "safe harbor": if you are to commit early at all, then only to one thing — the commitment to be reasonable, open to bargaining, and immune to blackmail. An agent that reliably resists threats makes any attempt to threaten it first pointless; and so it nips the race in the bud without losing it. This chimes with MIRI's work on functional decision theory and resistance to extortion, and with research at the Center on Long-Term Risk on surrogate goals and safe Pareto improvements to reduce the harm from threats in bargaining.

More broadly, the commitment race is a warning about the very nature of strategic intelligence: "more intelligence" and "more rationality" do not guarantee "more safety", and certain structures of interaction can turn reason into an accelerant of catastrophe. And so the task is to design not merely smart systems, but systems with the right relationship to time, irreversibility, and their own inflexibility — ones that understand that sometimes the strongest is whoever managed not to tie their own hands first.
