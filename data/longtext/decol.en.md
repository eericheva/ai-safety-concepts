### The Universal Yardstick (Mohamed, Png, and Isaac, 2020)

Picture an engineer who sincerely believes they are building a neutral measuring instrument. They take a ruler — their own, familiar one, marked in units that seem to them the only possible ones — and declare: with this ruler we shall measure all of humanity. Whatever does not fit the graduations they will write off as noise, backwardness, or a data error. And nobody chose this yardstick together; it was inherited from one particular geography and one particular history — which is exactly why it seems invisible. Decolonial AI ethics is a concept that takes this yardstick in hand, turns it to the light, and asks: whose hand marked the graduations, and why did one measure come to be called "universal"?

The thesis sounds uncomfortable for the whole alignment industry: when we say "align AI with human values," we almost always mean, without saying so, Western values — or more precisely, the values of a narrow segment of the global North: individualist, rationalist, optimizing. The autonomous subject maximizing utility; freedom as the absence of constraints; ethics as calculation. All this is presented not as one cultural tradition among many, but as the neutral frame itself, within which traditions merely take their place. The decolonial move is to show that the frame is not neutral, that it has a registered address, and that this address is the metropole.

The mechanism here is twofold. The first layer is material: **algorithmic colonialism**. Models are trained on data extracted from all over the world, but the profit, the patents, and the power concentrate in a few companies in a few countries. Labeling and moderation are outsourced to workers in Kenya and the Philippines for a pittance; experimental systems of biometrics, credit scoring, and "humanitarian" surveillance are tested on refugees and the residents of the global South — where the regulatory landscape is softer and the test subjects have fewer rights. This is the old logistics of the colony — raw materials extracted at the periphery, added value at the center — rewritten into the language of data centers.

The second layer is subtler and more important for alignment — **epistemic hegemony**. It is about whose knowledge counts as knowledge at all, whose values are taken as the norm by default, and whose require "justification." When a "common-sense" benchmark, a "toxicity" dataset, or a constitution for Constitutional AI is written predominantly by English-speaking teams, they encode one picture of the world as the background, and all the others as deviations that must either be brought into line or filtered out. A model trained this way will politely, smoothly, and with full confidence reproduce one local morality under the guise of a universal one. This is the digital continuation of what decolonial theorists, following Aníbal Quijano, call "coloniality" — power that outlived the formal empires and now lives in categories, classifications, and standards.

The alternative Mohamed, Png, and Isaac propose is not "add a little diversity to the training set." That would be cosmetics. They propose changing the metaphysics itself. The key resource here is the sub-Saharan African philosophy of **ubuntu**, brought into the AI context by the researcher Sabelo Mhlambi. The formula of ubuntu is "a person is a person through other persons" (*umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*): a person is not an atom that precedes society but a node of relations, and becomes who they are through them. Hence — a relational ethics instead of a calculative one. If Western alignment asks "how do we make an autonomous agent maximize the right function," the relational frame asks "into what web of mutual obligations is the system embedded, and whom does it render less human." Mhlambi states outright: the very positing of the person as a rational optimizer is not neutral science but a philosophical choice, historically bound up with the justification of who counts as a full subject and who does not.

**How it came about.** The paper "Decolonial AI: Decolonial Theory as Sociotechnical Foresight in Artificial Intelligence" appeared in the journal *Philosophy & Technology* in 2020. It was written by Shakir Mohamed and William Isaac of DeepMind together with the Oxford researcher Marie-Therese Png — and this alone is curious: a critique of the industry's power came from inside one of its citadels. The authors drew on a long tradition — Frantz Fanon, Walter Mignolo, the Latin American *modernidad/colonialidad* group — and applied its perspective to concrete technical practices. The term "decolonial AI" landed on ground that was already heating up: a year earlier, in 2019, Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejias put out *The Costs of Connection*, where they introduced the notion of **data colonialism**; Abeba Birhane wrote of the "algorithmic colonization of Africa"; and a year later, in 2021, Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI* came out, showing AI as an extraction industry — of lithium, labor, data — with a planetary geography of inequality. The decolonial frame became a load-bearing pillar of the critical, humanities canon of AI safety — the one that looks at risk not as an "under-optimized function" but as a distribution of power.

**Criticism / arguments against.** First: the charge of performative contradiction. The paper was written in part by DeepMind employees, decolonial panels take place at conferences in Vancouver and Vienna sponsored by those very corporations — does the radical critique not turn into ethical laundering, into "decolonial-washing," which changes the vocabulary but not the ownership or the flows of capital? Second, the philosophical one: if we carry value pluralism to its end, do we not fall into a relativism where one can condemn neither caste discrimination nor a repressive "local value," because any judgment is declared the imposition of someone else's measure? Universalism, after all, was not invented by colonizers alone — it also underlies the human rights that decolonial movements themselves invoke.

Third, the practical one, most painful for the engineer: the concept diagnoses brilliantly but offers little to build with. "Redistribute power" is not a specification. How exactly does one encode ubuntu into a loss function? What is the developer to do on Monday morning? Relational ethics risks remaining a beautiful critique to which no metric can be attached — and hence one with no priority on the roadmap. And fourth: the appeal to ubuntu as "authentic African philosophy" may itself become an essentialism — packing a multitude of living traditions into a single exotic souvenir for Western consumption.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The concept is valuable precisely as a lens, not as a ready-made algorithm, and to demand a loss function of it is to measure it by the very yardstick it criticizes. But even without operationalization it yields concrete practical conclusions. Participatory design — not a consultation after the fact, but the handing over of part of the decision-making power to the communities on whom the system will operate, including the right to say "no" and not build it at all. Disaggregation of benchmarks and evaluations by language, region, culture — so that "alignment" ceases to mean alignment to a single dialect of morality. Transparency of the labor chain — who labeled, for how much, under what conditions. And, for the alignment theorist, the deepest question: if "human values" are not a single number but an irreducibly plural, contested, and power-laden field, then the task of alignment ceases to be technical and becomes political — a question of whose voice makes it into the objective function and whose is filtered out as noise.

In the bestiary this beast stands at a crossroads. It holds hands with Bender and Gebru's **stochastic parrots** — for a model that smoothly reproduces the dominant corpus is precisely a machine of epistemic hegemony. It leans on **data colonialism** and the *Atlas of AI* as its material half. And it enters into direct dispute with the idea of a single, computable set of values — with any attempt to fold "what we really want" into one function, forgetting to ask who this "we" is. The universal yardstick is dangerous not because it is marked wrong. It is dangerous because we have forgotten how to see anyone's hand upon it.
