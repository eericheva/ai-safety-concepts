### The Snow Cornice (compute overhang; MIRI/LessWrong, ~2008; Bostrom, 2014)

On the crest of a mountain hangs a snow cornice — an overhang, built up by a whole winter's blizzards. It is motionless, it looks like part of the landscape, and tourists have posed for photos beneath it for years. But the mass has already accumulated; all that is missing is a trigger — a sharp sound, one extra snowflake, a careless step. And then what comes down is not a snowflake but the whole winter at once. The compute overhang is about this very cornice, only instead of snow it is the world that hangs over us, packed to the brim with computational power, and the trigger is a single lucky algorithm.

At any moment in time there is some amount of hardware installed in the world — processors, accelerators, data centers. And there is some best current way of turning that hardware into capabilities. Between "how much power is physically available" and "how much we know how to squeeze out of it" there is almost always a gap. An **overhang** is a situation where the gap is large: available compute is already far more than would be needed to launch a dangerously capable AI — as soon as the right trick is found. Until the trick is there, the cornice hangs. But on the day it is found, scaling happens not over years of building new fabs but instantly: the chips are already installed and running. One good trick, and the existing fleet of GPUs runs a system an order of magnitude more powerful than yesterday's. The smooth climb turns into a cliff.

The overhang has three faces. The **hardware overhang** in the narrow sense — a surfeit of the chips themselves: the world has produced more compute than it knows how to put to useful work. The **algorithmic overhang** — untapped potential for improvement: a smarter algorithm produces many times greater capabilities with the same power, and this reserve accumulates quietly, because improvements are discovered rarely and in fits and starts. And the **inference overhang** — the most insidious: a model trained once can then be run in millions of parallel copies, because running a finished network is incomparably cheaper than training it. That is, even without new training a single successful system instantly turns into a whole population of tireless workers, thinking around the clock and faster than a human. Training is the climb up the ladder; inference is when you reach the top and are met not by one copy of yourself, but by an army.

All this bears directly on **takeoff speeds**. The overhang is one of the few concrete physical mechanisms able to make takeoff fast and discontinuous rather than slow and continuous. If capabilities are bottlenecked by the time needed to build data centers, the world has years of warning signals while power is ramped up. But if the power is already there and everything rests on a single missing algorithm — there will be no warning: the cornice comes down on the day the algorithm is found, and comes down whole. This is precisely why the overhang is a favorite building block in arguments for the **intelligence explosion** and **recursive self-improvement**: if a system that has learned to improve itself additionally finds a mountain of idle hardware at hand, the feedback loop gets not only the idea but the fuel to spin up immediately.

**How it came about.** The idea grew out of early discussions of the intelligence explosion in the MIRI milieu and on LessWrong in the late 2000s — the same conversations from which came the Hanson–Yudkowsky dispute about takeoff speeds. The root goes back to Irving Good and his "first ultraintelligent machine" of 1965: if intelligence is able to design better intelligence, then somewhere a discontinuity lies in wait. The name was born in those same discussions: "hardware overhang" already appears in Yudkowsky's "Hard Takeoff" (2008), and Luke Muehlhauser and Anna Salamon, in the survey "Intelligence Explosion: Evidence and Import" (2012), cemented the sibling term *computing overhang*: they noted that computational power grows exponentially and will most likely outpace our skill at writing software for it — so that by the time human-level AI appears, there will already be an overhang of hardware beneath it, allowing many copies to be spawned at once and driven to full speed. Nick Bostrom in *Superintelligence* (2014) built this dynamic into the general framework of takeoff scenarios: for him hardware overhang is one of the factors pushing the transition toward a fast, local takeoff, where a single system gains a decisive strategic advantage before the world can react. Since then the notion has acquired variants — the algorithmic overhang and the inference overhang only reached broad discussion in the era of large language models, when it became visible how cheaply a finished model can be replicated.

**Criticism / arguments against.** A steelman is especially needed here, because over the last decade the factual picture has shifted against the classic overhang.

The chief objection comes from the **scaling hypothesis**. In a paradigm where capabilities grow predictably and smoothly along with compute — as shown by the scaling laws of Kaplan and colleagues (2020) — there is almost no overhang in the old sense. Capabilities do not wait for one magic algorithm to unlock them all at once; they accrue little by little with each flop invested, and the curve is smooth rather than sheer. If so, then the "cornice" melts as it accumulates: people spend almost all available power, in real time, on ever larger models: compute is not stockpiled for later but immediately devoured by the next training run. On this view the overhang is an artifact of the old picture, where intelligence was assumed to switch on with a threshold click.

Second — **the size of the overhang is simply a matter of dispute**. How much "excess" hardware exactly hangs over the world? Estimates range across orders of magnitude depending on assumptions: how much compute a brain-equivalent needs, how efficient a hypothetical future algorithm is, whether scattered chips can even be used together for one training task. Forecasts like Ajeya Cotra's "biological anchors" (2020) are precisely an attempt to feel out how much compute is really required for transformative AI — and the estimates vary so widely that the thesis "the overhang is enormous" can be neither proven nor refuted in advance. And algorithmic efficiency does indeed grow (Hernandez and Brown, 2020, reported a doubling of algorithmic efficiency roughly every 16 months between 2012 and 2019, as measured by the compute needed to train an ImageNet classifier to AlexNet-level accuracy) — but it grows gradually, leaving no one-off discontinuity of the kind the cornice image warns us about.

The third objection is no longer about facts but about how the argument is used. From the existence of the overhang one sometimes derives the strategy "earlier is safer": better to develop powerful AI *now*, while there is still comparatively little hardware beneath it — then the inevitable accident will happen on a weak system and come cheap, rather than on the gigantic accumulated overhang of the future. The logic is not flawless: it assumes we know how to control the timing of the breakthrough and that an early accident will indeed prove controllable rather than the first in a series. The same argument is easily turned inside out into a justification for racing — "let's floor the gas before it's too late" — and that is exactly the rhetoric critics fear more than the overhang itself.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical meaning of the overhang lies in **compute governance**. If the key resource separating us from a discontinuous takeoff is concentrated compute, then compute becomes the natural point of control too. Hence the interest in making computation observable and accountable: thresholds on the number of flops for training, export controls on top accelerators, monitoring of large clusters, hardware verification mechanisms. Hardware, unlike ideas, is physical, discrete, and produced by a few manufacturers — and so it lends itself to regulation in a way that an algorithm, which can be written on a napkin, does not.

But the overhang prompts opposite moves. The classic conclusion "earlier is safer" pushes toward deploying capabilities on modest hardware on the assumption that the cost of error would be small. The cautious conclusion is the reverse: do not let the overhang grow without a commensurate growth in our skill at aligning systems, because the greater the overhanging mass, the more catastrophic the single collapse. The reasonable stance here too is not to choose the scenario in advance, but to watch the gap between accumulated power and the ability to put it to use: to measure the pace of algorithmic progress, to track whether an inference overhang has appeared (one that lets a single model be replicated into a population), and to keep the self-improvement loop itself in view. The cornice is dangerous not because it is large, but because it comes down all at once and without warning. All the safety work here reduces to one thing: not to be under it at the moment someone finds the trigger.
