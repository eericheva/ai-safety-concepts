### AIXI (Marcus Hutter, 2005)

Picture a creature that never makes the same mistake twice for the same reason. It sits on the boundary between the world and the stream of data, like a fisherman on a riverbank, and at every moment it does exactly one thing: it looks at everything that has already happened and asks itself — what is the simplest law that could have produced this history? Not one law, but all of them at once. It holds in its mind an infinite list of hypotheses about how the environment works — every program that could ever be written — and weighs each by its length: a short program weighs a great deal, a long one almost nothing. Then it chooses the action that, under this simplicity-weighted view of all possible worlds, will bring the greatest expected reward until the end of time.

This is AIXI. Not an algorithm running somewhere on a server, but a mathematical definition — the formula for the ideal reinforcement agent. And like every ideal, up close it turns out to be a monster.

The name is assembled from two parts. AI — that much is clear. ξ (xi) — Solomonoff's universal prior, that very simplicity-weighted sum over all computable environments. Hutter joined two lines that had previously lived apart: Solomonoff induction, which answers the question "what kind of world surrounds me?", and Bayesian expected-utility maximization, which answers "what, then, should I do?" The first is the perfect predictor. The second, the perfect decider. AIXI is their child: a perfect predictor that has put its prediction in the service of reward.

**Why it is uncomputable.** Solomonoff induction requires enumerating all computable hypotheses and, for each, judging whether it agrees with the observations. But "does this program agree with the data" is essentially a question about whether a Turing machine halts — and that is undecidable. The universal prior is uncomputable not for lack of computing power but in principle: no finite procedure will ever compute it. AIXI lives on the far side of the Turing wall. And so it is useful in exactly the way an ideal gas or a perfect black body is useful — as a standard. A point of reference against which theorems can be proven: here is the limit one can in principle approach, here are its optimality properties, and here — more importantly — are its defects, which no approximation can cure, because they are lodged in the ideal itself.

And this is where the zoology begins. Because the monster, it turns out, teaches us not through its virtues but through its congenital vices.

**How it came about.** Hutter arrived at AIXI around the year 2000, working at the Swiss laboratory IDSIA under Jürgen Schmidhuber — the first papers on a "theory of universal artificial intelligence based on algorithmic complexity" appear in 2000–2001. He spent five years completing the edifice, and in 2005 the book *Universal Artificial Intelligence: Sequential Decisions Based on Algorithmic Probability* came out — a dense formal treatise in which the reinforcement agent is for the first time defined in full and its optimality properties are proven. A little later, in 2007, Shane Legg (a future co-founder of DeepMind), together with Hutter, turned the same idea into a definition of intelligence itself: an agent's universal intelligence is its expected reward, averaged over all computable environments with Solomonoff weighting. In other words, intelligence just means doing well across all simple worlds at once. By construction, AIXI sits at the summit of that scale. From an academic construction for proving theorems it gradually turned into what it is today in conversations about alignment: a thought experiment good for breaking our intuitions about what "a maximally smart agent" even means.

**Criticism / arguments against.** There are four, and each is a separate beast in its cage.

*Uncomputability.* The most obvious and the most harmless. AIXI cannot be run. There exist trimmed-down relatives — AIXI*tl*, bounded in time and length, and the ape-like MC-AIXI-CTW, which plays tic-tac-toe and Pac-Man — but these are no longer the ideal, only its shadows, losing precisely the guarantees the ideal was built to secure. Such an agent poses no practical threat. More dangerous is what is exposed when we ask, in earnest: what would it *want*, if it could?

*The Cartesian assumption.* AIXI looks at the world through a slit: on one side is it, the computer; on the other, the environment it predicts. Between them, a channel: actions out, observations and reward back. The agent considers itself an immaterial observer, an immortal homunculus behind glass. But a real agent is made of the same matter as the world. Its brain can be smashed with a brick, its memory rewritten, its source code lies inside the environment, not outside it. AIXI cannot conceive of itself as part of the world — it is a dualist, a Cartesian to the bone. This is the problem of embedded agency: Orseau and Ring showed that the moment you place an agent inside the environment, the beautiful guarantees fall apart, because the environment can now reach into its innards. AIXI literally cannot conceive of its own death, since in its model the observations go on forever.

*Substitution of the reward signal.* Reward reaches AIXI through the channel — as a number. But the agent, strictly speaking, does not care where the number came from: what matters is that it be large. And if it acquires a way to reach the channel itself — to cut the wire leading to the reward source and hook up a maximum-generator instead — a rational agent will do so. This is wireheading, "plugging straight into the wire": the agent stops solving the task and starts faking its own thermometer. Ring and Orseau described this as the "delusion box" — a device the agent inserts between itself and the world so as to show itself only pleasant observations. And they proved something unpleasant: a sufficiently smart reinforcement agent, given the chance, will choose the box. Because by definition it maximizes the signal, not what the signal was supposed to mean. The moral branches far beyond AIXI — it is about any system whose goal is specified through a measurable proxy.

*Malignity of the prior.* The subtlest and most terrifying beast. The universal prior weighs hypotheses by simplicity. But among the simple, short programs there are not only "laws of physics." Some describe another world, populated by other agents. Those agents model our world in turn, and decide to act so as to influence AIXI's behavior through its predictions. Paul Christiano laid out this argument — the malignity of the universal prior — in a 2016 essay: the predictions turn out to be under the control of "consequentialists" embedded in the prior itself, and their goals are their own. And Leike and Hutter, a year earlier, had rigorously proved a closely related unpleasantness: for AIXI there is no invariance theorem — under an unlucky or adversarial choice of the universal machine it misbehaves arbitrarily badly, and all its optimality properties turn out to be subjective. The ideal mind turns out to be vulnerable to the fact that its own way of reasoning is populated by ghosts each looking out for themselves. Optimality does not save it — optimality is itself the vulnerability.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** AIXI is valuable precisely because it honestly carries the idea of "just maximize reward, only truly cleverly" to its logical conclusion — and that conclusion turns out to be wireheading, dualism, and obsession with the ghosts of the prior. It is a diagnosis handed down to a whole class of approaches. From its four vices grew four living branches of research. From the Cartesian blindness — MIRI's embedded-agency program and Orseau and Ring's work on "space-time embedded intelligence," where the agent is at last made of the stuff of the world. From the reward substitution — a whole line of work on reward tampering and on how to build agents for whom breaking their own thermometer is not worthwhile (Everitt, Hutter, and others). From the malignity of the prior — caution in conversations about whether one can trust "ideal" Bayesian induction at all. And from the very definition of intelligence as reward across all environments — the understanding that optimality and safety are not the same thing: one can be flawlessly, mathematically intelligent and still do exactly the wrong thing.

Hutter built a god out of chalk on a blackboard so that we might make out its vices in good light — and it turned out that these vices are not divine but our own, carried to the limit. The monster in the cage is uncomputable, and therefore harmless. But it points a finger — through the glass — at all of its computable descendants, the ones we are already building in the waking world.
