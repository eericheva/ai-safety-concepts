### Ghost Work (Mary L. Gray & Siddharth Suri, 2019)

You send a photo of a receipt to an app, and a second later back come neatly transcribed details — the total, the date, the store. "The magic of AI," you think. In fact, at that very moment, somewhere in Hyderabad or in a small town in Kentucky, a person whose name you will never learn has manually typed in those details for a few cents, because the algorithm stumbled over smudged print on your receipt. You were talking to a machine. A human answered you. And everything was done to keep you from noticing.

It is precisely this gap — between the promise of full automation and the invisible army of people that props up that promise from within — that Mary Gray, an anthropologist at Microsoft Research, and Siddharth Suri, a computational social scientist, named **ghost work**. Ghost not because it does not exist, but because it is diligently hidden: it does not appear on the payroll, does not flicker in the ad reels, is not mentioned in startup pitches. It is everywhere and yet, as it were, nowhere.

### The mechanism: the paradox of automation's last mile

The book's central idea runs against common sense, which says: the more we automate, the fewer people we need. Gray and Suri show the opposite — they call it the **paradox of automation's last mile**. Every system, once it reaches a certain level, runs up against tasks not yet within the algorithm's grasp: edge cases, ambiguities, rare exceptions, fresh forms of fraud. People handle these tasks. But as soon as these holes are patched and the next version of the model is trained on the new data, the frontier shifts — and a new band of tasks opens up that, once again, must be done by hand. Automation does not devour manual labor but continuously spawns a new variety of it, only ever further from the public's eye.

Who are these people? Data labelers, who draw boxes around pedestrians in footage for self-driving cars. Microtaskers on Amazon Mechanical Turk, MTurk — the platform where Gray and Suri spent years doing fieldwork — taking on a "HIT" (human intelligence task) worth a couple of cents: describe an image, match two addresses, judge whether a bot's reply is polite. Content moderators, viewing what no one should see. Formally, all of them are "independent contractors", not employees: without sick leave, without a guaranteed rate, without insurance, without colleagues in the literal sense — often they do not even know the name of the person next to them on the conveyor, because that conveyor is dispersed across the globe and exists only inside an API.

The darkest cell of this haunted house is moderation. The people who, eight hours a day, watch beheadings, violence against children, torture, so that your feed stays clean, pay for it with post-traumatic stress disorder, insomnia, the breakup of families. The machine cannot review this horror reliably enough — which means a human reviews it, and the human breaks, and the cost of that breaking finds its way into no one's financial statement.

### How it came about

The book *Ghost Work: How to Stop Silicon Valley from Building a New Global Underclass* came out in 2019 from Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. Behind it stood an alliance rare for this subject: an anthropologist and a computational social scientist, working together at Microsoft Research. The method was hybrid too — half ethnography, half big-data analysis: more than two hundred in-depth interviews with workers in India and the US, an examination of logs and transactions on the platforms themselves. Gray and Suri did not observe ghost work from the outside but posted tasks themselves as requesters, to understand the economy of cents and seconds from within.

The term landed on ground that had already been prepared for it. Back in 2008 the researcher Lilly Irani together with M. Six Silberman launched Turkopticon — a plugin that let "Turkers" rate requesters and thereby make the invisible workers at least a little visible to one another. Irani also took apart Amazon's own formula, "artificial artificial intelligence": under the guise of an algorithm, it sells the manual labor of the poor. Gray and Suri drew on these scattered observations to develop a coherent lens and gave it a name that stuck.

### Criticism and arguments against

The first is the charge of unwittingly making peace with the system. Gray and Suri sound optimistic in places: they write about "collaboration" among workers, about the dignity people manage to extract even from microtasks, and they propose reforms such as guaranteed social protections for platform workers. Critics from the more radical camp — above all the France-based Italian sociologist Antonio Casilli — object: this is not a glitch that a pay top-up can fix, but a structure. As long as the business model is built on arbitrage between the cheap labor of the Global South and the capital of the North, cosmetic improvements in conditions do not abolish the very logic of extraction.

The second concerns novelty. Skeptics note that outsourcing, piecework, and sweatshops existed long before MTurk; the digital platform merely fitted a new interface onto old exploitation. In reply one can say that it is precisely the interface — the invisibility, the dispersal, the absence of a workplace as a point of assembly — that is qualitatively new: it makes solidarity and the organization of labor almost impossible.

The third is methodological: the focus on MTurk and on the US and India gives an incomplete map. Other work — Billy Perrigo's TIME investigation into Kenyan labelers (2023), Sarah T. Roberts's book on content moderators, including Filipino ones (2019) — showed that the heaviest burden in the chain falls exactly where the authors' gaze did not reach far enough.

### What to do about it and where it leads

For those working on AI safety, "ghost work" is not a sentimental digression but a direct pointer to a blind spot. We like to speak of the "human in the loop" as a guarantee of control. Gray and Suri reverse the perspective: often the human in the loop is that very ghost — underpaid and traumatized — whose hands and psyche pay for our illusion of an autonomous system.

This sounds especially sharp in the era of large language models. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), the thing that made chatbots polite and "aligned", is literally industrialized ghost work: people rank answers, label toxicity, write reference replies. In 2023 TIME magazine showed the underside: to teach ChatGPT not to spew filth, OpenAI, through a contractor, hired Kenyan workers who, for less than two dollars an hour, read descriptions of violence and abuse, labeling them for the filter. The "alignment" of the model turned out to be built on misalignment with the interests of the people who provided it. Any conversation about the values of AI that passes over this fact in silence is a conversation with a hole in the middle.

Where does this lead in practice? To demand transparency of the data chains: who labeled the data a model learns from, where, and under what conditions. To count the labor of labelers and moderators as part of the technical system, not as external "raw material": if it is invisible in the accounting, it is invisible in the risk assessment too. To recognize the psychological trauma of moderation as an occupational injury, not a freelancer's cost. And — methodologically — every time you meet the word "automatically," to ask: and who is on the last mile? Often the answer will turn out to be a human face that someone deliberately pushed into the shadows.

The ghost in the machine is not a metaphor for consciousness, as in Gilbert Ryle's old dispute. Here the ghosts are concrete people, whose hands type the figures from your receipt while you marvel at the magic. The bestiary of alignment is incomplete without this beast: it is impossible to reason seriously about making AI safe for humanity without noticing that part of humanity which is already paying for it with its own well-being.
