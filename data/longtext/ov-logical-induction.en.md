### Logical Induction (Garrabrant, Benson-Tilsen, Critch, Soares, Taylor, 2016)

What is the thousandth digit of π? It is already determined — π does not change, its decimal expansion has been fixed since the birth of mathematics — but you do not know it until you compute it. Is the twenty-digit number before you prime? Is the conjecture people have worked on for half a century provable? In all these cases the answer is logically determined: it follows from the axioms with iron necessity. And yet it is reasonable to behave as if we were unsure of it — to assign a probability of about one in ten to each possible digit of π, to pay money for a factorization, to argue about the Riemann hypothesis. An ideal Bayesian agent cannot behave this way. It is logically omniscient: the moment it assigns the axioms probability one, it knows all their consequences instantly, at no computational cost. For it, "provable" and "known" are one and the same. Such an agent does not and cannot exist: deriving consequences costs computation, and only a finite amount of computation is available.

The question the five researchers at MIRI (the Machine Intelligence Research Institute) posed sounds deceptively narrow: how do you assign meaningful, coherent probabilities to unproven mathematical statements under bounded resources? Not "is the statement true" — it is already true or false — but "what is it rational to believe about it while you have not finished computing." Classical probability theory is silent here: it is about the uncertainty of the world, not the uncertainty of one's own mind.

The answer is an algorithm called the logical inductor, and the criterion it satisfies. Imagine a market where "shares" of logical statements are traded: a share is tied to a statement φ, its price is the current probability assigned to φ, and its payout equals one if φ is true and zero otherwise. Over time a background deductive process runs — a theorem enumerator slowly writing out proofs from a fixed theory (say, Peano arithmetic, PA). Prices change from day to day. **The logical induction criterion** states: a sequence of prices forms a logical inductor if no efficiently computable trader can "fleece" it. More precisely: there is no polynomial-time betting strategy that, trading against these prices, extracts unbounded profit (with bounded risk). If such a trader exists, then there is a systematic, exploitable error sitting in the prices; if there is none, then the prices have in a certain sense already absorbed everything that can be noticed in reasonable time.

This is a direct logical analog of de Finetti's classical "Dutch book" argument. There: if your degrees of belief violate the axioms of probability, a bookmaker will compose a set of bets guaranteed to drain money from you — so rationality demands coherence. Here it is the same, but with two amendments. First, the exploiting agent must compute its trades in polynomial time — otherwise it would simply compute everything itself, and this would be omniscience, not induction. Second, the guarantee is asymptotic: not "you cannot be beaten at every moment," but "from some moment on, the accumulated profit of any fixed efficient trader is bounded." Inexploitability is not an instantaneous property but a property of the trajectory.

From this single criterion — "no polynomial trader grows unboundedly rich" — the authors derive an impressive list of desirable properties, and this is perhaps the chief beauty of the work. The inductor converges: the price assigned to each statement has a limit. The limit is coherent — a genuine probability distribution, consistent with the theory. Everything the theory proves gets a probability tending to one; everything refutable — to zero. Statements independent of the theory (and by Gödel, such statements exist) get intermediate, undogmatic probabilities strictly between zero and one — the inductor does not pretend to know the undecidable. It is calibrated: among the statements to which it gives about 70%, roughly 70% turn out true. It learns statistical regularities before it manages to prove them — for instance, it starts betting ≈1/10 on the digits of π without deriving each one. And it possesses the rudiments of self-trust: it assigns reasonable probabilities to statements about its own future beliefs, sidestepping some of the paradoxes of reflection on which earlier constructions had stumbled. All of these are theorems, not wishes.

**How it came about.** The problem of logical uncertainty is older than MIRI. As early as the 1960s, Haim Gaifman was thinking about probabilities over the sentences of a formal language; in the 2010s Marcus Hutter and his co-authors built distributions over the sentences of an expressive logic, and Abram Demski proposed the "Demski prior" based on algorithmic probability. All these approaches were either uncomputable or broke on some concrete test — failing to give calibration, failing to learn regularities, lapsing into dogmatism. Scott Garrabrant wrote a series of notes on the Intelligent Agent Foundations forum, formulating an ever longer list of "desirable properties" and showing that the known constructions did not combine them. The 2016 breakthrough is that, instead of chasing the properties one by one, the authors found a single generative principle — inexploitability of the market by efficient traders — and derived the rest as consequences. The preprint arXiv:1609.03543 was accompanied by an analysis on LessWrong; the LessWrong community welcomed it as a rare case of a clean, "grown-up" theorem from the MIRI stable.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and chief one is asymptotics. The guarantees live "in the limit": for any fixed efficient trader the profit is bounded *from some day on*, but that day is not given constructively and may be astronomically far off. On any finite horizon the inductor can behave arbitrarily badly; the criterion does not forbid an arbitrarily long stretch of stupidity, it only forbids an eternal one. For a theory of bounded rationality this is an irony: a construction conceived as a model of an agent with finite resources makes substantive promises only about infinity. The second is computational heaviness. The inductor itself is computable but hopelessly impractical: at each step it approximately solves a fixed-point problem, balancing prices against a growing list of traders — this is a constructive proof of existence, not a practically usable algorithm. The third is the gap between this theory and real ML. The connection between logical induction and how neural networks "reason" about mathematics or gauge their own uncertainty remains metaphorical: a market of traders is not gradient descent, a deductive process is not learning from data.

Finally, the choice of the class of exploiters (polynomial strategies) is in part conventional: shift the boundary and the guarantees shift too, and it is unclear what this boundary corresponds to in a physical agent.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The value of logical induction lies not in running it but in the fact that it gives the first rigorous, computable example of *boundedly rational reasoning about mathematics* and thereby a key building block of the embedded-agency program. The classical Cartesian picture — the agent outside its environment and by definition larger than it — collapses the moment the agent turns out to be part of the very world it models, physically smaller than it, and forced to reason about itself. Logical omniscience is one of the first casualties of this transition: an embedded agent cannot know all the consequences of its own beliefs — there are too many consequences for it to work out. Logical induction shows that giving up omniscience need not end in chaos — coherence and calibration are attainable even without it. From here threads run to neighboring nodes of the bestiary: to tiling agents, which need to trust the reasoning of their future versions without stumbling over the Löbian obstacle — and the inductor's self-trust hints at what such trust might look like; to decision theory under conditions where the very act of computing changes the stakes; to the question of what "rational" even means for a being whose thinking costs time and energy. The practical horizon is modest: there is as yet no direct bridge to today's LLMs. But as a conceptual lodestar — "here is what an agent honestly living with its own computational finitude might look like" — this work sets a bar below which it is already awkward to sink.

You still do not know the thousandth digit of π. Not knowing, it turns out, can be done competently.
