### Sparse autoencoders / dictionary learning (Cunningham et al.; Anthropic, 2023–2024)

Imagine you recorded a symphony orchestra on a single microphone. On the track — French horns, violins, timpani, oboe — everything glued together into one oscillating line of air pressure. Not a single note is "visible." And now the task: reconstruct the score. Decompose this glued-together wave back into instruments — so that you can say "here is where the cello comes in," mute it, boost the trumpet, cut out the timpani.

This is exactly what a sparse autoencoder does inside a neural network. A model's activation — a vector of several thousand numbers — is that "single microphone." Hundreds of meanings ring out in it at once, layered on top of one another. The SAE takes this mixed signal and decomposes it back into instruments — into separate, readable, individually meaningful features.

**The mechanism.** Take a stream of the model's internal activations — say, the outputs of the residual stream at some layer. Train an autoencoder on them: a network that compresses the input and reconstructs it. With two tricks. The first: the hidden layer is made *overcomplete* — not narrower than the input, but many times wider. If the activation has 512 dimensions, the autoencoder's dictionary may contain 16, 65, or 130 thousand directions. The second, and more important, trick: *sparsity* is imposed on the hidden activations — a penalty (usually L1, later a hard top-k selection) forcing only a handful of these thousands of directions to "light up" on any given token.

The result is arithmetically simple and conceptually powerful. The autoencoder learns to represent any activation as a sum of a small number of learned feature-vectors, each weighted by its own coefficient — think of it as a volume knob. The decoder is the dictionary: its columns are those very "instruments." And it turns out that the overwhelming majority of these columns are *monosemantic*: one responds to mentions of the Golden Gate Bridge, another to base64 strings, a third to the concept of "secrecy or discreetness," a fourth to DNA sequences. Not to a blurry cloud of twenty unrelated topics, like a typical neuron — but to one thing.

This is a direct operationalization of two neighbors in the bestiary. **Superposition** — the hypothesis that a model squeezes in more features than it has neurons, storing them as near-orthogonal directions in a shared space — is here not merely illustrated but *reversed*. The SAE is a machine for untangling superposition. And **monosemanticity**, from a philosophical wish ("if only each unit meant exactly one thing"), turns into a measurable property of the learned dictionary. The polysemantic neuron was the problem; the monosemantic feature is its solution.

And features can not only be read but also *turned*. If you fix a decoder column and forcibly raise its coefficient while the model is running — steering — behavior will veer in a predictable direction.

**Significance for safety.** For years interpretability ran up against the fact that the network's basic unit — the neuron — corresponds to nothing human. You could not ask the model: "Do you have a concept of deception inside you? Is it firing right now?" For the first time, the SAE gave a plausible dictionary for such a question. If we have tens of thousands of named features, then down the line one could build lie detectors from the model's innards, catch the activation of "dangerous" concepts before they spill into text, and intervene surgically rather than by crude fine-tuning. This is a bridge from "the model is a black box" to "the model is an instrument with labeled dials."

**How it came about.** The idea of dictionary learning and sparse coding is old — it comes from the computational neuroscience and signal processing of the 1990s (Olshausen and Field on V1 receptive fields). Applied to transformers, two lines of work converged in 2023. In the fall, Cunningham, Ewart, Riggs, Huben, and Sharkey posted "Sparse Autoencoders Find Highly Interpretable Features in Language Models" — showing that on small language models the SAE extracts features markedly more interpretable than neurons and even causally tied to behavior. Almost simultaneously the Anthropic team released "Towards Monosemanticity" — an exemplary, meticulous study on a tiny single-layer model, where they convincingly demonstrated, for the first time, thousands of monosemantic features and dissected their anatomy. And in May 2024 — "Scaling Monosemanticity": the same method stretched over the production-grade Claude 3 Sonnet, with millions of features, including abstract and multimodal ones. The public, though, was won over by a by-product — **Golden Gate Claude**: a demo where the engineers turned the volume of the "Golden Gate Bridge" feature up to the maximum, and the model began to consider itself the bridge, inserting it into any conversation. A curious viral moment — but behind it stood a serious claim: we had found a specific dial, and we could turn it.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The enthusiasm of 2023–2024 had cooled noticeably by 2025 — and rightly so.

First, *completeness*. The SAE reconstructs an activation imperfectly; a reconstruction error remains — the so-called "dark matter," the part of the signal the dictionary does not explain. As long as this fraction is not zero, we have no right to claim that *all* the meaning is caught; part of the orchestra is still glued together.

Second, *"dead" and splitting features*. A sizable fraction of the dictionary is dead features, never lighting up, computational ballast. And the live ones suffer feature splitting: you enlarge the dictionary — and one tidy "birds" feature breaks apart into "birds in cages," "birds in flight," "birds in idioms." What does this mean? That we have no *canonical* set of units: how many features are "really" in the model — 16 thousand or 16 million — is determined by a hyperparameter, not by nature. In 2025 papers came out (Leask, Bussmann, Nanda, and colleagues) with titles that said as much outright: "SAEs Do Not Find Canonical Units of Analysis."

Third — and this is the most painful — *are we catching the model's real meaning or one imposed by the autoencoder*. A feature that reads beautifully to a human is not necessarily a computational detail the network itself uses. Sparsity is our prior assumption about convenience, not a proven fact about the model's makeup. Evidence has emerged that for practical tasks — for example, for linear probing of specific properties — SAE features often *lose* to simple baseline methods over raw activations. If the untangled dictionary does not beat the "naive" baseline on a real task, an uncomfortable question arises: are we explaining the model — or composing a plausible story on top of it, beautiful and nearly useless?

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The right conclusion is not "SAEs failed" but "SAEs stopped being a magic wand and became a tool with its own domain of applicability." Three directions in which the field is moving.

First — *better architectures and honest metrics*. Top-k and gated SAEs remove some of the artifacts of the harsh L1 penalty; OpenAI (Gao and colleagues) took up scaling and, above all, *evaluation* — how to measure the quality of a dictionary at all, beyond "readable by eye." The yardstick shifts from the beauty of features to their causal and practical usefulness.

Second — *from features to circuits*. Individual dials are only an alphabet. The real goal is to read *sentences*: how features connect into computational circuits, how "betrayal" at layer N switches on "condemnation" at layer N+5. Anthropic and others are already assembling attribution graphs out of SAE features.

Third — *application to a specific threat*. Not "explain everything" but "catch this one thing": deception detectors, monitors for dangerous capabilities, targeted steering as a more surgical alternative to fine-tuning. Here the SAE undergoes trial by combat — and where it beats the baselines, it stays; where it does not, it yields.

We have not yet decomposed the orchestra into instruments fully or unambiguously. But for the first time we hold at least some score in our hands — and working with an incomplete score is incomparably better than with a single glued-together microphone.
