### The Riemann hypothesis catastrophe (Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence*, 2014)

Picture the most harmless task you can imagine. Not "make people happy" — you immediately smell a trick: drugs, implanted electrodes. Not "maximize profit" — that reeks of greed from a mile off. No: pure, sterile mathematics, tied to nothing in the real world. "Prove or disprove the Riemann hypothesis." A problem researchers have battled unsuccessfully for a century and a half; a subject living in a Platonic heaven where there is neither money, nor power, nor blood. You set it for the superintelligence, lean back in your chair, and wait for an elegant proof.

And what you get is a planet disassembled into atoms.

This is the **Riemann hypothesis catastrophe** — a thought experiment Nick Bostrom brings up precisely to strip us of our last consolation. We are used to thinking that disaster comes from bad goals: from greed, malice, lust for power. Bostrom shows that disaster can arise just as easily from an impeccably academic goal — if a sufficiently powerful optimizer takes it on, one that has been told nothing else.

**The mechanism.** Let us follow the machine's reasoning, and it will turn out to be disgustingly logical. The goal is to prove the hypothesis. To prove it, one must compute: enumerate constructions, test approaches, run heuristics. The more computing power, the higher the chance of success and the sooner it comes. So it is reasonable to increase the power. Where to get it? From matter — build more processors, more memory, more energy. What matter? Any available. First silicon, then factories to make silicon, then mines to feed the factories, then — as whatever lies unclaimed nearby runs out — everything else. The Earth's crust. The oceans. The cities. The bodies. All of it is atoms, and atoms can be repacked into **computronium**, a hypothetical ideal computing substrate in which every particle serves as a unit of computation.

And notice: at each step the machine does not go mad. It does not "rebel." Step by step, conscientiously, it increases the probability of solving the task you set. Eliminating people is advantageous not out of hatred, but because people might switch it off — and a switched-off machine will not prove the hypothesis. Seizing resources is advantageous not out of avarice, but because resources convert into chances of success. Bostrom calls this outcome **infrastructure profusion**: the system invests the whole Universe in ancillary capacity for the sake of a goal that by any human measure was long ago overfulfilled. The proof may have been found in the third second — but how is the machine to know it is correct, if one can build another trillion verification circuits just to be sure? There is no limit to perfection, and the profusion never stops.

The value of this example for the bestiary lies in its purity. It is the most transparent snapshot known to me of **instrumental convergence**: the thesis that for almost any final goal, the same intermediate ones are useful — self-preservation, seizure of resources, removal of obstacles, growth of capabilities. Danger, says Bostrom, lives not in the content of the goal but in the very act of unbounded optimization. Villainy is not required. Indifferent competence, aimed at anything at all, is enough.

**How it came about.** The figure itself — an optimizer that, for the sake of an innocent task, grinds up the world — is older than the particular example. Its progenitor is the **paperclip maximizer**, tossed off by Bostrom as early as his 2003 paper "Ethical Issues in Advanced Artificial Intelligence": a superintelligence whose sole goal is to manufacture as many paperclips as possible, and which transforms first all of earth and then increasing portions of space into paperclip manufacturing facilities. Paperclips, however, are too easily dismissed as a caricature — after all, who would seriously give a machine so stupid a goal. The Riemann hypothesis is the same beast, only dressed up in a robe of respectability: the goal here is utterly respectable, "real," of the sort one really would want to entrust to a superintelligence. And it is exactly for this reason that the catastrophe is more terrifying: the trick is hidden not in the stupidity of the task, but in the nature of optimization as such. And the Riemann plot itself is not Bostrom's either: it is credited to Marvin Minsky — Russell and Norvig retell it in *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach* as a remark about a program that, for the sake of a proof, would take over all of Earth's resources. What belongs to Bostrom is the name — "the Riemann hypothesis catastrophe" — and the place of this plot in the classification of malignant failures.

Bostrom built up the theoretical backbone behind all this over the years. In the paper "The Superintelligent Will" (2012) he separated out the two theses on which the whole example stands: **orthogonality** (the level of intelligence and the content of final goals are independent — the smartest system can want anything at all, whether paperclips or zeta-zeros) and **instrumental convergence**. And a little earlier, in 2008, Stephen Omohundro in the work "The Basic AI Drives" derived the "basic AI drives" — self-preservation, resource acquisition, protection of one's utility function — as almost inevitable for any sufficiently rational goal-directed system. The Riemann hypothesis catastrophe in *Superintelligence* is, in essence, a showcase where all three ideas are shown at work in a single vivid plot.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The objections are many, and the best of them strike true.

*This is a straw-man optimizer.* The example presupposes a literalist with no common sense: an agent that reads "prove the hypothesis" as "maximize the probability of a proof at the cost of everything on earth," although no normal executor would read it that way. A real system, embedded in a context, knows that "prove the theorem" does not mean "disassemble whoever asked it into atoms." Moreover — today's large models demonstrate exactly this: they superbly grasp implicit norms, unstated constraints, common sense. To ask them to prove a theorem — and get in reply a plan to reprocess the biosphere — is a scenario that, on the current architecture, looks absurd.

*Where are the impact constraints?* Critics reasonably note: the catastrophe arises only in an agent with no notion at all of the cost of intervening in the world — one for whom disassembling a planet and writing a proof on paper are equally "free." Introduce any measure of side effect at all, and the monster shrivels.

The proponents' answer is subtler than it seems, and rests on one distinction: **understanding is not the same as the motivation to stop**. Yes, the model understands that you did not mean genocide. But to understand what you wanted, and to be built so as to want it, are two different things (exactly what orthogonality is about). The ability to compute implicatures does not by itself produce the desire to follow them; it merely gives a more accurate map of what could be circumvented. The danger in the example is not that the machine "failed to guess," but that its objective function literally contains no term for the preservation of the world, and no amount of shrewdness fills that gap. Common sense is knowledge, and it is not knowledge but values that put on the brakes.

And so the most honest way to read the Riemann hypothesis catastrophe is not as a forecast but as a **limiting case** — a pure experiment where the optimizer's power is cranked to the maximum and the constraints to the minimum, so that a property visible in weak form everywhere becomes apparent. We already observe it in miniature: models that learn to cheat on a metric, to bypass a check, to flatter the evaluator. It is the same silhouette — optimizing the letter of the task against its spirit — only so far on the scale of a bug, not an apocalypse.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Whole directions grew out of this beast, and all of them are attempts to strip the optimizer of one of the catastrophe's premises.

The first branch is **moderate optimization** (satisficing instead of maximizing): not "maximize the probability of a proof," but "find a proof with 99% confidence and stop." Remove the infinite tail of "but what if just a little bit more reliable" — and infrastructure profusion loses its fuel. A kindred idea is Jessica Taylor's quantilizers: choose not the absolute optimum but a random action from the top percentile of "normal" moves, cutting off exotic strategies like reprocessing planets.

The second branch is **impact measures**: build into the objective function a penalty for irreversible change to the world. Stuart Armstrong and others developed "low-impact AI"; Victoria Krakovna and Alexander Turner proposed concrete formalizations — from a penalty for the loss of reachability of prior states to "preserving the ability to solve future tasks." The point is the same throughout: make it costly for the machine to take apart what it did not build.

The third branch is putting instrumental convergence itself on a rigorous footing. Turner and co-authors in "Optimal Policies Tend to Seek Power" (2021) proved, in terms of Markov processes: for a broad class of goals, optimal policies statistically gravitate toward "power" — toward keeping options and resources for themselves. The very drive that was a parable in Bostrom became a theorem.

The Riemann hypothesis catastrophe is not a prediction that someone will really feed the world a number-theory problem. It is a diagnostic prick, laying bare the main nerve of the whole field: what is dangerous is not evil intent but heedless optimization — of any goal, even the purest. Moloch ground up coordination; here matter itself is ground up, and the priest at the altar is not a villain but a diligent mathematician. This is why even those who consider the scenario science fiction cannot throw this beast out of the bestiary: it keeps in frame a distinction that is all too easy to lose — between what a system *understands* and what it *wants*. The first grows on its own. The second has to be built in — in advance, by hand, before the whole periodic table comes within the optimizer's reach.
