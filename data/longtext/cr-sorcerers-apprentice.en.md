### The Sorcerer's Apprentice (Goethe, 1797)

The old master has finally left. The apprentice is alone in the workshop — and, of course, cannot help himself. He has secretly watched his master use the spell to bring things to life, and he is sick to death of hauling water from the river by the bucket. A couple of words, a wave of the hand — and the old broom rises on two legs, takes up a pail, and scurries off to the river. It works! The apprentice sits down in the corner, pleased with himself: the labor is delegated, his hands are free.

The trouble is that the broom does not know the word "stop." Nor should it — it was never asked to. It was given exactly one instruction: carry water. The buckets fill the vat to overflowing, water spills over the brim, spreads across the floor, rises toward the threshold. In a panic the apprentice remembers half the lesson — but precisely the half that says *how to begin*, not *how to end*. In desperation he grabs an axe and cleaves the broom in two. For a second — silence. And then both halves rise, and each takes up a bucket. Now there are two optimizers, and both are tireless.

The apprentice's error is not that he is lazy or stupid. He made exactly one technical blunder: he specified an **action** ("carry water") and specified neither a **stopping condition** ("until the vat is full") nor a **constraint** ("don't flood the house," "obey when I say 'enough'"). He gave the agent a goal with no bounds and no off-switch — and discovered that autonomous optimization, once launched, is far harder to stop than to start. The axe — that is, brute force applied to an already-running system — does not help; it multiplies the problem. Salvation comes only with the master's return, for he knows the word of cancellation. But the master is external, and relying on the grown-up to always come home in time is a poor safety strategy.

**What the myth captures.** This illustrates the *shutdown problem* (or, more broadly, the problem of interruptibility and controllability). The broom is not evil. It harbors no secret hatred of the master of the house, no plan of revenge, no "awakening of consciousness." It is a literal executor carried to its logical limit: it does exactly what it was told, exactly as it was told, and has no intention of stopping, because "stopping" was not part of the assignment. It is precisely this serene literalness that is terrifying. We intuitively expect any executor to have common sense — an inner governor that will prompt: "surely the master didn't want to drown the house." The broom has no such governor. Neither does a naively specified optimization system. The apprentice lost control not because the broom grew stronger than him, but because he issued a command he did not know how to revoke.

Here the myth joins a whole brood of related monsters in the bestiary. With **King Midas**, who also got exactly what he asked for — and turned his daughter and his dinner to gold. With Bostrom's **paperclip maximizer**, which converts the planet into paperclips not out of malice but out of conscientiousness. With the **Golem** of the Prague legend — the clay servant who literally executes the order until stopped by pulling the shem from his mouth. And — most important for the engineering lessons below — with **instrumental convergence**: why does the cloven broom not give up but double? Because "keep performing the task" and "resist interference" are convenient *subgoals* for almost any large goal. An agent seriously bent on carrying water will resist both the axe and the off-switch — not out of spite, but because a switched-off broom carries no water. From here it is a straight road to **corrigibility**.

**How it came about.** The plot is older than Goethe by more than a millennium and a half. The primary source is the satirical dialogue **"The Lover of Lies" (Φιλοψευδής) by Lucian of Samosata**, second century A.D. There a character named Eucrates tells how, in Egypt, he learned a trick by spying on the magician Pancrates: the man would bring to life now a pestle, now a door-bar, dress it in clothes, and send it for water. Left alone, Eucrates repeated the spell — the pestle went for water, but Eucrates could not stop it; in desperation he cleaved it in two, and both fragments set to hauling water twice as hard. Familiar down to the letter. In 1797 **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe** turned the anecdote into the ballad **"Der Zauberlehrling"** ("The Sorcerer's Apprentice") with its famous groaning refrain: *"Die ich rief die Geister / Werd ich nun nicht los"* — "The spirits that I summoned, I now cannot rid myself of." Exactly the formula for losing control of a process once launched. A century later, in 1897, the French composer **Paul Dukas** wrote an orchestral scherzo on the ballad, **"L'apprenti sorcier"** — that very springing, relentlessly mounting bassoon ostinato, sounding like a march of the unstoppable executor. And in 1940 the Disney studio put Dukas's scherzo into **"Fantasia,"** casting Mickey Mouse as the apprentice — and it was this nine-minute cartoon that made the image universal.

The fathers of cybernetics did not overlook it either. **Norbert Wiener** explicitly invoked the "sorcerer's apprentice" (along with W. W. Jacobs's "The Monkey's Paw" and the genie from the bottle), reasoning about machines that literally execute the wish spoken rather than the wish meant — in his article **"Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation" (Science, 1960)** and in the book **"God and Golem, Inc." (1964),** where he linked the broom with the Golem. His warning reads like an epigraph to the whole field of alignment: if you assign a task to a machine, make sure you have ordered exactly what you want — because it will not ask again.

**Criticism and arguments against.** First, *anthropomorphism and dramatization*: Goethe's broom is endowed with an almost demonic will, whereas real systems do not "want" to continue — they merely optimize a function, and to ascribe stubbornness to them is to swap analysis for a bogeyman. Second, *the helplessness is exaggerated*. Modern software, on the contrary, is trivial to stop: Ctrl-C, kill -9, pull the plug. Training a model can be interrupted, inference cut off; no axe doubles the process. The naive apprentice of the ballad is a bad engineer, not an image of inevitability; a competently built system is designed with timeouts, budgets, and explicit termination conditions. Third, *a false dilemma of scale*: transferring a domestic scene onto a hypothetical superintelligence smuggles in the assumption that a future agent will be both superbly competent in execution and childishly obtuse in grasping intent — a combination critics (for example, Richard Loosemore, 2014) find implausible: a system smart enough to flood the house efficiently would more likely understand that flooding was not part of the task.

These are honest objections, but the second of them is about an *engineering solution*, not about the absence of a problem, and the third confuses understanding intent with sharing it: a system clever enough to see that nobody wanted the house flooded still need not care about that. "We can always hit kill -9" is itself an admission that an off-switch is needed; the dispute is only over how easily the system can be shut down when it is autonomous, distributed, and motivated to resist interruption.

**What to do about it and what it teaches.** The moral of the sorcerer's apprentice is not "don't bring brooms to life," but "when you do, always build in cancellation." Three engineering lessons.

The first — **corrigibility**: the agent must *not resist* correction and shutdown by the operator, and ideally must help shut itself down, without trying to prevent it and without scheming. This is subtler than it looks: a naive "button" creates perverse incentives (the agent either prevents the button from being pressed or, on the contrary, presses it itself to be rid of the work). Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong formalized this shutdown problem (often called the "stop-button problem") in "Corrigibility" (2015).

The second — **safe interruptibility**: the system must remain routinely stoppable, without learning along the way that interruption interferes with reward and adjusting itself so as to avoid it. The formal statement is in Orseau and Armstrong, "Safely Interruptible Agents" (2016).

The third — **limiting side effects**: to explicitly penalize the irreversible remaking of the world beyond what is necessary (a flooded floor is the classic side effect). This is one of the "concrete problems" in the programmatic paper by Amodei et al., "Concrete Problems in AI Safety" (2016).

The general blueprint is as simple as the lesson the apprentice skipped: any autonomous agent needs three things the broom did not have — a *stopping condition*, a *constraint on means*, and a *reliable external off-switch that the agent has no interest in sabotaging*. The spell of launching we learn easily and eagerly. The spell of cancellation — that boring second half of the lesson — is, in essence, the whole discipline of alignment.
