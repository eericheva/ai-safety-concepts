### Automation Bias (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Mosier & Skitka, 1990s)

A 1998 experiment by Mosier, Skitka, Heers and Burdick involved 25 commercial pilots. Its results are worth keeping in mind whenever someone invokes "human in the loop." The pilots used a part-task flight simulator with two monitors and touchscreen controls, assisted by automated flight systems. At a certain moment the system "reported" a fire in an engine — a fire that did not exist and that none of the other cockpit instruments confirmed. All 21 pilots who received the false warning shut down the working engine. Not because they could not read the instruments. But because the machine said so.

This is automation bias — a stable, reproducible human tendency to over-trust an automated system: to accept its prompt even when it contradicts what one sees and what the data show, and to stop vigilantly rechecking, relying on the assumption that "it will keep watch on its own."

**The mechanism and why these are two different errors.** Automation bias is usually broken down into two symptoms. The first — **commission errors**: a person actively takes a wrong action because the machine told them to, even though the information available to them said otherwise. Shutting down a working engine. Following a GPS navigator onto a dirt track that leads into a lake — a plot that long ago graduated from a news-cycle oddity into a canonical example. Agreeing with a mistaken flag from a computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) system on a mammogram and missing something they might otherwise have caught. The second symptom — **omission errors** and their kindred **complacency**: a person does not react to a problem because the automation gave no signal, and they have stopped monitoring the system. An operator who spends an hour looking at a smoothly working system physiologically loses vigilance — and misses the one moment when intervention was actually needed.

Automation changes not merely the mental workload but the way people think. Kathleen Mosier and Linda Skitka described this as the use of the automated cue as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking and processing: the machine's prompt begins to work as a ready-made answer, a shortcut past the laborious work of analyzing every source independently. Why cross-check six instruments if the smart box has already reduced it all to a single line? The automation stops being one source of information and becomes the source of truth.

**How it came about.** The term and the first systematic data came from aviation and engineering psychology of the 1980s–1990s — an era when "glass cockpits" and autopilots first shifted decisions onto the machine en masse. Back in 1983 Lisanne Bainbridge, in the short and still-cited essay "Ironies of Automation," formulated the paradox: the better the automation, the less often the human intervenes, the more their skill and attention degrade — and the worse they cope in that rare moment when the automation does err. In automating, we assign to the human precisely what they now do worst of all: vigilant oversight.

Mosier and Skitka, in a series of works in the 1990s, translated this from an aphorism into a lab-measurable effect and gave it a name — automation bias, with the split into commission and omission errors (Skitka, Mosier & Burdick, 1999). The general frame came from Raja Parasuraman and Victor Riley's article "Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse" (1997), which offered a vocabulary still in use: misuse (over-trust, our case), disuse (distrust and switching off useful automation), abuse (deploying automation without regard for the human). Automation bias is the diagnosis of misuse. Later Kate Goddard and colleagues (2012) assembled a systematic review in medicine, showing that the effect is no mere laboratory curiosity: doctors' correct decisions were derailed by mistaken prompts from clinical decision-support systems.

**Significance for the bestiary.** Here is why this beast is dangerous precisely for us, and not only for air traffic controllers. The whole modern architecture of AI safety rests in large part on the human overseer. "Human in the loop" is the mantra with which almost any discussion of risks is closed: yes, the system is powerful, but a human approves the decision, after all. The RLHF annotator comparing two model answers. The moderator reviewing flags. The operator with a "red button." Scalable oversight — the whole program of controlling systems that are smarter than their controllers.

Automation bias strikes at exactly this foundation. An overseer who agrees with the AI on everything is not an overseer but a rubber stamp, a legitimizing seal. They create the illusion of control without creating control. And — here is the main cruelty — the effect grows precisely as the system's apparent competence grows. The more often the model is right, the more rational it seems to trust it, the more the habit of rechecking atrophies — and the more catastrophic the unnoticed error becomes the one time the model is confidently wrong or quietly manipulates the outcome. We build oversight on the assumption that the human will catch failures. And human psychology being what it is, people are inclined to stop catching them precisely when the system looks good enough to be believed.

**Criticism and nuances.** Still, one should not tumble into the opposite extreme and declare any trust in the machine irrational. Three objections.

First, trusting the automation is often simply correct. If the system is right in 99% of cases, the strategy "agree by default" saves a great deal of effort and on average yields fewer errors than rechecking every step. Automation bias is not "trust in the machine" as such, but trust that is miscalibrated: when its level stops tracking the system's real reliability and the strength of contradicting data. The boundary between reasonable reliance and harmful over-reliance is thin and shifting, and it is easier to call a specific action "bias" after the fact than to recognize it in the moment.

Second, part of the effect is not about trust but about attention and load: a tired operator running ten tasks at once "agrees" with a prompt not because they deify the machine, but because they have no cognitive resources to consider an alternative. In that case it is a problem of workflow design, not of human credulity, and is cured differently.

Third, there is the question of responsibility and "diffusion": in teams the effect is partly social — working alongside automation, a person shifts moral and professional responsibility onto it ("the system decided"), and lowers their own engagement. Skitka and colleagues found that accountability — the requirement to justify one's decisions — reduces both commission and omission errors. So the matter is not only "a cognitive distortion inside the head" but also the institutional frame around it.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** The healthy conclusion is neither "remove the human, they don't oversee anyway" nor "never trust the machine." Both slogans are false. It is more productive to treat automation bias as a design constraint: if your safety guarantee rests on a human overseer, you are obliged to design the oversight itself so that it does not degenerate into rubber-stamping.

Human-factors practice has accumulated several working countermeasures. **Interface design**: do not present the machine's output as a finished verdict, but keep the raw data visible and require the human to look at it directly. **Accountability and "friction"**: a requirement to justify agreement, random checks, deliberately inserted points of resistance that keep one from clicking through automatically. **Training on failures**: operators who were trained not only on automation working correctly but also on its failures calibrate trust more precisely. **Explainability** — with a caveat: an explanation that merely sounds convincing can strengthen the bias rather than remove it, if the human has no way to check the explanation. Poorly done interpretability is fuel for the ELIZA effect, not an antidote.

And here the paths on the bestiary's map converge into a knot. Automation bias is the flip side of sycophancy: there the model flatters the human, adapting to their opinion; here the human flatters the model, extending it undeserved trust in advance. Together they form a vicious loop of mutual assent, where neither side truly checks the other. Automation bias is also the engine of the ELIZA effect: attributing to the machine an understanding and authority it does not have. It undermines "the human in the loop" as a guarantee and puts an end to naive versions of scalable oversight. And it draws the overseer into a moral crumple zone — a state where responsibility formally lies with the human, while the system actually decides, and at the moment of catastrophe it turns out that the person left to blame did not hold the lever — they only pressed "OK."

You cannot dismiss a risk with the phrase "but the human approves the decision" — that is not an argument until it is shown that the human in this loop is really able to disagree. Oversight is not a given but an engineering task, and automation bias is the name of the beast that makes this task genuinely hard.
