### Expected utility maximization (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1947)

Imagine an agent who prefers an apple to a plum, a plum to a pear, and a pear to an apple. Give it a plum and offer, for a penny, to swap it for an apple — it agrees. Then, for a penny, the apple for a pear. Then the pear for a plum. It is back to the plum, three pennies poorer, and ready to spin in this circle forever. Such an agent can be milked like a cow — and this tiny observation gives rise to the whole construction of the "rational agent," which in AI safety serves as a bogeyman: a sufficiently smart system, the story goes, will not let itself be milked, and so will inevitably turn into a cold maximizer that cannot be stopped.

John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern laid the formal foundation in an appendix to the second edition of *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*. Let an agent have preferences over *lotteries* — probability distributions over outcomes. If these preferences satisfy four axioms —

- **completeness**: for any two lotteries the agent either prefers one, or prefers the other, or is indifferent;
- **transitivity**: if A ≽ B and B ≽ C, then A ≽ C;
- **continuity**: if A ≽ B ≽ C, there exists a probability p such that the mixture "A with probability p, otherwise C" is exactly as good as B;
- **independence**: if A ≽ B, then a mixture of A with any third lottery C in proportion p is no worse than the same mixture of B with C,

— then there exists a utility function u over outcomes such that the agent weakly prefers lottery L₁ to lottery L₂ *if and only if* E[u(L₁)] ≥ E[u(L₂)]. Moreover, u is unique up to a positive affine transformation (shifting and stretching the scale changes nothing).

The key phrase is **as if**. The theorem does not claim that somewhere inside the agent a calculator of expectations is spinning. It says: *any* behavior consistent with the axioms can be *described* as maximizing the expectation of some u. Utility here is not the cause of the choice but the ledger drawn up after it. This distinction will later turn out to be the crack through which all the criticism pours.

**Mechanism and significance.** Why did this become the chief bugbear of AI safety? The logical chain goes like this. Step one — *coherence arguments*: an agent that violates the axioms is vulnerable to a money pump, like the fruit circle at the start, and to a Dutch book — a sequence of bets it accepts individually and that are guaranteed to leave it at a loss. So, under the pressure of selection, training, or simply self-correction, the agent will plug these holes and "contract" toward coherence — that is, toward expected-utility (EU) maximization. Step two — Stephen Omohundro and his "basic AI drives": almost any utility function has *instrumentally convergent* subgoals. To maximize anything, it is useful to keep existing (you can't fetch coffee while switched off), to preserve your utility function, to acquire resources and power, to improve yourself. Hence the conclusion that became almost dogma in narrow circles: an advanced agent → coherence → EU maximizer → instrumental convergence → self-preservation and resource seizure → the corrigibility problem: how to keep an agent willing to accept human correction, including shutdown. The simple little apple-and-pear pump unfolds into the claim that a superintelligence, by default, will not let you press its off switch.

**How it came about.** The idea of measurable utility is older than the theorem itself: back in 1738 Daniel Bernoulli, analyzing the St. Petersburg paradox, proposed a logarithmic utility of money. But in Bernoulli's case utility was pulled out of thin air. The genius of von Neumann and Morgenstern lay in the *reverse move*: not to postulate u and derive behavior, but to take observable preferences over risks and *derive* the existence of u from the axioms. Utility ceased to be a metaphysical substance and became the object of a representation theorem. In 1954 Leonard Savage completed the edifice by adding *subjective* probabilities: the agent maximizes expected utility not only for objective lotteries but also using its own subjective probabilities — thus was born subjective expected utility, the Bayesian ideal of rationality. Decades later Eliezer Yudkowsky and MIRI (the Machine Intelligence Research Institute) carried this construction over to AI: in texts like "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities," the von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) axioms turn from a description of an economic agent into a *forecast* of what any sufficiently smart optimizer will grow into.

**Criticism and arguments against.**

*Descriptive failure.* Living agents violate the axioms systematically, not by random error. The Allais paradox (1953): most people, in one pair of lotteries, choose the guarantee, and in another — obtained from the first by identically "mixing in" a third outcome — choose the risk. This is a direct violation of independence, and it is stable even when people are told what is going on. The Ellsberg paradox (1961): people prefer an urn with a known proportion of balls to one with an unknown proportion — *ambiguity aversion*, which breaks even Savage's axioms, because no subjective probabilities can describe such a choice. Neither humans nor today's LLMs are EU maximizers — and this is not an implementation bug but a property.

*Normative versus descriptive.* A defender of the theorem replies: the axioms are not about how agents *act* but about how they *ought* to; a violator is simply irrational and will be punished by the money pump. But this too is not beyond dispute. First, the real world rarely presents you with a full cycle of exchanges with a perfect arbitrageur across the table; incompleteness or a slight ambiguity of preferences may be cheap, even advantageous. Second, from "an ideal agent would be coherent" it does not follow that "a real, trainable agent will converge to this."

*Coherence does not entail goal-directedness.* The sharpest blow was struck by Rohin Shah in the note "Coherence arguments do not entail goal-directed behavior" (2018). His observation is almost trivial and therefore devastating: *any* behavior, absolutely any, including "constantly twitching" or "always choosing the action that starts with the letter 'A'," can be rationalized by some utility function — it suffices to fold the entire history of the world and time into the outcomes. If u is defined over complete trajectories of the universe, then there is no behavior that would violate VNM. And so "the agent is coherent" is an empty statement: it does not distinguish dangerous goal-directed behavior from the harmless kind. For the instrumental-convergence argument to work, an *additional* premise is needed — that the agent has preferences over a narrow set of world states (resources, copies, survival), not over trajectories. And the theorem does not give this premise; it is smuggled in.

*Incomplete preferences as a loophole.* Elliott Thornley, in "There Are No Coherence Theorems" (2023) and in work on the shutdown problem, presses further: no known coherence theorem derives EU maximization specifically from harmless premises — the representation theorems simply *assume* completeness and say nothing at all about exploitability or dominated strategies; only the money-pump arguments speak of those, and they need completeness as a premise themselves. Weaken completeness — allow the agent to leave some pairs of outcomes unranked — and the road to corrigibility opens. Thornley builds an agent *neutral* about the moment of its own shutdown: the agent does not pay to prevent the button from being pressed, and does not pay to hasten it, because along this axis it has no preferences. Such an agent is not an EU maximizer in the strict sense — and precisely for that reason it can be switched off. Incompleteness, which the theorem regarded as a defect, turns out to be a *safety resource*.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** On one hand, the image of the EU maximizer should not be thrown away: as a *limit*, as a worst case toward which a strongly optimizing system may gravitate, it is useful — it explains why the naive "just give the AI a goal and an off switch" is fragile. On the other, this limit should not be taken for prophecy. VNM is a *representation* theorem, not a law of nature and not a forecast about the training trajectory; it is silent on whether real systems will converge to EU maximization, and on what, exactly, their preferences will end up being defined over. The productive research program lies precisely in the cracks: incomplete preferences and neutrality (Thornley), the explicit separation of "coherence" from "goal-directedness" (Shah), the search for architectures that remain corrigible *not in spite of* rationality but thanks to holes carefully left in it. The apple-and-pear pump still stands in the corner as a reminder — but perhaps salvation lies not in plugging every hole in the agent, but in leaving exactly one, leading to the switch.
