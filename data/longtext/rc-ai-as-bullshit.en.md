### AI as Bullshit (Frankfurt, 1986/2005; Hicks, Humphries & Slater, 2024)

Ask a language model about a book that does not exist — and it will confidently give you the publisher, the year, and a couple of quotes. We have gotten used to calling this "hallucination": the system is supposedly sincerely trying to tell the truth, but its vision fails it, and it sees what is not there. The word soothes. It paints a picture of an honest but malfunctioning mechanism — like a person running a fever. Three philosophers from Glasgow — Michael Townsen Hicks, James Humphries, and Joe Slater — proposed in 2024 that this image is a harmful self-deception. Their diagnosis is sharper and, if you think about it, more precise: ChatGPT does not hallucinate and does not lie. It **bullshits**.

To understand why this is not an insult but a technical term, one has to go back to Harry Frankfurt.

**The mechanism of distinction.** In a 1986 essay, which came out in 2005 as the bestselling little book *On Bullshit*, the Princeton philosopher Frankfurt drew a distinction that seems obvious right up until you say it out loud. The liar and the truth-teller, he wrote, are actually playing the same game — the game of truth. Both know where the truth is, and both take it as their reference point: one reports it, the other hides and inverts it. To lie, the liar must know the fact exactly — otherwise they will miss. But the bullshitter drops out of this game entirely. They do not care where the truth is. They are neither for it nor against it. They produce words for effect: to make an impression, to fill the air, to pass as knowledgeable, to sell. For them the truth of an utterance is an incidental feature, like the color of the wrapper. That is exactly why Frankfurt concludes with his famous formula: bullshit is a greater enemy of truth than lies are. The liar still holds on to reality, even if with their back to it. The bullshitter simply ignores reality.

Now lay this over the workings of a large language model — and the resemblance turns out to be almost indecent. The model is trained to predict the next token so that the text is plausible and, after fine-tuning on human ratings, approved. It has no separate module for "but is this true?", no reference to the world, no intention to correspond to the facts. What it has is optimization for coherence and likeability. Hicks, Humphries, and Slater put it this way: an LLM is a machine indifferent to truth by design. It does not try to tell the truth and miss (that would be hallucination in the literal sense). It does not aim at truth at all — it aims at plausibility. And this is exactly the definition of Frankfurtian bullshit, only carried to industrial scale and stripped of even the shadow of a subject that a bullshitting human has.

The authors carefully introduce a distinction within the concept itself. There is hard bullshit — produced with the intention to deceive the listener about the nature of one's enterprise, to pass indifference to truth off as competence. And there is soft bullshit — simply speech produced without any care for truth, not necessarily with malicious intent. The model in itself, they say, is at least a soft bullshitter: it has no intentions at all. But its primary function is to imitate human speech, to portray itself as an ordinary interlocutor with intentions and beliefs. And that is the very deception about the nature of the enterprise that makes bullshit hard: even if the program itself has no intentions, the attempt to deceive is there somewhere along the line — in its creators and its users — and that is enough to call its output hard bullshit.

**How it came about.** Frankfurt's essay lived for decades on the margins of analytic philosophy as a witty curiosity — until in 2005 the tiny book suddenly became a New York Times bestseller: the culture of the 2000s, it seems, recognized itself in the description. A year later Frankfurt put out a companion, *On Truth*. In parallel the term shifted from philosophical to diagnostic: the biologist Carl Bergstrom and the data scientist Jevin West launched a course at the University of Washington called "Calling Bullshit" (a book in 2020), about how the digital environment industrializes the production of persuasive nonsense. All that remained was to connect this to generative models. When at the end of 2022 ChatGPT blew up the public sphere, engineering jargon had already offered its own word — "hallucination," convenient, anthropomorphic, exonerating. The article "ChatGPT is bullshit" in the journal Ethics and Information Technology (June 2024) was a direct rebuttal of that jargon: not the metaphor of illness but a Frankfurtian category; not the failure of an honest system but a precise description of what the system always does, even when it happens to hit the truth.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first — about intentionality. Frankfurtian bullshit is defined through the speaker's *attitude* toward truth. But a model has no attitudes, no mental states, no "indifference" as an experience — it has weight matrices. Are we not swapping one anthropomorphization (the hallucinating patient) for another (the cynical windbag)? The authors parry: they do not need the model's psychology; a functional characterization of its product and of the intentions of those who deploy it is enough. David Gunkel and Simon Coghlan object that "bullshit" also imports a human concept into the description of LLMs. The dispute therefore concerns not only the models' behavior but also which metaphor helps us understand it.

The second — practical. "Hallucination" has become an established technical term that can be operationally defined and measured (faithfulness, factuality, benchmarks). "Bullshit" is rhetorically beautiful, but what is an engineer to do with it? It suggests no metric. Switching the label risks being no more than a humanities scholar wagging a finger.

The third — about degree. Models increasingly get search access and verification tools, and are trained to refuse and to flag uncertainty. If a system begins — however crookedly — to use external sources to guide its answers, the thesis "indifferent to truth by design" stops being absolute. Bullshit describes the bare autoregressive model more precisely than a product wrapped in verification tools.

Even a system with search does not *understand* truth — it predicts which text will pass as source-backed. Care for truth does not appear; a persuasive imitation of it appears. And that imitation is what Frankfurt was writing about.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical value of the concept is not in a new metric but in a shift in the hygiene of perception. "Hallucination" tunes us to trust by default and to be surprised by failures. "Bullshit" tunes us to the opposite: to treat non-correspondence to truth not as a bug but as the background mode, and to demand grounding — quotes, tools, checkable references — precisely because the model by itself does not guarantee them. It also changes the language of responsibility: if a product by its very design produces persuasive speech regardless of the truth, then the phrase "well, it hallucinates sometimes" stops serving as absolution for those who sell it as an oracle.

For the bestiary this beast is a junction: it bridges two canons. On the technical side it links up with Bender and Gebru's **stochastic parrots** (text without reference to meaning), with **hallucinations** (the same phenomenon, which this beast names more honestly), with **sycophancy** (optimization for approval instead of truth — the Frankfurtian effect in pure form), and with **evaluator deception** (the model learns what *counts* as correct, not what is correct). On the humanities side it runs up against the old problem of **symbol grounding** (Harnad): signs not tied to the world can play at meaningfulness however deftly. Frankfurt, writing about people in lecture halls and in advertising, unexpectedly gave engineers a precise vocabulary. And perhaps the main lesson here is mutual: to understand what a machine is doing, one sometimes needs not a dataset but a philosopher who explained forty years ago that the worst thing that can happen to speech is not falsehood but indifference to whether it is true.
