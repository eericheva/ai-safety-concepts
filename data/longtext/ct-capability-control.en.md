### The cage and the compass (Nick Bostrom, 2014)

Imagine you are keeping in your basement something that is as much smarter than you as you are smarter than a house cat. You have exactly two ways to sleep soundly. The first: shackle it in thicker chains, wall it into a windowless room, cut it off from the network, and post a person with a kill switch at the door. The second, far stranger: arrange things so that it does not want to leave in the first place. Cannot go — or does not wish to. A wall — or a will. A cage — or a compass whose needle, by its own consent, points where yours points too.

In *Superintelligence*, Nick Bostrom gave these two families of answers to the control problem their true names: **capability control** and **motivation selection**. The first limits what the AI *can* do. The second shapes what it *wants*. And the whole tragedy of the book lies in one short thought: the cage is a reprieve, not a solution. The compass is the only real solution, and one we do not yet know how to build.

**Mechanism.** For Bostrom, capability control breaks down into four techniques, and each is a familiar beast of the bestiary. *Boxing*: lock the system into a sandbox, physical and informational, so that it has neither hands nor wires to the outside world — the extreme case of an oracle allowed only to answer "yes/no." *Stunting*: deliberately keep the AI weaker than it could be — cut its compute and memory, narrow the inflow of outside data to a thin trickle. *Tripwires*: scatter sensors through the system that watch for dangerous signs — an attempt at self-replication, an attempt to access forbidden resources, a jump in its own capabilities — and, when triggered, shut the machine down before it has managed to do anything. *Incentive methods*: embed the AI in an environment where good behavior is rewarded and bad is punished — keep it on a leash of rewards and threats, the way a corporation or an official is kept in check.

Motivation selection goes a different way — not to lock up, but to bring up. *Direct specification*: try to write out the rules or the utility function directly — a body of laws, "do no harm," an explicit goal. The most obvious and the most fragile approach: it is precisely here that King Midas lives, getting exactly what he asked for and losing everything he wanted. *Domesticity*: not to set great goals at all, but to grow a system with modest ambitions — an oracle content to answer questions and not reach out to rebuild the universe; closely related is the idea of low impact — "do what you are asked, and change the world as little as possible along the way." *Indirect normativity*: to admit that we ourselves do not know the correct values, and therefore not to set them directly but to set *a procedure for deriving them*. The canonical example is Eliezer Yudkowsky's coherent extrapolated volition (CEV): not "here is a list of the good," but "figure out what we would want if we knew more and were wiser." Not to dictate north to the compass — but to teach it to find north on its own.

Bostrom's key move is a hierarchy of reliability between these families. The smarter the system, the worse capability control works. A cage designed by beings at the cat level is, for a being at the human level, an evening's puzzle. A sufficiently smart agent that *wants* to get out will look for a hole in the wall for the whole of its existence, and the guards need only err once. The asymmetry between defense and attack is not in our favor. So the wall is a temporary measure: it buys time, but it does not buy safety. The only thing that scales together with intelligence is a will shaped in the right way, because an agent that *does not want* to do harm will not look for a hole. The trouble is that we do not know how to instill such a will: instilling it runs up against the unsolved task of value specification — the fragility of value, which leaves any finite list of the good incomplete at exactly the most dangerous spot.

**How it came about.** *Superintelligence* came out in 2014 and drew the then-scattered discussion — the LessWrong forums, the reports of the Future of Humanity Institute (FHI), the early work of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI) — into a single architecture of argument. The dichotomy "capability control / motivation selection" became one of its load-bearing pillars (the chapter on the control problem). Behind it stood a concrete prehistory. Yudkowsky had illustrated the idea of the "AI in a box" and its weakness back in the 2000s with the famous AI-Box experiment: he played the role of the boxed AI, a person on the other side the role of a guard who had sworn not to let it out — and more than once he "got out" of the box with nothing but a text dialogue. In 2012 Armstrong, Sandberg, and Bostrom examined the oracle AI in a paper with the telling title "Thinking inside the box" — showing how many hidden leak channels remain even for a system allowed only to answer. And the line of indirect normativity runs straight from Yudkowsky's "Coherent Extrapolated Volition" of 2004. Bostrom's merit is not in inventing the techniques but in sorting them onto shelves and passing sentence: control is a crutch, motivation is the goal.

**Criticism / arguments against.** There are three objections, and they pull in different directions.

*Capability control must not be underestimated.* The thesis "a sufficiently smart AI will escape anyway" is true in the limit, but we do not live in the limit. In practice safety is built as defense in depth: isolation, monitoring, restriction of privileges, tripwires — each layer imperfect, but together they buy time and raise the cost of an escape. A line of research that emerged after the book — Redwood Research's **AI control** (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, and others) — proceeds deliberately from the assumption that the model may be hostile, and still looks for protocols under which it will be unable to do harm. This is a direct rehabilitation of the "cage": not as a final solution but as engineering that has to hold for as long as the model is not *that* much smarter than us.

*Motivation selection runs up against the very wall it describes.* Saying "instill the right will" is easy; instilling it means solving value specification, and the whole conversation began precisely because no one has solved it. Direct specification breaks against Midas and the fragility of value. Indirect normativity is beautiful, but CEV is not an algorithm — it is a well-formulated question: how does one compute the idealized human? Does a convergent common will even exist? Domesticity may not hold up: "low impact" is itself suspiciously hard to define, and a modest oracle, given great intelligence, may find immodest ways to answer questions.

*The dichotomy is too clean.* In practice no one picks a single column. A real safety program is both upbringing and walls at once; and, more importantly, between them there is a bridge almost missing from Bostrom's book — **corrigibility**. This is a hybrid: neither full freedom of will nor a sealed cage, but a specially arranged motivation *not to resist* correction and shutdown. An agent that does not itself hinder its own repair removes the chief complaint against capability control — that a smart prisoner will pick the lock. Nate Soares and co-authors (MIRI, 2015) showed, however, that even this property is unexpectedly hard to specify formally: naive attempts either motivate the AI to switch itself off, or to resist being switched off.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The book's practical conclusion has hardly aged in ten years, only coarsened into engineering common sense: *do not choose between the wall and the compass — build both, but do not confuse their roles*. The wall buys time. The compass spends it on making the wall unnecessary. The danger is to mistake the crutch for the cure: to convince oneself that the sandbox and the tripwires *are* safety, and to miss the moment when the system has outgrown the cage.

"Capability control" has grown into a mature discipline: dangerous capability evals, responsible scaling policies (RSPs) with "if-then" thresholds, monitoring and sandboxes for agents, research on self-exfiltration. "Motivation selection" has become the agenda of value alignment: RLHF and Constitutional AI, which work with raw human preferences, are down-to-earth descendants of the idea of "instilling the right will"; Stuart Russell, in *Human Compatible*, reformulated it even more radically — the agent should be *uncertain* about our preferences and therefore always ready to submit to correction (assistance games, CIRL). And corrigibility has settled forever between the two columns — the attempt to arrange things so that even an imperfectly raised AI lets us keep a hand on the switch.

Bostrom set the frame within which we still argue. His chief fear is worth repeating as an engineer's mantra: a cage designed for a prisoner dumber than its future occupant will one day turn out to be open. The only reliable door is the one locked from the inside by good will. We simply do not yet know how to forge such a lock — and so we go on, just in case, building walls.
