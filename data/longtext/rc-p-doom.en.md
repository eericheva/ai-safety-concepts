### p(doom) (the AI safety community, ~2022–2023)

Picture a party in San Francisco where, instead of "what do you do for work?", people ask "so what's your p(doom)?" And it is no joke: a person takes a sip, thinks it over, and answers — "about ten percent." Or "ninety." Or "zero, bro, we're gonna build a god." Your interlocutor uses this number to pigeonhole you instantly — doomer, normie, accelerationist — faster than they could with your zodiac sign.

p(doom) is the subjective probability a person assigns to the proposition that the development of AI will lead to an existential catastrophe: the extinction of humanity, or an irreversible, final loss of our potential. The letter "p" is for probability, "doom" for the fate itself, which for some will arrive through nuclear war with autonomous drones, for others through the quiet removal of humans from all the levers of control, and for still others in some way nobody understands but which is definitely bad. Formally it is a Bayesian degree of belief, a number between zero and one. In fact it is a cultural password.

### How one number became a meme

Probabilistic language was in circulation in the rationalist and AI safety community long before the meme: there it is customary to "put your beliefs on the line," to state your credences, to play at calibration. p(doom) takes to its limit the habit of compressing an entire worldview into a single figure. Convenient: no need to recount twenty pages about mesa-optimization and the capabilities gap — it is enough to say "0.35" and everyone will nod knowingly.

The number does double duty. On one hand, it is an honest attempt to express uncertainty — not "everything will be fine" and not "we're all going to die," but a weighted bet. On the other, it is a banner. Eliezer Yudkowsky believes that the most likely result of building a superhumanly smart AI under circumstances not unlike today's is that literally everyone on Earth dies. He stands at one pole. Geoffrey Hinton, who left Google in order to speak freely about the risks, gave an all-things-considered estimate of 10–20% in 2024, while putting his own independent impression above 50%. The median ML researcher with their "well, about five percent" is in the middle. The ideologues of e/acc (effective accelerationism) with their proud zero (or even negative values, as a joke) are at the other end. The spread — from near zero to over ninety percent — is not a range of estimates of one phenomenon. These are different phenomena called by one word.

### How it came about

The exact authorship of the term cannot be established. The earliest known use is a 2009 LessWrong comment by programmer Tim Tyler, but p(doom) crystallized in exchanges on LessWrong, in labs' Slack channels, and in AI-Twitter threads somewhere in the early 2020s as semi-ironic jargon: a shorthand with which people needled themselves and each other. For a long time it was an inside joke among a few hundred people.

The explosion came in 2022–2023, and it has its anchor points. In 2022 the AI Impacts project ran a large survey of authors of publications at leading ML conferences; in 2023 it repeated the survey on an even larger scale. The median respondent in both surveys estimated the chance of an "extremely bad outcome" (something like the extinction of humanity) at roughly 5% — even though a noticeable share named zero and a noticeable share named figures in the tens of percent. The typical ML researcher, it turned out, holds in their head a small but nonzero probability of apocalypse — and calmly goes off to train the next model.

Then a chain reaction set in. March 2023: Yudkowsky writes in TIME that a moratorium won't cut it, that we need to "shut it all down." May 2023: Hinton leaves Google and, in an interview with the NYT, speaks of his alarm. Around the same time the Center for AI Safety rolls out a one-sentence statement — "mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war" — which the heads of the leading labs sign. Against this backdrop the press picked up p(doom) as a ready-made, clickable format: journalists began making the rounds of researchers and collecting their numbers like autographs. The meme came out of the underground and became a headline.

### Criticism / arguments against

**It is poorly defined.** What exactly counts as "doom"? Extinction down to the last human? An irreversible loss of control under which people live but decide nothing? Simply "very bad"? By what deadline — 2050, 2100, "someday"? And what is the probability conditional on — circumstances as they are today, or the assumption that we build superintelligence? Two people name "10%," meaning entirely different events over different horizons, and gladly agree that they "roughly converge."

**False precision.** A single number looks like the result of a computation, though behind it is an intuition squeezed into a decimal fraction. "0.17" sounds more scientific than "I don't know, but it's worrying," even though it carries exactly the same information. Worse: the figure hides a model of the world. Two people with p(doom) = 20% may disagree about everything — the mechanism of the catastrophe, the timeline, what to do — yet the number makes them seem like allies. And the argument about *why* exactly twenty is far more important than the twenty itself.

**Performativity and tribalism.** A number stated publicly is an act of belonging. To name a high p(doom) is to enlist among the doomers, a low one among the accelerationists. Soon the figure is chosen not by analysis but by the desire to end up in the right camp (or to needle the wrong one). A quantity conceived as a measurement turns into a tattoo.

**Incommensurability.** Without a shared definition of the outcome, time horizon, and conditions, averaging these numbers is meaningless — it is like adding a temperature in Celsius to a temperature in Fahrenheit without converting. "The median p(doom) in the industry is X percent" is a statistic exactly as elegant as it is meaningless.

**The anchoring effect.** As soon as the discussion turns to "so how much have you got?", it gets stuck on the axis of "more or less" of a single figure. The important forks — which scenarios exactly, which early signs, which interventions work — get crowded out by haggling over percentages.

And a separate trap on the horizon — **Pascal's mugging**. If doom is harm of astronomical size, then even a vanishingly small probability, multiplied by such a magnitude, demands giving up everything. A logic by which any sacrifice can be justified by any negligible chance of doom — and one readily used to devalue caution ("it's Pascalian blackmail!") or, conversely, to force it through. With tiny probabilities and gigantic stakes, expected utility behaves insanely, and p(doom) regularly ends up right where this math breaks down.

### What to do about it / where it leads

The temptation is to wave off p(doom) as a Twitter vulgarity. The temptation is understandable but, it seems, wrong. The number has a real use, so long as you don't mistake it for an answer.

First, it forces uncertainty to be *spoken aloud*. A person made to name a figure cannot hide behind "we'll live and see": they are obliged to admit that the risk is nonzero, and that already shifts the conversation from "nothing will happen" to "how much exactly." For a subject where denial was the norm for decades, that is no small thing.

Second, it helps *compare priorities*. If you genuinely believe there is even a 5% chance of catastrophe, it is strange to spend a thousand times less on reducing that risk than on, say, ad-click metrics. The number is a crude but working lever for a conversation about the allocation of effort.

Treat p(doom) not as a forecast but as the *beginning* of a conversation. What is useful is not the number but what stands behind it — the model of the world, the timeline, the definition of catastrophe, the presumed mechanism. Ask not "how much" but "how much, under what definition, by what year, and through what exactly." Demand operationalization before comparing. Remember the anchor and don't let the discussion collapse into a tug-of-war over percentages.

And keep in view this beast's neighbors in the bestiary: **existential risk**, out of which p(doom) grew as a household yardstick; the eternal skirmish of **e/acc versus doomers**, where the number serves as a battle cry; the idea of the **warning shot** — an event that could shift everyone's estimates at once, if we lived through it and managed to get wiser; and **Pascal's mugging** as a reminder that the arithmetic of "tiny chance × infinite harm" is not an argument but a warning sign.

p(doom) is a thermometer everyone has tucked under their own coat, proudly displaying its reading, though nobody remembered to agree on a scale. Measuring the common temperature with it is meaningless. But the very fact that people have finally taken out their thermometers is already a symptom that someone has suspected a fever.
