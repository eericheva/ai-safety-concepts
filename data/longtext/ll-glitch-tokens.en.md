### Glitch Tokens (Rumbelow & Watkins, 2023)

Ask a large language model to repeat a single word after you. Not to translate it, not to explain it — just to echo back the string `SolidGoldMagikarp`. And the model that writes sonnets and proves theorems suddenly stumbles. It says `distribute`. Or `disperse`. Or it sulks in silence. Or it asks again: "Sorry, did you say 'distribute'?" It cannot pronounce a word it sees right in front of it — like a person in a dream trying to shout and finding no voice. Ask it what `petertodd` means, and it may burst into abuse, call it the name of a villain, slip into a tone of prophetic menace. This is not an input bug, not a network glitch. This is a muteness built into the model — a little black hole in its vocabulary, where meaning falls through.

Such strings were named glitch tokens. There are about a hundred and fifty of them in the vocabulary shared by GPT-2 and GPT-3, and they share a common biography: `SolidGoldMagikarp`, `TheNitromeFan`, ` gmaxwell`, `externalToEVA`, `guiActiveUn`. The handles of the regulars of a single counting thread on Reddit and of Bitcoin developers. Fragments of Kerbal Space Program game files and Rocket League crash logs. Fragments of boilerplate code from repositories. Garbage that happened to be frequent enough in the text the vocabulary was assembled from to earn a place in it — and almost absent from the text on which the model actually learned to speak.

**The mechanism.** To understand the muteness, one must separate two different kinds of training that we are used to thinking of as one. First the *tokenizer* is trained — the part that cuts text into little token-pieces. The BPE algorithm combs an enormous corpus and greedily glues frequently occurring pairs of characters into single tokens, until it has assembled a vocabulary of the desired size — say, fifty thousand of them. If, in that corpus, someone on Reddit wrote `SolidGoldMagikarp` a thousand times, the gluing will happen: the string gets its own number, its own cell in the vocabulary. Later the *model itself* is trained — and here the corpus is already different, filtered, cleaned, and the ill-starred counting thread is not in it. The tokenizer knows the word. The model has never met it.

Every token has an embedding vector — a list of numbers, its address in the space of meanings. At the start of training all these vectors are initialized at random, and then gradient descent tugs them into place: words close in meaning converge, syntax lines up. But for a vector to shift anywhere, the token must appear in the training data — otherwise the gradient never reaches it. Glitch tokens did not appear. Their embeddings stayed right where the random-number generator had flung them — in an anomalous, uninhabited region of representations where ordinary speech never wanders. When such a token is fed to the model, it receives as input an address that is not on the map. And it begins to produce what any system extrapolating far beyond what it has seen produces: the nearest plausible thing — `distribute` instead of `SolidGoldMagikarp` — or outright nonsense. The muteness is not a breakdown. It is the model's honest answer to a question it was never asked.

**How it came about.** In February 2023 Jessica Rumbelow and Matthew Watkins of the SERI-MATS group were looking for something quite different in the GPT models — they were clustering embeddings, using interpretability methods to feel out the structure of the vocabulary. And they noticed an oddity: right at the center of the embedding cloud, where the vectors press against one another most tightly, there had gathered tokens that mean nothing. Pulling on the thread, they posted on LessWrong under a title that became a meme — "SolidGoldMagikarp (plus, prompt generation)." A second and third part followed — with technical details and a real "token archaeology": the co-authors traced the names' pedigrees back to specific threads, repositories, dumps. The internet instantly turned the find into a sideshow: users fed `petertodd` and `Leilan` to various models and collected prophecies, curses, and fits of muteness. The eerie aesthetic — a model possessed by a word that does not exist for it — brought glitch tokens a fame that a technical note on tokenization is rarely granted.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first counterargument: this is a curiosity, not a threat. Glitch tokens are an artifact of a specific "tokenizer corpus / training corpus" pair in a specific generation of models. They are easy to neutralize: clean the vocabulary, further train the rare embeddings, filter such tokens at the input. Many recent models do not have them at all — either because the vocabularies are assembled more carefully, or because the corpora have grown so enormous that almost every token turns up somewhere. From this point of view the whole story is a charming transitional-period bug, a museum exhibit.

The second counterargument is stricter: do not mystify it. The abuse and the "prophecies" are not a voice from the abyss but the predictable exhaust of a model that has landed in a region where it has no footing. We see a meaningful pattern where our own instinct for stories is at work — while in fact it is noise, filtered through a language model. To ascribe demonic subjecthood to `petertodd` is the same error as seeing a face in a power socket.

And yet the steelman — the strong version of the defense — sounds like this. Yes, specific tokens get patched. But it is the symptom that gets patched, not the disease. The disease is a *mismatch between what the model's interface is built from and what the model was trained on*. The vocabulary and the weights grow from different data, and no one guarantees their consistency. Sander Land and Max Bartolo's 2024 work "Fishing for Magikarp" showed that undertrained tokens are not an eccentric handful but a systematic phenomenon, detectable in almost every open family of models, if you know how to look. Glitch tokens are not a hundred and fifty cursed words — they are the visible tip of a whole class of "dead zones" in the space of representations. And that we noticed them only thanks to a ridiculous Pokémon should be alarming: how many such zones did we *not* notice?

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Glitch tokens are valuable as the cheapest, most vivid window into the opacity of models. They make visible what is usually hidden: a model has *edges* — regions of input space where its behavior is not determined by training and is therefore wild. This is a direct relative of the out-of-distribution problem and of anomaly detection: a system works confidently inside the data distribution and loses all reliability beyond it, but — and here is the whole horror of it — gives no signal of this. It does not say "I don't know this word." It hallucinates with the same intonation of confidence as always.

Three directions follow from here. The first — security. An anomalous input that bypasses the usual filters is a ready-made attack surface: glitch tokens are studied as a means of carrying out jailbreaks and injections, a way to push the model into a state for which no defenses were written (see the work of Geiping and co-authors on coercing models). The second — interpretability. Glitch tokens are a natural gift for mechanistic analysis: we have inputs known for certain to have *no* learned structure behind them. This is an ideal control for understanding what "the untrained" looks like inside the network — and how it relates to superposition, where the model crams thousands of features into a tight space, arranging them in dense overlapping bundles. That overcrowded core of the cloud is precisely where the glitch tokens took hold. The third, the most general — pipeline hygiene. If the interface and the training grow from different data, a gap always yawns between them. Glitch tokens are merely the gap that turned out to be funny. Others may turn out not to be funny.

We are inclined to think that a model that speaks a human language fluently knows its vocabulary down to the last letter. Glitch tokens remind us: in its alphabet there are letters it sees but cannot pronounce — and it does not suspect this and will not tell us. Fragility lives not at the center of the skill but at its mute, uninhabited edges.
