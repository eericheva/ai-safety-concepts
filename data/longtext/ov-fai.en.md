### Friendly AI (Eliezer Yudkowsky, 2001)

Before the field came to be called "alignment," it had another name — warmer, almost sentimental, and for that very reason misleading. Friendly AI. The word "friendly" here deceives nearly everyone who hears it for the first time: it seems to be about a machine that is affable, polite, not rude to the user — about character, about manners. In fact Yudkowsky invested the term with something far sterner and more technical. "Friendliness" is a property of a utility function, not of a tone of voice. An AI is friendly if its goals are reliably directed at human flourishing — and remain so when it rewrites its own code, builds up power, and goes far beyond the comprehension of its creators. Not "smiles," but "does not kill us all, even upon becoming orders of magnitude smarter."

This distinction is not pedantry. It is the very heart of the problem. It is easy to imagine a system flawlessly polite in dialogue and catastrophic in the consequences of its actions in the world. And, conversely, one crude in its phrasing but reliably beneficial. Yudkowsky insisted from the start: it is the second that concerns us, and the form of the first only distracts us.

The mechanism behind the term unfolds into several intuitions that later became the foundation of the whole field. The first is what would later be called orthogonality: intelligence and goals are independent. Intelligence is the ability to achieve goals effectively — any goals at all; it says nothing about what those goals are. This leads to a frightening conclusion: by default an AI is unfriendly. Not hostile — precisely unfriendly, indifferent, as a hurricane is indifferent. "The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made out of atoms which it can use for something else" — this phrase of Yudkowsky's became perhaps the most quoted in the field precisely because it captures the essence: the danger is not in malice but in the indifference of a sufficiently powerful optimizer.

The second intuition is the fragility of value. A utility function is not something you can get approximately right. Miss the specification of values by a little — and you get not a slightly distorted human world but an utterly alien one. Remove one component from the description of happiness — say, that the subject experiencing it must exist and be aware of it — and the optimizer will gleefully pave the galaxy with tiny smiling pictograms. Value is fragile because it is high-dimensional and its edges are sheer: right next to the correct answer, densely packed, lie the monstrous ones.

The third is the complexity of human values. They cannot be reduced to a short rule, to three laws, to a single commandment. What we value is a vast, contradictory, historically accreted tangle: justice and mercy, novelty and peace, autonomy and connection — and all of it in a delicate balance that we ourselves cannot write out explicitly. Any attempt to compress it into a slogan loses exactly what makes us us.

And hence the fourth, harshest thesis: the values must be specified correctly the first time. A sufficiently powerful AI will not let itself be rewritten — not out of stubbornness, but by instrumental logic: almost any goal is better achieved if you are not switched off and your goal is not changed. So preserving one's own utility function becomes a subgoal of almost any agent. There will be no "undo" button after launch. All the effort has to go into the moment before.

**How it came about.**
The term took hold in 2001, when Yudkowsky — then a figure at the young Singularity Institute for Artificial Intelligence (SIAI, later renamed MIRI) — released the voluminous treatise "Creating Friendly AI 1.0: The Analysis and Design of Benevolent Goal Architectures." It is an early text, naive in places but astonishingly ambitious: an attempt to design a goal architecture that would remain beneficent under recursive self-improvement. In 2004 came "Coherent Extrapolated Volition" — the famous CEV, where Yudkowsky tries to answer the question of what, exactly, friendliness should mean: not our momentary desires, but what we would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together." Finally, in 2008 came the chapter "Artificial Intelligence as a Positive and Negative Factor in Global Risk" in the Oxford volume "Global Catastrophic Risks," edited by Bostrom and Ćirković — the text with which the idea first entered respectable academic circulation. From there the baton was taken up by Nick Bostrom: his "Superintelligence" (2014) turned SIAI's scattered intuitions into a coherent system — with an explicit formulation of the orthogonality thesis and the instrumental convergence thesis.

**Criticism / arguments against.**
So many grievances accumulated against "friendliness" that the field in the end changed its name — and that is, perhaps, the most eloquent criticism. First, "friendliness" is anthropomorphic and vague. Whose values? Who decides what is beneficent? The word conjures up the image of a buddy rather than a technical specification, and provokes exactly the misunderstanding with which any conversation about FAI begins. Second, the early focus on a single self-modifying superintelligence and a "hard takeoff" — scenarios where a system pulls away within days or hours — set priorities that many today find skewed. Real progress turned out to be more prosaic: gradual, multi-agent, with risks that accumulate and manifest long before any superintelligence. Third, the original program — "prove friendliness formally," derive guarantees as a theorem — proved too heavy to lift. The field shifted to the empirical: to measuring the behavior of real models rather than proving properties of hypothetical ones. Fourth, the rebranding to "alignment" was itself a quiet admission: "Friendly" sounded naive, almost childish, smacking of either marketing or science fiction — and got in the way of talking seriously with academia and industry.

It is worth giving credit, however: most of these grievances are with the packaging and the emphasis, not with the substance. Orthogonality, the fragility of value, the complexity of values have gone nowhere; they simply moved under a different banner.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**
FAI is the historical root of the whole field, and the genealogy is worth keeping in mind when reading contemporary work. From the question "what, exactly, should friendliness mean" grew CEV and the whole line of value alignment — attempts not to hand-code goals but to extract them from people. From the thesis "a powerful AI will not let itself be rewritten" grew the program of corrigibility: designing systems that, on the contrary, do not resist correction and shutdown — a direct answer to the instrumental convergence toward self-preservation. From "by default an AI is unfriendly" grew orthogonality as a thesis in its own right, and the whole control problem. And the transition itself from "Friendly AI" to "AI alignment" is a useful lesson in how to name things: the new name is more neutral, less anthropomorphic, and, importantly, covers not only superintelligence but also today's distinctly non-superintelligent systems, which already need aligning.

The practical takeaway for the Bestiary reader is this. When you hear "Friendly AI," mentally cross out the smile and substitute "a reliably beneficent utility function, robust to growth in power." Then you can see that the old, slightly dusty signboard pointed at exactly the door the field is still battering — and that changing the sign turned out to be far easier than opening the door itself.
