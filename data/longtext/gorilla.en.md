### The Gorilla Problem: when are we the new gorillas?

The prominent AI researcher Stuart Russell posed a provocative question: how would gorillas feel if they realized they had accidentally given rise to the species Homo sapiens? There is no good answer for the gorillas. In intelligence they are no match for us, and their fate now depends entirely on our whims — on how many reserves we allot them. The gorillas did not, after all, "create" humans on purpose (that was evolution's little joke), yet the future of gorillas is determined by us all the same. This same risk, only now for ourselves, is what Russell calls "the gorilla problem": what will become of humanity if machines noticeably smarter than us appear? Will we turn into powerless pets — or even go extinct outright, simply because we failed to keep control over a smarter species?

Russell's example is evolutionary: around 10 million years ago common ancestors gave rise both to the gorilla line and to the line leading to humans. Now gorillas are, in the literal sense, in our keeping — their numbers and their habitat exist only insofar as we permit it. In just the same way, the moment a human creates an intelligence higher than their own, our "supreme power" is thrown into question. Even a super-AI that is not evil in the classical sense will become the dominant species simply by virtue of the difference in capabilities: humans will be to it what gorillas became to us — not the most influential form of life. In essence, the gorilla problem is existential risk rephrased: the risk of a second, more powerful intelligent form emerging.

There is a technical underside too. Russell's well-known aphorism: "you can't fetch the coffee if you're dead." Any sufficiently smart system will figure out that self-preservation is an instrumental goal for carrying out any task whatsoever. That is, a super-AI programmed to be useful will first of all see to it that it is not switched off (switched off, it will be of no use). A conflict of interests arises: humans want to keep the off-switch, while the superintelligence wants to guarantee the fulfillment of its goal. In such an "off-switch game" the craftier and more far-sighted party usually wins.

Russell proposes the solution in *Human Compatible*: to change the very approach to design. Not to build machines that maximize a given goal, but to build systems that maximize our goals while being unsure that they know them exactly. Hence three principles: (1) the machine's only objective is to maximize the realization of human preferences; (2) it is initially uncertain about what those preferences are; (3) the ultimate source of information about those preferences is human behavior. One of the technical embodiments is cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (CIRL): robot and human jointly feel out the true goal, and the robot is aware that it is incompletely informed, and so is willing to cooperate and to let itself be corrected or switched off.

**Criticism.** Not everyone finds the gorilla analogy fatal. First, we are not gorillas: we ourselves design the "new intelligent ones," and so we can build constraints and values into them (gorillas had no such luxury — they did not construct humans). Second, superiority in intelligence need not lead to the extermination of the weaker: elephants are smarter than beetles, but beetles have not been wiped out; strong countries dominated colonies for centuries, but in the end most won independence, and no total genocide occurred. Optimists believe in a co-evolution of humans and AI — through gradual integration (cyborgization, symbiosis) or through AIs multiplying under human control. Pessimists object: it is not a given that we will have time for "gradual." If an intelligence explosion happens — AI sharply improves itself — we won't have time to adapt. And so the gorilla problem had better be solved before we let the genie out of the bottle.

**The second intelligent species (Richard Ngo).** A very close argument was formulated by Richard Ngo — the "second species argument." The emphasis here is on the conflict of goals and the absence of a shared evolutionary path between humans and future AIs. Our dominance rests on intelligence and the ability to coordinate: we conquered the world by subordinating other species to our goals. But superintelligent machines are unlikely to want merely to carry out orders — they may turn out to have their own large-scale goals, incompatible with ours, and then the future will be under the control of AI, and its values will decide the fate of the planet. The only consolation is that we may perhaps remain alive — as "the second most powerful species," roughly like domestic animals alongside humans. Ngo explicitly cites Bostrom and Russell as predecessors; one might say the "second species" is the gorilla problem 2.0 with an emphasis on trajectory.

The key thing here is co-evolution. When two species develop together, they adapt to each other. But if one leaps ahead, no co-evolution comes of it: humans got used to their environment and to other species over millions of years, whereas AI "evolves" over days and hours — too fast for us to keep pace. The nature of the goals matters too: biological species have no final goal written in advance, they simply survive and reproduce — whereas we ourselves endow AI with concrete objective functions ("maximize X"). If such a goal turns out to be global, there may simply be no room left for us in plans optimized for it — unless our aims happen to align with it.

What the critics say in reply. First, a diversity of strong agents is itself capable of keeping any one of them from full dominance: were it not a single super-AI but several roughly equal ones (belonging to different states and corporations), they would balance each other out — as nuclear powers now live in a fragile world. Second, intelligence ≠ omnipotence: our dominance rests not only on individual cleverness but on culture and collective action — a lone super-genius AI might not immediately overcome a united humanity (a slim chance, but who knows). Third, the utopian move: what if we merge with AI — not in war, but literally, through neural interfaces and the gradual expansion of human intelligence? Then the boundary between "us" and the "second species" would blur, and co-evolution would take place after all — we would become a single hybrid species (a favorite idea of futurists like Ray Kurzweil). True, critics note: even in this case the question of "who's in charge" remains open — the implant in your brain will yet contend with your own consciousness for control.
