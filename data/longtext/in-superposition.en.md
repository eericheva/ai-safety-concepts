### Superposition (Anthropic, Elhage et al., 2022)

Imagine a suitcase built for ten shirts. You pack twenty into it — rolled, squashed, sleeve tucked into sleeve. The lid closes. Formally there was room for ten, but in fact twenty went along, only now you cannot pull out any one item without disturbing three neighbors. This is exactly what a neural network does when there are more features it wants to distinguish than neurons at its disposal. It stuffs them in anyway. And it pays for this by having each neuron cease to mean any one thing.

This very observation — that a network encodes more features than it has dimensions in a layer — is called **superposition**. It seems almost arithmetically impossible: how, in a space of `n` directions, can you distinguish far more than `n` things? But the impossibility is illusory, and it resolves beautifully.

**The discovery.** In the paper "Toy Models of Superposition" the Anthropic team took deliberately tiny models — autoencoders with a handful of hidden neurons — and fed them synthetic features whose two parameters could be controlled precisely: importance and sparsity. When the features were dense — active in nearly every example — the model behaved decently: a few of the most important features got their own orthogonal directions, and the rest were simply not represented at all. But the moment the features were made rare — such that in a typical example only a small fraction of them is active — the model changed strategy. It began to pack several features into the same space, assigning them directions that are almost, but not quite, perpendicular to one another. In the paper's pictures this looked literally like geometric figures: features seated themselves at the vertices of polygons, triangles, tetrahedra — anything to stay as far from one another as the cramped space allows.

**The mechanism.** Why does this work at all? The trick is in the geometry of high dimensions. In a plane you can draw only a couple of mutually near-orthogonal directions. But in a space of dimension `n` the number of directions whose pairwise angles are close to a right angle grows not linearly but exponentially. The formal support here is the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma: a set of points can be projected into a space of astonishingly small dimension almost without distorting the distances between them. The network exploits this margin. It agrees that the feature directions will be not strictly perpendicular but only approximately so — and in exchange gains the ability to store many times more of them.

The price is interference. Since the directions are not perfectly orthogonal, each feature "leaks" a little into the others: reading one, you get a ghostly admixture of the rest. This is noise, and it is harmful. Sparsity saves the day. If features are active rarely and almost never simultaneously, then in a given example one or two are lit and the rest are silent — and the pickup from the silent ones is near zero. The model in effect makes a bet: yes, if two features stuffed into the same place flare up at once, there will be a mess, but this is such a rare case that the risk can be neglected for the sake of the gain in capacity. Training solves an optimization problem: squeeze in as many important features as possible until the accumulated interference starts to cost more than the gain from cramming more in. The sparser the world, the more aggressive the superposition.

**Significance for safety.** A direct consequence of superposition is the polysemanticity of neurons. Since there are more features than neurons, an individual neuron simply cannot be assigned exactly one meaning: it is forced to respond to several unrelated things at once. The classic example from earlier observations of vision networks is a neuron that lights up for cat faces, and for car fronts, and for cat legs. There is no common idea behind this set; three different features simply share one physical direction, and the rest of the network tells them apart by context.

For interpretability this is bad news, and here is why. The tempting program of "reading the neurons" — going through the hidden units, labeling each ("this one is about Paris, this one about the syntax of negation") and thus understanding the model from the inside — runs into a wall. We've simply picked the wrong unit of analysis. The neuron doesn't carry meaning on its own; meaning lives in the directions of activation space, of which there are more than neurons and which do not coincide with the neuron basis. To try to understand the model by looking at neurons is like reading a text whose lines are jumbled and overlaid: the letters are in place, but you cannot assemble the words. If we want to audit a model for dangerous capabilities or hidden goals, we need to be able to name what it represents at all. Superposition says: naively, it will not work.

**How it came about.** Superposition did not fall from the sky in 2022. It was preceded by a long line of work on "circuits" in vision networks — the program of Chris Olah and colleagues, back in Distill and at OpenAI, where the polysemanticity of neurons was noticed and recognized as the chief thorn: it prevented decomposing the network into intelligible parts. The question stood thus: is polysemanticity an annoying artifact of training that can be cleaned out, or an inevitable consequence of something deeper? "Toy Models" gave the answer: a consequence. Through toy models, where everything is under control, the authors showed that polysemanticity is not dirt on the glass but a direct manifestation of superposition, which the network chooses rationally when features are sparse. This reformulated the problem: not "how to get rid of polysemanticity," but "how to untangle superposition."

**Criticism and arguments against.** The picture is elegant, but it has vulnerable seams, and it's only honest to name them.

First, "Toy Models" are precisely toy models: synthetic features, hand-set sparsity, autoencoders instead of transformers. Carrying the conclusions over to real language models is a plausible extrapolation, not a proven theorem. Real features are not obliged to be as conveniently sparse and independent as in the sandbox.

Second, the whole construction rests on the linear representation hypothesis — the assumption that features correspond to directions and that their combinations add up linearly. The hypothesis is strong and not universal. The work of Engels and co-authors, "Not All Language Model Features Are Linear" (2024), showed that some features are by nature multidimensional and are not described by a linear direction — for example, cyclic structures like the days of the week live on a ring, not on a line. So "a direction per feature" is not always the correct model, and superposition of directions does not describe the whole menagerie.

Third, the dispute over the unit of analysis is not closed. The very notion of a "feature" remains semi-intuitive, and it is not certain that untangling yields a canonical, uniquely correct dictionary: the decomposition depends on the method and its hyperparameters, and different runs find different sets of "features." There are works that directly cast doubt on whether current methods isolate objective atoms of meaning rather than fragments convenient for a particular algorithm.

**What to do about it.** If meaning lives in near-orthogonal directions and not in neurons, then one must look for directions. From this grew the main practical bet of recent years: sparse autoencoders (SAEs) as a tool for dictionary learning. The idea is straightforward. We train, on top of a model's activations, an auxiliary network that decomposes each activation vector into a sparse sum over a large dictionary of learned directions — "features." The dictionary is deliberately made overcomplete: it has far more elements than there are neurons — precisely so as to bring back into explicit form those very stuffed-in features the network held in superposition. Sparsity in SAE training (a penalty on the number of simultaneously active elements) plays the role of the same assumption on which superposition stands: in each example only a few should be lit.

This program has brought notable results. Cunningham and co-authors (2023) showed that SAEs find far more interpretable units in language models than the neurons themselves. Anthropic, in "Towards Monosemanticity" (Bricken et al., 2023), decomposed a small transformer into thousands of monosemantic features, and then in "Scaling Monosemanticity" (Templeton et al., 2024) scaled the approach up to the production model Claude 3 Sonnet — and extracted millions of features, including abstract and safety-related ones, which could moreover be used for controllable intervention in behavior.

But here too one should keep a cool head. The SAE is not the final answer but the best tool available today, and it has its own maladies: dead features that never activate; feature splitting, when one meaningful concept fragments into a dozen near-duplicates as the dictionary is enlarged; sensitivity of the result to training settings. Untangling the suitcase turned out to be possible — but making sure you laid the items out exactly the way they were packed, and not just conveniently for yourself, is still hard. Superposition explained why neurons stay silent about their meaning. Getting to reliable model-reading is still a work in progress.
