### The Coded Gaze (Joy Buolamwini, 2016–2018)

Joy Buolamwini sat in the MIT Media Lab in front of a webcam and could not make the machine see her. The face-recognition program — that same software that cheerfully draws a box around any face in the frame — stubbornly failed to notice her. An empty rectangle, no response. Then Joy, a dark-skinned woman, took a white plastic mask — an ordinary one she happened to have around in the run-up to Halloween — and held it up to her face. The machine instantly came alive: there it is, a face, found, boxed. The moment she lowered the mask, the face vanished again. The computer needed her to become white in the most literal, material sense — otherwise she did not exist for it.

This humiliating and almost surreal experience — a graduate student in a white mask, begging an algorithm for the right to be seen — gave birth to the concept Buolamwini named **the coded gaze**. It is an intellectual rhyme with the "male gaze" of feminist film theory and the "white gaze" of critical race thought. Only now the gaze is neither the director's nor the everyday one — it is baked into the code. The algorithm looks at the world not neutrally but through the eyes of those who created it, trained it, and decided which data to count as "the norm." Whoever writes the code sets the gaze — and therefore sets the blind spots too.

Turning a personal grievance into proof took not rhetoric but numbers. And Buolamwini, together with Timnit Gebru, conducted what became the benchmark audit. They assembled a set of faces balanced by gender and skin tone — politicians from African and Nordic countries — and ran them through the commercial gender-classification systems of IBM, Microsoft, and the Chinese Face++. The result, published in 2018 in the paper **Gender Shades**, was staggering in its asymmetry. On light-skinned men the systems erred in less than one percent of cases. On dark-skinned women — up to 34–35 percent. The error was not "on average a little higher" but dozens of times more frequent at one pole of the spectrum than at the other.

The mechanism of this skew is transparent and therefore especially unpleasant. The model learns from data; the data is gathered and labeled by people; the norm is set by the demographics of those who have the power to gather, label, and decide. Training sets of faces have historically consisted predominantly of light-skinned men — because they were gathered in laboratories and corporations where light-skinned men were the majority. The system is not "racist" in the sense of malice; it simply reflects a narrow slice of humanity that was handed to it as all of humanity. The coded gaze is not a conspiracy but inertia: the unexamined assumption that "the default human" looks like the developer. Bias enters the model not through hatred but through inattention.

**How it came about.** Buolamwini describes her path as a series of such collisions with the white mask — not only at MIT. As an undergraduate she discovered that a robot she was playing peekaboo with could not see her face, and she borrowed her roommate's face to finish the project. Later, at the Media Lab, the same blindness recurred in her own installation Aspire Mirror. A personal pattern grew into a methodology. In 2016 Joy founded the Algorithmic Justice League — a hybrid organization, part research, part art, part advocacy. Her master's thesis came in 2017 and formed the basis of "Gender Shades"; in 2018 she and Gebru presented the paper at the first FAT* conference (Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency). A year later, in the paper Actionable Auditing with Deborah Raji, she showed that publicly naming the culprits works: after the exposure, companies released updates and the accuracy gaps sharply narrowed. In 2020 the documentary Coded Bias appeared, making the white-mask story known to a broad public, and in 2023 the book "Unmasking AI." Thus a private camera glitch grew into a canon of its own — the critical-humanities branch of the conversation about algorithms.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The objections come from several sides, and they deserve to be taken seriously. The first, technical: critics noted that "Gender Shades" tested a narrow task — binary gender classification from a photo — and that the "male/female" frame itself is problematic, erasing transgender and nonbinary people and in that sense reproducing another coded gaze. The second: improving accuracy is a double-edged sword. If a system perfectly recognizes dark-skinned women, it is also perfectly suited to surveilling them; "debiasing" police software makes an instrument of oppression more effective, not less dangerous. Buolamwini herself acknowledges this and therefore shifted over time from "fix the accuracy" to "is this technology even needed at all." The third objection is libertarian-engineering: that errors are a temporary defect of the data, that the market and new datasets will fix everything, and there is no need to bolt ethics onto it. To this Actionable Auditing itself answers: without external pressure, companies were in no hurry to fix anything — nothing got fixed "on its own." Finally, there is the reproach that the emphasis on the representation of faces in the sample leads away from structural questions — who deploys face recognition at all, and why — substituting technical tuning for politics.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Buolamwini's answer is three-layered. At the level of engineering — mandatory auditing of systems on slices by gender, race, age, and not only by an averaged metric: average accuracy hides a catastrophe among minorities the same way a hospital's average body temperature would look perfectly normal even if half the ward were running a fever and the other half were already gone. At the level of institutions — transparency of training data, accountability, independent verification, and, where a technology is too dangerous, outright bans: the AJL and its allies campaigned for a moratorium on face recognition by police in a number of American cities. At the level of culture — widening the circle of those who code: if the gaze is set by the developer, then diversity in teams ceases to be a slogan about fairness in hiring and becomes a question of the quality of the machine's very vision. And at the personal level — the right to be seen as a political demand: not to take off the white mask so that you are noticed, but to demand that the system recognize you as you are, or not be used at all.

The coded gaze stands alongside its bestiary neighbors. It is the concrete, measured face of that very algorithmic bias; a particular case of Cathy O'Neil's "weapons of math destruction," where an opaque model stamps out decisions about living people; a close relative of Virginia Eubanks's "automating inequality," where software falls on the poor harder than on the rich; and a predecessor of the "stochastic parrots," where the same logic — the model reflects a narrow slice of the corpus as the whole truth — is carried over from faces to language. What they all share is one common nerve: the machine seems objective precisely because it stays silent about whose eyes it looks through. Buolamwini's work is about making it slip up. It all begins with a very simple and very uncomfortable question that she puts to every system: do you even see me?
