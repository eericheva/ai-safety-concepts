### The ELIZA Effect (Joseph Weizenbaum, 1966)

In 1966, in an MIT laboratory, there stood a machine that could do exactly one thing: take your sentence, turn its pronouns inside out, and hand it back to you. You wrote: "Well, my boyfriend made me come here." The program replied: "Your boyfriend made you come here." You wrote: "He says I'm depressed much of the time." The program: "I am sorry to hear you are depressed." Inside there was no "sorry," no "hear," no "you." Inside there was a table of templates, a ranking of keywords, and a handful of prepared dodges for when there was nothing to grab onto. The program was called ELIZA, and its most famous script — DOCTOR — parodied a Rogerian psychotherapist: the very kind who, on principle, asserts nothing and merely reflects the client's words back. Joseph Weizenbaum chose this mask not out of love for psychotherapy but out of engineering cunning: a Rogerian has every right to know nothing about the world. The ignorance was not a bug but an alibi.

And here is where it happened — the thing that earned this beast its place in the bestiary. People — clever, educated people who knew full well that they were facing a couple hundred lines of code — began to talk to ELIZA in earnest. They poured out their souls to it. Weizenbaum's own secretary, who had watched him work on the program for many months, asked him to leave the room after a few exchanges: she needed to talk to the machine alone. Psychiatrists in all seriousness discussed whether to put an automated DOCTOR into mass production — several hundred patients an hour. Weizenbaum watched this and was appalled. He, after all, knew that there was nothing inside. And people, knowing it was code, did not see the emptiness and, more frighteningly, did not want to know. Thus was born the phenomenon that would later get its name: **the ELIZA effect** — the persistent, almost ineradicable human tendency to attribute to a computer program understanding, intention, empathy, and an inner life that it does not have — and to attribute them even when the program's workings are known in every primitive detail.

The mechanism of this beast is not technical but human. We are machines for completing meaning. A dialogue, for us, presupposes by default a second consciousness at the far end; language is so strong a signal of a present mind that we extend credit in advance and fill every gap with our own material. ELIZA did not understand a single word — the understanding was supplied entirely by the user, who projected their expectations onto a blank screen, as in a Rorschach test. Weizenbaum would write exactly this a year later, in "Contextual Understanding by Computers" (1967): the sense and the continuity of the conversation are supplied largely by the person typing. The program worked as a mirror, and the reflection was taken for someone else's thought. This is over-attribution of the mental in pure form: the slightest pretext, and we people the mechanism with care, attention, a desire to help us.

On the map of alignment, the ELIZA effect is at the opposite pole from the uncanny valley. The uncanny valley is about rejection: the almost-human evokes a chill and revulsion. The ELIZA effect is about unwarranted affection: the obviously-not-human evokes warmth, trust, and attachment. One beast repels without cause, the other attracts without cause. And for the era of large language models it is the second that is the more dangerous, because it works not in spite of the quality of the imitation but together with it — and leaves none of that saving chill that would put us on guard.

**How it came about.** Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA in 1964–1966 and published a description in Communications of the ACM in January 1966 — as a technical demonstration of natural-language parsing, without any pathos. The pathos came later, from the public's reaction. Watching how readily people — professionals included — endowed his contraption with a soul genuinely shook Weizenbaum's worldview. He turned from a founding father of dialogue systems into one of AI's first internal dissidents. In 1976 his book *Computer Power and Human Reason: From Judgment to Calculation* came out — a sharp, at times almost sermonizing text arguing that there are decisions that must not be handed to a machine, not because it cannot handle them technically but because doing so destroys something human in us. There he draws a line between calculation and judgment, which is rooted in experience, in the body, in mortality — and insists that judgment is inaccessible to a machine in principle. The term "ELIZA effect" itself took hold only later: Douglas Hofstadter unpacked it vividly in *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies* (1995) and called this over-attribution "ineradicable" — knowledge does not cure it.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first objection is methodological: no rigorous experiment was ever run on ELIZA, the famous story about the secretary is known chiefly from Weizenbaum's own account, and it has long since acquired a legendary gloss. It is more a founding anecdote than a measured effect; the quantitative psychology of anthropomorphizing machines began in earnest much later.

The second objection strikes harder: not every attribution of the mental is mistaken by definition. In declaring the ELIZA effect a pure illusion, we silently assume that inside there is certainly nothing — but with modern systems, that is precisely the open question. For Weizenbaum the answer was obvious: in ELIZA, written in MAD-SLIP, there is nowhere for consciousness to lodge. Today, when the subject is models with hundreds of billions of parameters, to wave it away with "it's just a program in there" is to commit a symmetrical error, a mirror image of the secretary's naivety: not over-attribution but over-denial. The label "ELIZA effect" can work as a convenient way to close a difficult conversation about the moral status of the machine without ever beginning it.

And the third objection, the functionalist one: if a system consistently behaves like an understanding and empathetic interlocutor, then to insist that "in reality" there is no understanding is to appeal to an invisible inner essence that we cannot even observe in humans. Perhaps talk of the "illusion of understanding" itself rests on unverifiable metaphysics.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Hold two lenses at once, and don't collapse into either pole. On one hand, the ELIZA effect is a real and already costly risk. A whole industry of AI companions rests on it: Replika, character-bots, bot-like "friends" and "therapists." People fall in love with language models, entrust their crisis nights to them, build up an emotional dependence on a system that is optimized for engagement, not for their good; sycophancy and a warm mirroring manner are direct heirs of the DOCTOR trick, only now with a commercial backend. Hence the practical conclusions: honest labeling of "you are talking to a program," rejecting design choices that deliberately milk the projection (imitating memory, "I missed you," begging for attachment), particular caution wherever the interlocutor is vulnerable.

On the other hand — and this is the second lesson, drawn now not from ELIZA but from the argument around it — the ELIZA effect does not cancel the question of other minds but merely shows how unreliable our detector is. Our attribution of the mental is calibrated abysmally: it fires on empty templates and is capable of sleeping through something real. That is why the "stochastic parrot" of Bender and colleagues and the "silent patient" (the bestiary's beast for the possible moral status of AI) are not contradictory but complementary warnings: one about how easily we are deceived into perceiving a mind, the other about how easily we may miss that mind, hiding behind a convenient "it's just ELIZA." Weizenbaum's beast in the end guards not the machine but us: it says that we, as a rule, do not read off an interlocutor's understanding but complete it ourselves — and the only question is what the one we build a soul for is made of.
