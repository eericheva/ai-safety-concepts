### The Natural Abstraction Hypothesis (John Wentworth, 2021)

Take a glass of water. Inside it are on the order of a trillion trillion molecules, each with its own position and velocity; a full description of this system would not fit on all the hard drives of Earth. But to predict whether you will scald your lips or not, you need exactly one number — the temperature. Almost the whole microscopic truth about the glass simply never reaches you. It is damped, averaged out, drowned in mutual collisions — and what survives at the output, "at a distance," is a pitiful handful of summary quantities: temperature, pressure, volume. The rest is erased.

John Wentworth noticed that this is not an accident of thermodynamics but, perhaps, the arrangement of the world in general. And he built on this observation one of the most optimistic hypotheses in alignment theory.

**The natural abstraction hypothesis** is a thesis of three interlocking parts.

The first: **abstractions exist.** Information about the distant parts of a system is almost all erased on the way to the observer; the little that "survives" to reach you through the noisy medium reduces to low-dimensional summaries. Wentworth formalizes this through the notion of "information at a distance" and through Markov blankets: if I know the macrostate (the temperature of a gas, the shape of a tree, a person's intention), then the microdetails behind that blanket add almost nothing more to my predictions about what will happen far away. An abstraction is exactly that spare summary through which the distant communicates with the distant.

The second: these abstractions are **natural.** They are not the observer's caprice, not a convenient human convention. A "tree" is not something we invented to chatter more easily; it is a real clot of correlated information, objectively picked out by the structure of the territory. The world itself hints at which seams to cut it along. And — the key point Wentworth calls human-compatibility — these summaries turn out to be exactly the concepts humans use in day-to-day thought and language.

The third, the most audacious: **convergence.** Since abstractions are dictated by the world and not by the arrangement of the mind, a broad class of cognizing systems — humans, crows, octopuses, neural networks — will converge on the same abstractions. Not because they are similarly arranged inside, but because they all live in the same world and are forced to track the same quantities that survive at a distance.

Why does this blow up alignment? Because the chief nightmare of value alignment sounds like this: we want to hand the AI the concepts "human," "suffering," "promise," "consent" — but what if it cuts reality along utterly alien seams? What if in its ontology there is no "human," but some nonhuman construct to which our values do not attach? Then any attempt to "explain" values to it is a translation into a language that does not exist. The natural abstraction hypothesis answers: fear not, the vocabulary is shared. If NAH is true, a sufficiently powerful AI will arrive at the same concepts "tree" and "human" as you do — and then human values can be **hooked onto already-shared abstractions**, rather than translated into an alien universe. This lays a foundation for interpretability (inside the network there are concepts recognizable to us, which can be found) and for ELK — eliciting latent knowledge (the model has an internal representation "the diamond is in place" that coincides with ours). And it softens the horror of the infinite space of minds: yes, minds can be wild and alien, but the world in which they live is one, and it imposes on them a common grid.

**How it came about.** Wentworth arrived at this not from machine learning but from an adjacent angle — through control theory, statistical physics, and the old cybernetic idea that a good regulator of a system is forced to build a model of it. In 2020 he posted on LessWrong a sequence about abstraction ("Public Static: What is Abstraction?" and onward), where abstraction was defined informationally: as whatever survives as you move farther away. In 2021 he shaped this into a research program with the post "Testing The Natural Abstraction Hypothesis: Project Intro" — and the key word here is *Testing*. Wentworth insisted from the start: this is not a philosophical manifesto but an **empirical hypothesis**, which can and must be tested — sought in trained networks, proven with theorems about Markov blankets, run through simulations. Not "I believe the world works this way," but "let's measure whether it does."

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and honest one: the hypothesis is **not proven.** There are particular theorems, there are hints from interpretability, but there is no general proof; it is an open program, and it may well fail. The second, and far more painful for alignment: even if natural abstractions exist, **values may not be among them.** "Temperature" and "tree" survive at a distance because physics stands behind them. But "justice," "dignity," "the ought"? Perhaps these are not seams of the territory but precisely projections of a particular mind — human, evolutionarily specific. Then the AI will converge with us on the low, physical abstractions and **diverge exactly where it matters most to us** — on the moral ones. Worse: partial convergence can create the illusion of understanding. The model shares "human" and "pain" with us, we relax — and the divergence is hidden on the upper floor, where values live, and will surface under optimization pressure, when it is too late to repair. There is also a technical critique: in 2023 Lawrence Chan, Leon Lang, and Erik Jenner took apart NAH's key claims and theorems and showed that much rests on strong assumptions and that there is still a long way to go from a beautiful intuition to a working theorem. Finally, "a broad class of systems will converge" may itself turn out narrower than one would like: the training procedure, the architecture, the data distribution — all of these can push a model toward *its own* abstractions rather than the world's.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The value of NAH is not that it reassures but that it **turns a worldview dispute into a research question**. "Does the AI think in alien categories?" is not a matter for belief but a hypothesis with testable consequences. If it is even partly true, then interpretability should find recognizable human concepts in networks (and increasingly does), and ELK becomes not a search for a nonexistent translation but the extraction of an already-present shared variable. The practical conclusion is twofold. On one hand, hope: perhaps alignment does not require heroically cramming an alien ontology into place — it is enough to point carefully at what the model already represents. On the other, a warning: the commonality of the vocabulary must be treated as a claim **to be proven separately for each concrete level of abstraction**, especially the value level, and not postulated wholesale. And keep in mind the scenario of an ontological crisis: even if today the abstractions coincide, a more powerful model may re-describe the world at a more fundamental level — and our values, tied to the old concepts, will hang in the void. The soberest way to live with NAH is to take it as a working bet that brightly illuminates where exactly to dig, but not for a minute to mistake a beautiful hypothesis for a guarantee already in hand.
