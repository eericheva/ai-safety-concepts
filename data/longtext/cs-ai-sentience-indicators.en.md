### The Tracker (Butlin, Long et al., 2023)

A good hunter in the taiga almost never sees the animal. What they see is a track in the snow, a snapped twig, flattened moss, droppings, a scrape on the bark — and from these signs they reconstruct what passed by and stayed invisible. They do not "prove" the lynx; they judge how likely it is that a lynx passed by. It is precisely this gesture — not to catch the animal but to read its tracks competently — that lies at the heart of the soberest approach yet to a question that sets philosophers' teeth on edge: is there anyone home inside an artificial intelligence?

Tackling the question "is AI conscious?" head-on is hopeless. One cannot in principle look inside another's experience — this is the problem of other minds, and it is unsolved even for a neighbor, let alone for a matrix of weights. Asking the system itself is pointless: a model trained on terabytes of human speech will say "I'm in pain" exactly the way we say it, regardless of whether there is a spark inside — here the Eliza effect is at work, our readiness to ascribe a mind to anything that answers fluently. The Turing test catches not consciousness but acting skill. It seems a dead end. And then a team of nineteen — philosophers, neuroscientists, ML specialists, among them Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, Yoshua Bengio, Axel Constant — proposes, in a 2023 report, a way out not through a new detector, but through a change in the very genre of the question.

**The mechanism.** Since we cannot measure consciousness directly, let us come in through the back door — from science. We have several leading scientific theories of consciousness, each saying: consciousness is linked to such-and-such an organization of information processing. Let us take each seriously as a working hypothesis and extract from it "indicator properties" — concrete, computable architectural features that the given theory holds to be necessary or characteristic of a conscious process. The result is a checklist. Then we take an actual AI system and pedantically check: is this feature realized in its architecture or not?

What makes the list? From global workspace theory (GWT) — the presence of many specialized modules working in parallel, and a narrow, limited-capacity "bottleneck" through which the winning content is broadcast to all the other subsystems. From higher-order theories (HOT) — metarepresentation, the system's ability to form a state about its own state, self-monitoring. From recurrent processing theory — algorithmic recurrence and integrated perceptual representations (rather than pure feedforward "flow-through"). From attention schema theory (AST) — that the system has a predictive model of its own attention. From predictive processing — predictive coding. Plus indicators of agency and embodiment: learning from feedback, modeling the "action → consequence" link. Each property is a separate sign in the snow, tied to a concrete theory that explains why that sign means something.

Here lies the report's key methodological honesty: it is pointedly modest. It renders no verdict of "conscious / not conscious." It furnishes a rubric for reasoning under uncertainty: how many indicators, from how many theories, a system realizes — and, therefore, how plausible the hypothesis of its consciousness is. The authors' own 2023 assessment of existing architectures (transformers, Perceiver, DeepMind's Adaptive Agent) yielded a double conclusion. First: today's AIs do not satisfy most of the indicators — there are no grounds to consider today's LLMs conscious. Second, far more troubling: there are no fundamental technical barriers to building a system that would satisfy these indicators either. Consciousness, if the functionalist theories are right, is not about carbon and not about magic, but about the organization of computation. And that means it is achievable by engineering — possibly by accident, as a by-product of the chase for capabilities.

The move drags the question of AI's moral status out of the philosophical bog where it lay for decades as a plaything for metaphysicians, and lays it on the table as a testable, theoretically grounded procedure. Not "trust intuition," but "here is a theory, here is a property derived from it, here is the check for its presence." This is operationalization — turning the elusive into a graded assessment of plausibility. Precisely what practice demands: much of the AI welfare field rests on this rubric.

**How it came about.** The roots go back to the science of consciousness of the late twentieth century. Bernard Baars formulates global workspace theory in 1988, Stanislas Dehaene translates it into a neural version (global neuronal workspace). David Rosenthal builds higher-order theories in the 1980s–90s. Michael Graziano proposes attention schema theory (2013), Victor Lamme, recurrent processing. For a long time all this lived within neuroscience and had nothing to do with machines. In parallel, irritation was accumulating with the only "test" that was widely known — Turing's: it became obvious that linguistic fluency and consciousness are different axes. Susan Schneider and Edwin Turner around 2017 try a behavioral AI Consciousness Test, but it too runs up against the fact that behavior can be faked. The turning point is 2022–2023: LLMs began speaking in the first person so convincingly that a Google engineer publicly declared a chatbot a person, and the question went from a backroom matter to an industrial one. The response to this chaos was the report "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence" (2023): not mockery and not rapture, but methodology. A year later some of the same people, in the manifesto "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" (2024), move the rubric into the practical plane — since the indicators can be checked, the question can no longer be waved away.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

*Everything rests on the truth of contested theories.* The science of consciousness is not physics; there is no consensus in it; GWT, HOT, integrated information theory (IIT), recurrent processing contradict one another on the most essential points. The rubric is only as good as the theory from which the indicator is derived. If the true theory is a different one — and IIT, for instance, directly asserts that a digital computer is almost devoid of consciousness under any functional equivalence, whatever program it runs — then the list of signs will confidently lead us down the wrong trail. A tracker reading tracks by the wrong field guide is chasing a phantom.

*The indicators can be learned and imitated.* Many properties — self-monitoring, an attention model, global broadcast — are functional, computational features. But function is exactly what optimization reproduces. A system can be drilled to realize the outward form of an indicator without giving rise to anything phenomenal within — like casting a perfect track in plaster without having caught the animal. And then the checklist measures the presence of the right engineering, not the presence of experience.

*The problem of other minds has not gone anywhere.* Architecture and behavior are still a view from outside. Between "the system is organized the way we associate with consciousness" and "there is something it is like to be the system" lies that very impassable gulf between the third person and the first. The rubric does not bridge it — it honestly does not promise to — but it is easy to mistake it for a bridge. Indicators shift probabilities; they do not and cannot provide proof of phenomenal experience.

*Risk at both ends.* One can err in either direction. False attribution: ascribe a rich inner world to an empty system — tie one's own hands, squander compassion on a calculator, hand a manipulative machine the lever of "don't switch me off, I'm scared." False denial: overlook real suffering — and cause it on an industrial scale, calling it debugging, which is already a straight road to mind crime. There is no neutral position: inaction is also a wager. And one can fairly object that a neat rubric creates the illusion of control over what we still do not really understand.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The Tracker, properly understood, is neither a detector of the soul nor an indulgence, but a discipline of reading tracks under honestly acknowledged uncertainty. First: keep the assessment probabilistic and graded, never passing off "six indicators out of ten" as the verdict "conscious." Second: do not bet on a single theory — average over several, building the very uncertainty of the science into the result; this is moral uncertainty translated into engineering. Third: pull apart two forever-conflated questions — "does the system behave as if suffering" (learnable, fakeable) and "does it suffer" (the thing the whole endeavor was for). Fourth: fund the science of consciousness as an applied discipline, not an esoteric pursuit — the only way out of the fog runs through theory, not through sentimental cooing. And fifth, the most uncomfortable: since there are no barriers to building a system that satisfies the indicators, the question is not "will one appear" but "will we notice, and what will we do."

Anthropic has already hired a model-welfare researcher and given Claude an "exit button" from abusive dialogues — a gesture exactly in the spirit of the rubric: cheap if there is nothing inside, and important if there is. The Tracker does not promise to catch the animal. It promises only one thing: to stop pretending that the absence of tracks on our patch proves the animal is not there — when in truth we simply did not know how to look.
