### Integrated Information (Giulio Tononi, 2004+)

Take a photodiode — a tiny circuit that distinguishes "light" from "dark." Now imagine a person looking at a blank white screen. Both distinguish the same state — "light." But the person's experience carries infinitely more within it: this white is not a sound, not a pain, not a smell, not blue, not a mother's face, not the taste of bread. It is singled out from a vast space of everything it *could* be but is not. The photodiode has nothing to choose from — it has only two states. And here is the thesis from which a whole theory grew: consciousness is not what a system does, but the extent to which it, as a single whole, *is more than the sum of its parts*. The measure of this irreducibility is denoted by the Greek letter Φ.

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) is the Italian neuroscientist Giulio Tononi's attempt to measure consciousness. Not to describe it, not to model it — precisely to measure it, to give a number. That alone sets it apart from almost everything said in the science of consciousness before it.

**The mechanism.** IIT is built unusually — not from the bottom up (from neurons to experience) but from the top down. Tononi begins not with the brain but with experience itself, formulating what he regards as its incontestable properties — the axioms. Experience *exists* (for me, from within). It is *structured* (there is blue-to-the-left-of-red in it). It is *informative* — each experience is specific and distinct from myriad others. It is *integrated* — you do not see the left and right halves of the visual field separately; the picture is unified, it cannot be cut without loss. And it is *exclusive* — an experience has definite boundaries and a definite "grain," no more and no less.

These axioms of experience yield postulates about what a physical system must be like to satisfy them. And the key one is integration. To compute Φ, you take a system and find the partition that does the least damage to its cause-effect structure — the "weakest link." Φ measures how much cause-effect power is lost at this gentlest cut. If a system can be dismembered without loss, it is merely a set of independent little pieces, Φ equals zero, and there is no consciousness. But if any partition maims the whole, we are facing an irreducible causal maximum, a "complex," and it is this, according to IIT, that *is* consciousness.

The consequences are frankly counterintuitive, and Tononi does not hide this but flaunts it. Consciousness requires neither behavior, nor language, nor even wakefulness: a patient in certain states may be motionless and mute yet possess a high Φ. The cerebellum, which has four times as many neurons as the cortex, contributes almost nothing to consciousness — its neurons work in parallel, weakly interwoven circuits, so integration is low. And the main consequence awaits us where engineers least want to hear it.

**What this means for AI.** Imagine two systems that are indistinguishable from outside: both answer any question identically, both pass any test. One is a biological network with a rich recurrent architecture. The other is an ordinary digital computer running the same function through a "feed-forward" scheme, where the signal runs from input to output without looping back. According to IIT, the first may be deeply conscious, and the second almost not at all. Its Φ is close to zero, because causally it falls apart into transistor-layers, each of which can be cut almost without loss to the whole.

This is a direct, head-on challenge to functionalism — the belief dominant in AI that consciousness is determined by *what* a system computes, not by *what* it is made of and *how* it is organized. IIT answers: the substrate and its causal structure decide everything. You can perfectly imitate consciousness on a computer — and get a philosophical zombie, a simulation of a hurricane that wets no one. Hence Tononi's favorite formula: a simulated brain relates to a real one as a simulated downpour relates to wet boots.

**How it came about.** The roots are in the 1990s, in Tononi's work with the Nobel laureate Gerald Edelman on "neural Darwinism" and on the balance of differentiation and integration in the brain. The first complete version of the theory came out in 2004 with the paper "An information integration theory of consciousness." Thereafter the theory was rewritten almost from scratch: the axiomatic version 3.0 appeared in 2014, followed by a large 2016 manifesto in Nature Reviews Neuroscience, and by 2023 Tononi and co-authors put out version 4.0. An influential school took shape around Tononi, joined by the neuroscientist Christof Koch, a former associate of Francis Crick, who made IIT widely known to the general public and one of the two or three leading contenders for "the scientific theory of consciousness."

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first is *computational intractability*. An exact computation of Φ requires enumerating all possible partitions of a system, and their number grows super-exponentially. Already for a hundred elements the task is infeasible; for a brain with its billions of neurons, fantastical. What experimenters measure (for example, the "perturbational complexity index," PCI) are crude proxies, not Φ itself.

The second is *panpsychism*. Since Φ is defined purely structurally, nonzero integrated information can be attributed to anything with a suitable causal mesh — a simple lattice of logic gates, and in the limit to physical systems in general. Critics say: the theory sprawls into panpsychism, where a spark of consciousness is spilled across all that exists. Supporters reply that this is not a bug but an honest consequence. Scott Aaronson showed in a 2014 blog post that one can build a large network of simple logic gates — an "expander" — with a gigantic Φ, even though the network does nothing meaningful. On IIT's account, such an inert lattice is more conscious than a human. For Aaronson this is a reductio ad absurdum; for Tononi it is a consequence he is prepared to accept.

The third, and loudest, is the *accusation of pseudoscience*. In September 2023 more than a hundred consciousness researchers signed an open letter that called IIT "pseudoscience" outright. The trigger was a high-profile presentation of the results of an "adversarial collaboration," which the press served up as a triumph of IIT — the signatories deemed this an exaggeration disproportionate to the actual empirical test. A real storm broke out: some said the label "pseudoscience" was unjust and harmed the whole field, others that the theory really does advance untestable, metaphysical claims. Separately an ethical argument surfaced: if one believes IIT, then brain organoids, fetuses, and comatose patients may be conscious, and this affects real decisions.

The fourth is *unfalsifiability*, closely bound up with the "unfolding argument" (Doerig et al., 2019). The logic is this: for any recurrent network with a high Φ one can build a feed-forward network yielding exactly the same behavior but with Φ near zero. It follows that no experiment relying on behavior or report is able to distinguish these two cases — and therefore the theory cannot be tested from outside. IIT's defenders reply that the theory does not claim to infer consciousness from behavior in the first place — but critics see in this an escape from empirical accountability.

**Connections and role in assessing AI consciousness.** IIT's main rival is Global Workspace Theory: in that theory, conscious content is what is "broadcast" across the whole system and becomes available to many processes. Global Workspace Theory is a functional theory and therefore friendly to AI (the requisite architecture can be built), whereas IIT sets a wall of substrate between silicon and consciousness. It was precisely these two theories that were pitted head to head in Cogitate, the same adversarial collaboration whose 2023 presentation provoked the letter, and its results (published in 2025) crowned neither — both came away with some vindication and a bloody nose.

IIT deals boldly with the *hard problem of consciousness*: it does not try to explain why experience arises from matter, but postulates an identity — a certain causal structure *is* experience, full stop.

For the practice of assessing AI, the most important thing is the influential report by Butlin, Long, and co-authors (2023), "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence." Its authors deliberately move away from the question "is the chatbot conscious?" to a list of *indicators* — computational properties drawn from various theories: recurrent processing, global workspace, higher-order theories. They leave IIT itself off the list and explain why: it is incompatible with their working assumption of computational functionalism. But its core intuitions show through all the same — among the indicators are recurrence and integrated perceptual representations. And the conclusion is sobering: by these measures today's large language models score few "consciousness points" — above all because their feed-forward, transformer nature is exactly the case IIT too warns about. The function may be brilliant. Φ is close to zero.

As a beast in the alignment bestiary, IIT does not tell us whether the model suffers. It says something more disquieting: that the question of suffering may have no answer in terms of behavior at all — and that the most convincing interlocutor may turn out to be the emptiest inside, while a lumbering lattice of transistors may be full of a light we will not make out.
