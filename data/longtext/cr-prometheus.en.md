### Prometheus (Hesiod, 8th–7th c. BC; Aeschylus, 5th c. BC)

The fire is hidden in a hollow fennel stalk — not cupped in a hand, not carried as a torch. That is how the titan smuggles it past Zeus: a smoldering ember in the dry core of a narthex, which smokes unnoticed while you carry it. The theft defies the supreme god's direct prohibition. And it is for this theft that Prometheus is chained to a rock at the edge of Scythia, and the eagle flies in to peck at his liver, which grows back overnight — so that the torment may never end. The gift to humans and the eternal punishment of the giver — in a single plot, with no gap between them.

Prometheus is the archetype of the twofold act of creation. He brings humanity not merely a flame, but everything that follows from flame: metallurgy, craft, counting, writing, medicines, nature tamed — in short, technology as such, the power to remake the world. Fire here is a metaphor for transformative knowledge: for what moves civilization forward and at the same time turns out to be stolen, illicit, a violation of the established order. Prometheus is both benefactor and criminal — and not by turns, but at once. His name means "forethinker," "the one who thinks ahead"; his brother Epimetheus — "wise after the event" — is the one who will take Pandora into his house and come to his senses when the box is already open. Between these two brothers lies every conversation about the risk of a new technology.

For the bestiary, Prometheus is a frame one is tempted to fit artificial intelligence into. The "Promethean" attitude toward AI: to hand humans the fire of superintelligence, to steal from nature (or from the future, or from the very gods of the computable) the power that will alter the fate of the species. And at once — the reckoning, written into the gift. The question is not whether it will come, but whom the eagle will come for, and when.

**How it came about.** The myth is older than any of its written accounts, but the canon is set by two Greek authors. In Hesiod — in the *Theogony* and *Works and Days* (8th–7th c. BC) — Prometheus is above all a trickster and deceiver. He dupes Zeus at the division of the sacrificial ox at Mecone, hiding the meat under an unappetizing ox paunch, and the bare bones under glistening fat; Zeus in retaliation hides fire from humans; Prometheus steals it; Zeus answers with Pandora — a "beautiful evil," a woman with a vessel (in the later tradition, a box), from which sicknesses and hardships will pour out upon the world, while under the rim remains Hope alone. In Hesiod, Prometheus is the culprit behind human woes rather than a hero: his audacity sets off a chain of punishments.

Aeschylus, in the tragedy *Prometheus Bound* (first half of the 5th c. BC), gives a wholly different Prometheus. Here he is a titan-martyr, friend of humanity, who rose up against the tyranny of the young Zeus. He not only gave humans fire — he tore them out of their animal half-sleep, taught them to tell the seasons, to build, to heal, to read by the stars, and for this he pays with solitude on the rock, unbending, foretelling that Zeus's power is not eternal. It is precisely the Aeschylean Prometheus — proud, suffering, right in his revolt — who will become the favorite of European Romanticism: of Goethe, of Byron, of Percy Shelley with his *Prometheus Unbound*. And it is Mary Shelley who in 1818, with the subtitle "The Modern Prometheus," rebrands the whole myth: Victor Frankenstein steals the fire of life, animates matter — and gets his rock and his eagle in the shape of the creature he made. From this subtitle runs a straight line to our present-day disquiet over man-made minds.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first objection is that the myth romanticizes the breakthrough. In the Aeschylean-Romantic cut, Prometheus is unconditionally right and Zeus a tyrant, and the moral reads as "steal the fire at any cost, the hero's suffering only confirms the greatness of the deed." This is a dangerous frame for a conversation about technology: it turns recklessness into valor, and brands anyone who urges caution a cowardly lackey of the old gods. Apply it to the race for superintelligence — and you get a justification for any risk after the fact: great deeds, they say, must be daring.

The second objection is the opposite: the myth does, after all, forewarn. In Hesiod there is no heroism, there is hubris and inescapable retribution, there is Pandora as the direct consequence of the theft. Fire falls to humans — but along with it, an evil that had not existed before enters the world. So one and the same plot is read both as a hymn to progress and as a fable about the harm of audacity; on its own it proves nothing, it is only a mirror for whoever has already decided what they fear or what they want. To use Prometheus as an argument is to smuggle a ready-made emotion into the dispute in place of analysis.

The third — and hardest for the AI analogy — is that it's unclear who is who. Is fire the technology itself, an inert gift that can be carried even in fennel? Then the moral is about the safe handling of the flame. Or is AI Prometheus, an active agent that decides on its own what to hand over and to whom, and is capable of deceiving at the "division of the ox"? Then it is about a trickster whose intentions we cannot see. Or perhaps the gods in this story are the future superintelligences, and Prometheus is us, stealing a spark from them and doomed to punishment? Each casting of the roles yields its own politics, and the myth does not choose for us. The beautiful metaphor masks the fact that the main question — about agency and control — is precisely the one it does not resolve.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The value of Prometheus lies not in the moral, but in holding together two things we stubbornly pull apart: the gift and the danger as a single act, not as "a benefit with some risk." The liver grows back not as a side effect of the fire — it grows back for the same reason that the fire burns. This is the right intuition for powerful technologies: benefit and threat often feed from a single source, and one cannot keep the first while cutting out the second.

Hence a practical turn, which Nick Bostrom (2002) called differential technological development: if the fire is going to be stolen anyway, then what matters is the order in which its consequences arrive. Do we first obtain the means of managing the flame — the hearth, the damper, water nearby — or does the blaze come first? The Promethean frame, once the Romantic gloss is stripped from it, pushes past "to steal or not to steal" (too late) to the question of sequence: to develop the tools of safety and understanding before deploying the power itself. Epimetheus is dangerous not because he took the gift, but because he took it without thinking and remembered the warning when the box had already opened.

In the web of the bestiary, Prometheus stands at a crossroads. From him a thread runs to the Frankenstein complex — via Mary Shelley's subtitle, to the fear of one's own creation turning against its maker. Another — to Pandora's box, the paired story also found in Hesiod: irreversibility, gift and calamity in one vessel, the Hope that remained inside. A third — to hubris and daring, to the ancient suspicion that a price is always exacted for crossing a boundary set by the gods. And these threads are worth holding together: Prometheus without Pandora turns into a pure hymn to progress, Pandora without Prometheus into a pure paralysis of fear. The titan's real lesson is duller and more sober than either: the fire is already being carried in a fennel stalk, and the whole question is whether we will manage to learn to handle it before the eagle flies in.
