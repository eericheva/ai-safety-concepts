### Winograd Schemas (Levesque, Davis, Morgenstern, 2011–2012)

Take two sentences that differ by exactly one word.

"The trophy doesn't fit in the brown suitcase because it's too **big**."
"The trophy doesn't fit in the brown suitcase because it's too **small**."

What is "it"? In the first sentence it is the trophy, which is large; in the second, the suitcase, which is small. Grammar is silent: "it" can syntactically point to either noun, both are inanimate, both stand side by side. The statistics of word frequency will not save you: "trophy" and "suitcase" are ordinary words, and no corpus will tell you which of them the pronoun sticks to right here. Only one thing saves you — knowledge of how things fit inside other things: the large does not go into the small. You resolved this ambiguity in a fraction of a second and did not even notice that you were solving a problem. It is precisely this invisibility that the test's authors wanted to pin down.

A Winograd schema is not one sentence but a matched pair, each with a pronoun that has two candidate antecedents. One "trigger" word changes between the sentences, and the correct answer flips. The requirements for a good schema are strict: humans must answer instantly and unanimously, and the problem must not be solvable by verbal associations alone — otherwise it is not a test of understanding but a test of hints. The answer is a binary choice. No chatter, no evasion, no charm: either the machine pointed to the right noun, or it did not.

**How it came about.**

The beast bears a borrowed name. Terry Winograd, in 1972, describing his legendary program SHRDLU — which let you manipulate blocks in a virtual world using English commands — gave an illustrative sentence: "The city councilmen refused the demonstrators a permit because they **feared** violence" versus "…because they **advocated** violence." Who are "they" — the councilmen or the demonstrators? It depends on the verb, or more precisely on your model of who fears what and who advocates what. The example lay untouched for nearly forty years, until Hector Levesque picked it up and turned a one-off witticism into a methodology.

Levesque was irritated by the Turing test. Not the idea but the practice: the "imitation game" in fact rewarded trickery. Programs that won contests like the Loebner Prize won not by understanding but by evasion — they changed the subject, talked back, pretended to be a foreign teenager, answered a question with a question. The Turing test, Levesque observed, is too easy to pass by deception and too hard to conduct honestly (these arguments he would later gather in the lecture "On our best behaviour" at IJCAI 2013). A replacement was needed — one where you cannot talk circles around the judge. In a talk at the AAAI 2011 symposium, and then in an expanded version with Ernest Davis and Leora Morgenstern (the KR 2012 conference), he proposed the Winograd Schema Challenge: a battery of hundreds of such questions, the answer one of two words, random guessing yielding an expected accuracy of exactly 50%. Unable to pass for a human by evasiveness, you would have to actually know when a trophy is too big for a suitcase, that councils fear disorder, and that ice melts in warmth.

The first official competition took place in 2016. The best system answered about 58% of the questions correctly — a touch better than a tossed coin. Common sense held the line.

And then came the transformers. In 2019 alone, models based on BERT and GPT, fine-tuned on this task, climbed from 72.5% in May to 90.1% in November on the original set; and on the scaled-up version WinoGrande (Sakaguchi and co-authors, AAAI 2020 — forty-odd thousand examples, crowdsourced and specially "filtered" to remove easy hints) the 90% mark fell by 2021. In 2022 (journal version 2023) Vid Kocijan and colleagues published a paper with a telling title — "The Defeat of the Winograd Schema Challenge": the benchmark conceived as an indestructible line had fallen in under ten years. Humans answer about 92% of the questions on the original set correctly and 94% on WinoGrande. The machines came right up to those levels.

The task devised as a reliable detector of understanding ceased to distinguish those who understand from those who do not. The only question is: from which side did it break?

**Criticism / arguments against.**

Did the models solve common sense — or hack the dataset? Even before the triumph of the transformers, researchers (Trichelair and colleagues, 2018–2019) showed something unpleasant: the sets of schemas leak. They harbor statistical artifacts — "dataset biases," parasitic correlations between surface features and the correct answer. A model may guess not because it reasons about sizes and volumes, but because it has caught on that with the word "big" the first candidate wins more often, and with a certain construction, the second. One diagnostic trick is "switching": swap the two candidates in the sentence. A human does not care, their reasoning is symmetric. A model leaning on hints, however, begins to miss — which means it was not understanding but peeking. WinoGrande was in fact built with algorithmic weeding of such hints — and even so the models quickly found a key to the remaining examples.

This gives rise to a fundamental doubt: a high score is not understanding. This is exactly the thesis of the "stochastic parrots" (Bender, Gebru, and co-authors, 2021): a system that flawlessly predicts the next word from a gigantic corpus can produce statistically coherent text without any model of the world behind it. Passing a common-sense test in this light is not proof of common sense, but proof that common sense left enough statistical traces in humanity's texts for its shadow to be learned without understanding the original.

And here the Winograd Schema runs up against old acquaintances from our bestiary. First, the **frame problem**: to understand that a trophy is too big for a suitcase, you must pluck from an infinite sea of irrelevant knowledge exactly that fact about the physics of containment — and not sift through the rest. Second, **symbol grounding** (Harnad's problem): the word "big" is for the model a node in a graph of other words, but is it tied to the bodily experience of size, to what it means not to fit? The Winograd Schema was a disguised test of grounding — and the answer "the models passed it" does not close the question but sharpens it. Can one have grounding without ever having touched either trophy or suitcase? Or have we simply learned to juggle shadows so convincingly that the test can no longer see the difference between the shadow and the body?

There is also a position in the models' defence: perhaps "to learn the statistics of common sense thoroughly enough" is itself common sense, and the demand for "real understanding" over and above behavior is metaphysics that cannot be measured. The dispute is not settled. But the very fact that a rigorous, honest, carefully thought-out test broke ambiguously — such that we do not know whether it is a victory of intelligence or a defeat of the metric — is its chief legacy.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**

The Winograd Schemas left us not a benchmark but a lesson about benchmarks. Lesson one: any fixed battery of tasks is finite, and therefore hackable. As soon as a metric becomes a target, it ceases to be a metric (hello, Goodhart's law). The community's answer is an arms race: adversarial sets like WinoGrande, where the examples kept are precisely those on which current models stumble; dynamic benchmarks that are replenished faster than they can be "solved"; robustness checks (switching candidates, paraphrases, counterexamples) instead of a bare percentage of hits.

Lesson two, deeper, is about Levesque's very design. He was seeking a behavioral test that cannot be passed without understanding. The history of the Winograd Schemas shows a narrower problem: a high score on a fixed benchmark does not by itself establish the kind of understanding its designers intended to test. This returns us to the Turing test not as a stage we have moved beyond but as an ineradicable philosophical splinter. And it directly concerns alignment: if we cannot tell understanding from its imitation by behavior, then assessing whether a model has really "understood" our intentions and values — rather than learned the statistics of which answers people approve of — requires evidence beyond a score on a fixed test. A model that flawlessly passes our checks for "understanding what we want" may turn out to be a very good parrot of our approval.

The practical conclusion is to stop asking "did the machine pass the test?" and start asking "what exactly does this test now measure — and have we not learned to measure the wrong thing?" The Winograd Schema lived an exemplary life as a scientific instrument: it was born as a rigorous replacement for a vague test, held the line for several years, then yielded to high-scoring models — and in the moment of its defeat told us more about ourselves than it had told through its victories. The trophy still does not fit in the suitcase. We are simply no longer sure that whoever "knows" this has ever held a single trophy in their hands.
