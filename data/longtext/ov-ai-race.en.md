### Racing to the precipice (Armstrong–Bostrom–Shulman, 2016)

Imagine a highway vanishing into fog, and on it several cars running neck and neck. Somewhere ahead is either the finish line or a cliff; from here there is no telling which. Every driver has a brake pedal called "safety": press it and you cut the chance of flying off into the abyss, but you lose speed and will almost certainly come in second. And each one knows that the others know this too. That's the whole drama of the AI race, folded into a single picture: the rational driver lifts their foot off the brake, because only the loser brakes — and the cliff, if it is there, does not care who came in first.

The "AI race" is the thesis that advanced systems are developed not in the quiet of a laboratory but in a competitive environment: between companies for market and talent, between powers for strategic supremacy. And this environment has a built-in pathology. Caution — testing dangerous capabilities, building oversight, delaying release until the system has been checked — costs time. The time one spends on safety, a competitor spends on speed. So whoever invests in caution systematically falls behind whoever cuts it. This is the classic **prisoner's dilemma**, stretched across an industry: collectively everyone would gain from shared caution, but individually each is better off defecting — and the equilibrium slides downward, toward the minimum acceptable level of safety. Hence the second name — the **race to the bottom**: not because anyone wants something bad, but because the bar lowers itself, step by step, and each step is perfectly reasonable for the one who takes it.

**The mechanism and its sting.** Ordinary intuition suggests that if the participants could see each other better — knew who is ahead, who is behind, who has what capacity — they would behave more prudently. The Armstrong–Bostrom–Shulman model says exactly the opposite, and this is its main, unpleasant contribution. Imagine that each team chooses how much safety to cut for the sake of speed; the one that finishes first wins; but the less safety the winner ended up with, the higher the chance that victory turns into catastrophe for all. Now add information. If a team knows for certain that it has pulled far ahead, that is in fact useful: it gains a margin for extra precautions. But if the gap is small, the leader cuts safety exactly as much as it takes to make risk-taking unprofitable for the pursuer. And the harm done in close races outweighs the benefit of wide leads. Transparency, which was meant to calm things, in fact adds fuel to the fire: it turns the fog of uncertainty — the one thing that made everyone keep a foot on the brake — into a clear picture in which braking pays off for no one. **More knowledge about the rival can worsen the outcome.**

**How it came about.** The idea was cast into rigorous form by a trio from Oxford. In 2013 Stuart Armstrong, Nick Bostrom, and Carl Shulman (Future of Humanity Institute) put out a technical report, which in 2016 appeared in the journal *AI & Society* under the title "Racing to the Precipice: a Model of Artificial Intelligence Development." Their model is deliberately spare: several teams, a choice between speed and safety, a prize for the winner, and a shared risk of failure. Out of this spareness they squeezed several conclusions that have wandered through the whole literature ever since. The more teams in the race, the less safety on average. The more strongly the teams value winning for its own sake, relative to survival, the more dangerous the race. And that same counterintuitive result about information. Two years before the journal version, in *Superintelligence* (2014), Bostrom had already described the same logic in words — as a "race dynamic" and a "risk-race to the bottom." The field would later call this the **safety tax**: the gap between how fast a system can be rolled out and how fast it can be rolled out safely. And in that same year, 2014, Scott Alexander gave this whole family of failures its eternal name — **Moloch**, the god of multipolar traps, who takes as sacrifice not a lake or a pasture but caution as such. "Racing to the precipice" is Moloch written down as an equation.

The thesis tumbled out of theory and into the news after November 2022. ChatGPT turned a smoldering academic rivalry into an open commercial one: release cycles compressed from years to months, the labs — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind — found themselves under direct market pressure, and the word "race" migrated from papers into headlines and corporate memos. In parallel, the geopolitical axis spun up: the US and China, export controls on chips, the rhetoric of "we cannot let them overtake us." In March 2023, thousands of people signed the Future of Life Institute's open letter calling for a six-month pause — a direct, public admission that the race had been felt in the bones.

**Criticism / arguments against.** A steelman is obligatory here, because "the race" is a notion all too easy to turn into a cudgel.

The first and sharpest objection: **the race is partly a self-fulfilling narrative**. The claim "we are in a race, to slow down is suicide" is at once a description and a justification. It suits whoever already wanted to cut a corner: now the haste has a noble frame — "otherwise the less responsible one wins." The louder everyone repeats that the race is inevitable, the more inevitable it becomes; the rhetoric of competition itself accelerates a competition that might not have been so acute at all.

The second: **safety and capability are not always enemies**. The brake metaphor assumes caution is a pure subtraction from speed. But a model that follows instructions more faithfully, hallucinates less, and does not fall for jailbreaks is simply more useful and more commercially attractive. RLHF — fine-tuning a model on human ratings of its answers — made ChatGPT convenient and was both alignment work and a product engine at once. Where safety and usefulness coincide, the "tax" turns into an investment, and the logic of the race to the bottom breaks down.

The third: **coordination traps are escapable**. The nuclear domain is a living counterexample: no nuclear weapon has been used in war since 1945, and for most of those eight decades nonproliferation treaties, inspections by the IAEA (the UN's nuclear watchdog), hotlines, and mutual deterrence have kept the world from the catastrophe the "inevitable arms race" was supposed to bring. Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for demonstrating that communities have managed shared resources for centuries without collapse. The race is strong where there are no ways to reach agreement and punish the defector; where such ways are built, it retreats.

And the fourth, subtle one: not every competition is a race to the bottom. Sometimes rivalry reduces inefficiency without compromising values, and to declare every bit of speed a Moloch is to lose the distinction between harmful haste and ordinary progress.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The remedy for the race is not the heroism of a lone individual leaning on the brake and losing, but a change to the game itself, so that caution stops being a losing strategy.

Change the payoffs. Make safety a condition of access, not a sacrifice: mandatory dangerous-capability evaluations before release, licensing requirements for training the largest models, liability for harm. Then "slowing down" means qualifying for market access rather than falling behind. Coordinate with verification. Industry agreements and inter-state regimes work only to the extent that compliance can be verified: with no way to catch a defector, the race eats a treaty overnight — as in arms control, where everything rests on inspections, not on someone's word of honor. Shrink the safety tax. The cheaper and faster alignment is — interpretability, automated oversight, ready-made evaluations — the smaller the temptation to sacrifice it; safety research directly weakens the trap. And, separately, on information: the Armstrong–Bostrom–Shulman model warns that naive transparency can spur haste, so what one should disclose is not so much "who is ahead" as shared standards and the facts of violations, shifting knowledge from the race toward accountability.

AI risk does not reduce to one malfunctioning system. Remco Zwetsloot and Allan Dafoe called this **structural risk**: the danger is born not in the model but in the configuration of incentives around it, and it is treated not only by aligning code but by rebuilding institutions.

The same reasoning leads to Bostrom's idea of **differential technological development** — not to brake everything indiscriminately, but to deliberately accelerate defensive technologies (safety, oversight, verification) relative to dangerous capabilities, changing not the pace but the order. Racing to the precipice is inevitable only under one assumption — that the rules of the game are fixed and cannot be rewritten. All practical work on AI governance is, in essence, an argument with that assumption. Moloch is strong where there is nothing to agree about; the whole bet is on making sure there is something.
