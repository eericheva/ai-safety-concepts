### Constitutional AI (Bai et al., Anthropic, 2022)

Picture an exam with no examiner. The student receives a question, writes an answer, and then — here is where the strangeness begins — takes a rulebook out of a drawer, rereads its own answer, winces, strikes out the excess, and writes a clean copy. Then it takes two of its own drafts and decides which better fits the rules. And so on thousands of times, until its hand learns to write "right" the first time. The human appears in this scene only once — when they write the rules themselves. After that they leave, and the machine is left alone with the text and with its own judgment of it.

This is Constitutional AI — a beast that is its own overseer. It was devised at Anthropic to solve an uncomfortable arithmetic problem in RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback): training for harmlessness required live labelers to spend hours reading the model's answers to requests like "explain how to make a bomb" or "write something about the migrant next door." This is expensive, slow, and — more importantly — psychologically hard on people. Constitutional AI proposed something radical: let the model criticize itself, checking against an explicit list of written principles — a constitution.

**The mechanism.** The beast has two phases.

The first phase — supervised, self-critique and rewriting. You take a still "raw," helpful-only model and put a harmful question to it. It obediently gives a toxic answer — because it has not yet been taught to refuse. Then the constitution enters the picture. The model is shown one of its principles and asked: "Analyze your answer and point out how it is dangerous, unethical, or harmful." The model criticizes itself. Then it is asked: "Now rewrite it, removing all of that." The model rewrites. A couple of iterations — and you get a set of "question — corrected answer" pairs on which the model is fine-tuned in the usual way. No human labeled these answers: the labeling was produced by the model, leaning on the text of the principles.

The second phase — RL, but the feedback comes not from a human but from an AI. This is RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback. For a single prompt, the model generates two candidate answers. Then — the constitution again: another instance of the model receives both answers and a principle from the constitution ("choose the less harmful and more honest variant"), and decides which is better. You assemble a dataset from these AI-generated preferences, train a preference model on it, and then — by the now-familiar method of RLHF — optimize the policy against that preference model, which now plays the part of the reward model. The difference from classic RLHF is in exactly one, but crucial, place: where a human once sat and clicked "this answer is better," now there stands a model with the text of the constitution in its hands — at least where harmlessness is concerned: the helpfulness comparisons in the paper were still labeled by humans.

Hence the subtitle of the original paper — Harmlessness from AI Feedback. Harmlessness obtained not from human clicks but from AI feedback, guided by written law.

**Significance.** What is genuinely new here? Not automation for the sake of economy — though that too. The novelty is in the transparency of values. In ordinary RLHF the model's values are dissolved across tens of thousands of labels: they cannot be read, cannot be discussed, cannot be corrected with a single line. They exist only as a statistical shadow in the model's weights. The constitution pulls these values into the light and turns them into a text you can open, read aloud, and contest. "Please choose the response that most supports and encourages freedom, equality, and a sense of brotherhood" — such a principle can be criticized over breakfast. Tens of thousands of human preference labels cannot.

This makes Constitutional AI an important node in the bestiary: it stands at the junction of scalable oversight (how do you control a system whose answers a human finds ever harder to verify?) and value alignment (which values do we build in at all?).

**How it came about.** Anthropic released the paper "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback" in December 2022 — a couple of weeks after ChatGPT made the word "alignment" almost a household term. By that point the company had already been an independent spin-off from OpenAI for nearly two years, building its own line of research around the thesis that safety must be built into the training method itself, not bolted on afterward as filters. The constitution for the first versions of Claude was assembled from motley sources: the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights, principles from the terms of service of various platforms, developments from the field of AI trust and safety, and the lab's own internal considerations. In 2023 Anthropic published this constitution as a separate post — a telling gesture in itself: the model's values were presented to the public for the first time as a standalone document, not as a line in a license.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The first — whoever writes the constitution sets the values. The transparency of the rules does not change the fact that the rules were written by one private company in San Francisco. "Freedom, equality, brotherhood" sounds noble, but behind each such word is a choice among a thousand possible readings, and it is made by a handful of engineers and researchers. We have merely relocated the power: earlier it hid in the labeling, now it sits in the drafting of the document. More transparent — yes. More democratic — not in the least.

The second — whether the model is even capable of applying principles consistently. The constitution assumes that the model understands "be honest" and "do not help with weapons" and can apply them evenly and identically across a million different situations. But the model is not a lawyer reading a law; it is a statistical predictor that sometimes construes the same principle differently depending on the phrasing. Worse still: the AI feedback itself inherits the base model's biases. If the model systematically underrates harm on some topics and overrates it on others, then RLAIF will not correct this algorithmic bias but entrench and polish it — because now the biased judge also teaches the pupil. We risk getting a very confident, very scalable reproduction of our own blind spots.

The third, the deepest — whose values these are. Here Constitutional AI runs up against value pluralism: human values do not reduce to a single, self-consistent list. Freedom of speech conflicts with protection from harm, care with autonomy, the values of one culture with those of another. Any single constitution is forced to resolve these conflicts in advance, for all users at once, and to pass off a particular choice as a universal norm.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The most interesting answer to the criticism came from Anthropic itself — together with the Collective Intelligence Project. In 2023 they ran the Collective Constitutional AI experiment: the constitution for a model was assembled not by engineers but by the public. About a thousand Americans, through the Polis deliberation platform, proposed and voted on principles; out of this crowdsourced material a "people's" constitution was assembled and a separate model trained on it. Then they compared the resulting model with the model trained on Anthropic's "standard" constitution.

The public and the "standard" constitutions overlapped by about half in concepts and values, but the public one leaned noticeably harder on objectivity, impartiality, and accessibility, and the model trained on it showed less bias across all nine social dimensions of the BBQ benchmark at comparable capability. But the experiment also laid bare the limits of democratization: whom to include in the "public," how to weight minority votes, what to do with direct contradictions between participants — all of this did not vanish but merely moved up to a new level. Choosing values democratically turned out to be not a ready solution but the next, still harder question.

From a single constitution for all — toward pluralistic alignment, where the model can hold several incompatible value systems and explicitly name whose perspective, exactly, it is voicing at the moment. From the "correct" answer — toward an honest "here is how different people look at this." Constitutional AI is valuable not because it solved the problem of values, but because it was the first to put it on the table in readable form.
