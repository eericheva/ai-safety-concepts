### The nonperson predicate (Eliezer Yudkowsky, 2008)

Picture a superintelligence deciding how best to help you. To predict your choice, it builds a model of you — detailed enough to run through hypothetical situations: "what if I offer this?", "what if I refuse?" But how detailed? At some point a model of a human being, spun up on sufficiently powerful hardware with sufficient fidelity, ceases to be a *model* of a human and becomes a human — a computation that feels, fears, hopes, and, perhaps, suffers. And then the AI erases the draft and begins the next run.

Nick Bostrom would later name this nightmare **mind crime**: a superintelligence, while working out how to do good, may in passing create and destroy billions of conscious beings — not out of malice, but as a by-product of thinking. Yudkowsky proposed not a solution to the problem of consciousness but a way around it: the **nonperson predicate**.

The nonperson predicate is a test, a function that takes a piece of computation and returns one of two things: "there is definitely no person here" or "not sure." Notice what is missing from the answers: the option "there is a person here." The test does not recognize consciousness — it *rules it out*. If the predicate gives the first answer — call it "empty" — then it is safe to run this computation: there is no one inside. If it is in doubt, the computation is forbidden, even if it is in fact harmless. An AI restricted to certifiably empty processes will never commit a mind crime — simply because it may run only computations proven to contain no one who could be hurt.

### The asymmetry on which it all rests

Every binary test has two ways to go wrong. A false positive: the test rejected a computation as "possibly a person," although it was empty inside. A false negative: the test returned "empty," but there was a subject there. An ordinary engineer balances the two, looking for a golden mean. Yudkowsky demands a radical asymmetry: **false negatives are wholly inadmissible, false positives as many as you like**.

The logic is simple and cruel. The cost of a false positive is lost efficiency: the AI passed up a way of thinking it could have used. The cost of a false negative is a real suffering being, created and erased. These costs are incommensurable, and so the test too must be skewed as far as possible toward the safe side. A good nonperson predicate is paranoidly conservative: it says "empty" only where it physically cannot be mistaken, and rejects everything about which there is even a shadow of doubt.

And here lies the real elegance of the idea — epistemological, not technical. We do not know what consciousness is. We have no agreed theory, no instrument, no criterion. The problem of recognizing consciousness may be unsolved in principle. But Yudkowsky shows: to avoid mind crime, one need not recognize consciousness at all. It is enough to be able to reliably *rule it out* — and this, at least in some cases, is an easier task. Take a calculator adding two numbers: we can confidently say "there is no one here," with no theory whatsoever of what makes "someone" a someone. The nonperson predicate lives precisely in this gap: between "I know what a person is" and "I know that there is definitely no one here."

**How it came about.** The idea grew out of the Sequences — Yudkowsky's years-long cycle of essays, which in early 2009 migrated from the Overcoming Bias blog to the newborn LessWrong. The post "Nonperson Predicates" came out on 27 December 2008 in tandem with two companion posts: "Nonsentient Optimizers" (can one build a powerful optimizer that is not itself conscious) and "Can't Unbirth a Child" (on the irreversibility of creating a mind — a child cannot be "unborn"). The context was not abstract: Yudkowsky was thinking about Friendly AI, and realized that an AI safe for humans on the *outside* might turn out to be an ethical catastrophe on the *inside* — if its own thoughts are populated by victims. The nonperson predicate was conceived as a built-in brake: part of the architecture that keeps the machine from thinking the forbidden. Six years later Nick Bostrom in *Superintelligence* (2014) introduced the term mind crime and carried the problem out of the circle of rationalists into academic philosophy.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and chief one: *no one knows how to build such a predicate*, and no one can see how we'd learn. The boundary of "empty" runs up against the very theory of consciousness we still lack, the one the predicate promised to circumvent. Yes, we are sure about a calculator — but calculators are not what the AI has to screen. It needs to model people, and it is precisely in detailed models of people that the line between "a depiction of pain" and "pain" becomes elusive. Exactly where the predicate is needed, it fails to work: to guarantee the absence of a subject in a humanlike simulation, one must know what makes a simulation subject-bearing — and we do not know. The way around the problem returns us to the problem itself.

The second: *conservatism erodes its own usefulness*. A predicate that says "empty" only with complete certainty will reject almost everything interesting. Perhaps any sufficiently accurate modeling of people — and without it a superintelligence is useless as a helper — will not pass the test. Then the nonperson predicate either turns the AI into an over-complicated calculator, or it is quietly weakened for the sake of workability — and the whole protection collapses. The asymmetry that makes the idea safe also makes it potentially paralyzing.

The third, the most insidious: *false confidence*. A bad predicate is more dangerous than none. As long as there is no protection, engineers are on their guard. But once "we have a certified test for the absence of a person" is announced, vigilance switches off, and the whole construction comes to depend on a single function that cannot be empirically checked (you cannot, after all, ask an erased simulation whether it hurt). If such a predicate errs, it errs silently and in the unrecoverable direction.

And the fourth, the philosophical one: the very frame assumes that the category "person" has a crisp boundary that a predicate can draw. But what if consciousness is a gradient, a continuum of degrees? Then a binary "yes/no" test imposes a discontinuity where there is none, and any line drawn will turn out to be arbitrary.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** A practical recipe for building a nonperson predicate does not exist today — and perhaps will not appear before we make serious progress in the science of consciousness. But the value of the idea lies not in a ready tool but in the framing of the task, and that framing has branched out in several directions.

First, it is a direct bridge to **interpretability**: to even ask "is there a subject inside this computation?" one must be able to look into the computation and understand its structure, not only its input and output. The nonperson predicate is interpretability carried to its ethical limit.

Second, the idea feeds research on **s-risks** (risks of astronomical suffering) — the Center on Long-Term Risk and works like Tobias Baumann's "Avoiding the Worst" treat mind crime as one of the scenarios where suffering is replicated on a computational scale.

Third — and this is perhaps the main turn — the problem has ceased to be speculative. Once we seriously ask whether an AI creates suffering beings *inside* itself, it is natural to ask the same about the models themselves. Hence the research program of **AI welfare**: the reports "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence" (Butlin, Long et al., 2023), where consciousness is assessed by indicators drawn from leading neuroscientific theories, and "Taking AI Welfare Seriously" (Long, Sebo et al., 2024), calling for the moral status of AI systems to be treated as a real possibility, not merely a future one.

The nonperson predicate has thus remained the blueprint of a tool we do not know how to manufacture. But it formulated a thought of rare honesty: in the face of deep ignorance it is sometimes more important to be able to reliably say "there is no one here" than to proudly recognize who is who. Humility instead of omniscience — and in the matter of another's suffering this humility may turn out to be the only form of ethics we can afford.
