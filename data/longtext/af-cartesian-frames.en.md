### Cartesian Frames (Scott Garrabrant, 2020)

Draw a vertical line on a sheet of paper. On the left — "me," the agent; on the right — "the world," the environment. Across the line, in one direction, flow my actions; in the other, my observations. This is the Cartesian picture of agency: a thin interface, a clean boundary, no leakage. Almost all of classical theory rests on it — from Turing and cybernetics to reinforcement learning, where there is a policy and there is an environment, and between them an honest API of actions and rewards.

And now a question that breaks the picture: where exactly does the line run? My brain is part of the world. My body is part of the world. The atoms with which I "act" obey the same physics as the atoms of the environment. If an agent is embedded — made of the same stuff as the world, and wholly lodged inside it — then the boundary between it and the environment is not given by nature. An observer draws it. And draws it arbitrarily.

Cartesian frames are Scott Garrabrant's formalism, which makes this arbitrariness honest: it does not hide the boundary but makes it an explicit, manageable object.

The definition is deceptively simple. A Cartesian frame over a set of worlds W is a triple (A, E, ·), where A is the set of possible "decisions" of the agent, E is the set of possible states of the environment, and the outcome function · maps a pair (decision, environment) to a concrete world. It is convenient to picture this as a matrix: rows are elements of A, columns are elements of E, and in cell (a, e) sits the world a·e. The agent chooses a row, the environment chooses a column, and the universe is at the intersection.

The key move is that W is fixed, but the partition into "agent" and "environment" is not. One and the same reality W can be factored by many different frames. You can draw the boundary otherwise: what was part of the environment (say, my hand or my future "self") can be folded into the agent — and there will be more rows, fewer columns. Or, conversely, you can hand over to the environment what seemed like the agent's choice. You can coarsen the agent up to a coalition, refine it down to a subagent, merge two agents into one. Each such redrawing of the line is a new frame over the same W, and the formalism gives rigorous operations for passing between them.

Here is where category theory enters. Frames are precisely **Chu spaces** over W: a construction long known in computer science and logic. Between frames there are morphisms (a pair of maps on A and on E, compatible through ·); they form a category, and in it lives a rich algebra. There is a duality — **transposition**: swap A and E, that is, literally look at the same matrix "through the environment's eyes," making the agent an environment unto itself. There are operations answering to the connectives of linear logic — tensor and its dual par, sum and product — by which frames are glued together and taken apart. And there are substantive derived notions: the set of worlds the agent is able to **ensure** regardless of what the environment chooses (ensurables), and the set of differences it is able to **control** (controllables). It was all set in motion for their sake — to pose rigorously the question "what can the agent secure, whatever the world does?" without leaning on a boundary given in advance.

**How it came about.** Cartesian frames grew out of the agent foundations program at MIRI and directly out of Abram Demski and Garrabrant's "Embedded Agency" (2018), a catalogue of the breakdowns that occur when an agent stops being external to the world. Four classes of problems were gathered there: decision theory, embedded world-models — home to the very Cartesian-boundary blurriness that frames exist to clarify — robust delegation, and subsystem alignment. In the fall of 2020 Garrabrant posted the "Cartesian Frames" series on the Alignment Forum: first "Introduction to Cartesian Frames," then a dozen more posts with definitions, operations, and theorems; an online MIRI seminar on the series ran in parallel. The intent was methodological: to give the foundations of agency a language in which one can prove things about the agent's boundary rather than wave one's hands. Later, for Garrabrant himself, this line grew into "Finite Factored Sets" (2021) — now about factorization and causal structure, but with the same obsession: how to cut reality into parts correctly.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The main charge is detachment from practice. Cartesian frames are extremely abstract; between a Chu space over W and the training of a language model lies a chasm, and no one has yet crossed it. Over the whole decade the agent foundations program at MIRI was regularly reproached for producing beautiful mathematics with no applied alignment payoff — and after MIRI's pivot in 2023–2024 toward communication and strategy, this criticism only grew stronger. Skeptics reasonably ask: does the formalism yield a single result that could not have been obtained more simply, in the language of game theory or ordinary Markov processes? There is also a subtler objection: frames make the choice of boundary explicit, but do not say which boundary is the right one — they give a tool, not a criterion, and the risk is to mistake the richness of the formalism for progress on the substance. In fairness, Garrabrant himself never claimed otherwise: he positioned Cartesian frames as a research tool, "a new way of modeling agency," and not as a solution to the alignment problem.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The value here is not in a theorem you can build into a training loop tomorrow, but in a discipline of thought. Once you have seriously accepted that the agent's boundary is your choice and not a fact of the world, many confusions in safety clear up. What is a subagent that has split off inside a large model — and in which frame is it even "an agent"? What does it mean to "give a system autonomy within this shell" — where does that shell end, and who drew it? From here there is a straight road to Andrew Critch's work on the boundaries and "membranes" of agents, to the question of self-preservation and control, to defining carefully what exactly an optimizer optimizes. Cartesian frames stand alongside embedded agency, functional decision theory, and Vanessa Kosoy's infra-Bayesianism — with that wing of alignment research which holds that we do not yet know how to state rigorously even the question of an agent-within-the-world, and that without such a language practical guarantees will stand on sand. Perhaps it is a dead end. Perhaps it is that rare case where a rightly posed question is worth more than a ready answer. But to draw the line between yourself and the world and not notice that you drew it — that is surely worse.
