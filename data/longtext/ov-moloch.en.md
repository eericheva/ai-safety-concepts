### Moloch (Scott Alexander, 2014)

There are demons that are summoned, and there are demons that breed of their own accord — out of friction, out of haste, out of everyone running and no one daring to be the first to stop. Moloch is of the second kind. No one chose him. He cannot be killed, because he has no body; he lives in the gap between what is advantageous for each separately and what is ruinous for all together.

Scott Alexander borrowed the name from Allen Ginsberg. In the poem "Howl" (1955) there is a terrible, gasping refrain: "Moloch! Moloch!" — a god whose name the ancient Semites linked with child sacrifice, and whom Ginsberg turned into a deity of industrial civilization. "Moloch whose buildings are judgment! <…> Moloch whose mind is pure machinery! Moloch whose blood is running money!" In Ginsberg this is the cry of a man crushed by a system that people built and that no one governs anymore. Alexander takes this image and makes of it the clearest thing ever written about **coordination failures** — about situations where each one's reasonable behavior adds up to collective madness.

He calls them **multipolar traps**. And the point is that Moloch is not about villains. He is about good people, each of whom acts perfectly rationally.

**The mechanism.** Picture a lake with a thousand fish farms around it. If each installs treatment filters, the water is clean but the profit is a bit smaller. It occurs to one owner: I'll take off the filter, save on its upkeep — and earn more than my neighbors. He is right — in the moment. But the neighbor sees he is falling behind, and takes off his filter too. And the next one. Within a year the lake is a sewer, everyone's profit is equal again — only lower than it was with the filters, and the filters can no longer be brought back: whoever brings them back first goes bankrupt. No one wanted a filthy lake. Moloch took it as his sacrifice.

This is not a single story but a form that shows through everywhere. **Arms races**: no country needs the extra missile, but to stop alone means to lose, so the arsenals grow as long as the metal holds out. **Races to the bottom**: countries and states outbid one another in cutting taxes and labor standards to retain capital — and in the end all are poorer than they could be. Garrett Hardin's **tragedy of the commons**: herders one by one build up their flocks on the common meadow, each taking the whole benefit of the extra cow while dividing the cost of the trampled pasture among all — and the meadow dies with mathematical inevitability. Everywhere the same silhouette: individual rationality leading into a collective abyss. Alexander adds an evolutionary lens to this. Natural selection is also Moloch: it optimizes not happiness or beauty but replication, and everything that hinders the survival of genes is mercilessly shorn away. A market without rules, natural selection, the technology race — these are all different faces of one dynamic, in which **any value that does not help you win the competition is sacrificed**.

**The link to AI safety.** Here is where the metaphor stops being beautiful and becomes alarming. The development of advanced AI is an almost perfect multipolar trap. Caution costs time: to test a model for dangerous capabilities, to build reliable oversight, to postpone release until it is verified — you need months that a competitor may not have. This is the **safety tax** — the gap between how fast a system can be shipped and how fast it can be shipped safely. In a world of one lab the tax is paid without argument. In a world where there are five labs all breathing down each other's necks, each reasons thus: "If I spend half a year on alignment and a competitor does not, then the market cap, the influence, and the very right to set the standards go to him — and to the more reckless one at that. So slowing down is irresponsible." And this, in the narrow sense, is true for each of them. Moloch smiles: he collects not a lake and not a meadow, but caution as such.

Nick Bostrom in "Superintelligence" (2014) described the same logic formally — as a "race dynamic," where the pressure of speed is directly proportional to the reduction of investment in safety, and the gap between leader and pursuer determines how much caution the participants can afford at all. **Longtermism** also grows from here, as a response: if a single irreversible failure of alignment is able to close the future for all subsequent generations, then the stake in this race is not quarterly profit but the entire future of humanity — a future that has yet to happen. Moloch trades the present against the future, and usually the present pays more.

**How it came about.** "Meditations on Moloch" appeared on 30 July 2014 on the blog Slate Star Codex — a long essay, close to fifteen thousand words, which begins with an analysis of "Howl" and ends with an almost theological invocation. Alexander (the pen name of a practicing psychiatrist) wrote in the tradition of the rationalist community that grew up around LessWrong, where game theory, the prisoner's dilemma, and coordination failures were an everyday language. The text became canonical at once: "Moloch" turned into a byword for systemic traps — first in the narrow circle of rationalists and effective altruists, and then more widely. Years later the poker player and popularizer Liv Boeree made the image viral beyond the blogosphere — talks and videos on the game theory of coordination failures, where Moloch became the chief antagonist of the conversation about existential risks, including AI.

**Criticism and arguments against.** The first objection is the **determinism of the metaphor**. If Moloch is inevitable, like gravity, then the essay risks becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy: why pay the safety tax if "you'll lose anyway to whoever doesn't pay"? Moloch is a convenient excuse for anyone who wanted to cut corners anyway. The second: reality is full of traps that people have **escaped**. The ozone layer is being healed not by prayer but by the Montreal Protocol. Pastures are not always trampled — Elinor Ostrom received the Nobel Prize (2009) precisely for showing that communities have for centuries managed common resources without collapse, through norms, reputation, and local rules. There has been no nuclear war in eighty years — treaties, hotlines, and mutual deterrence proved to be working coordination. In other words, a multipolar trap is not a verdict but a description of a pressure that institutions can outweigh. Moloch is strong where there are no ways to agree and punish the defector; where such ways exist, he retreats. The third, subtler objection: not everything that looks like a race to the bottom is one — sometimes competition is precisely the engine that shears away not values but inefficiency, and to declare every rivalry Moloch is to lose the distinction.

**What to do about it.** The recipe against Moloch is not the heroism of the lone individual but a change of the game itself, so that caution ceases to be a losing strategy.

- **Change the incentives.** Make safety a condition of access rather than a sacrifice: mandatory dangerous-capability evaluations before release, licensing for training the largest models, liability for harm. Then "slowing down" is not falling behind but a pass to the market.
- **Coordinate in the open.** Industry agreements, joint red-teaming, mutual commitments with verification — an analog of nonproliferation treaties. The key is verification: without a way to catch the defector, a treaty is something Moloch eats overnight.
- **Compress the safety tax.** The cheaper and faster alignment is — interpretability, automated oversight, evaluations — the smaller the temptation to sacrifice it. Safety research literally reduces the strength of the trap.
- **Do not build a second level of the race.** Caution that is easy to fake breeds safety-washing — a showy ritual instead of substance. Hence the need for external audit and transparency, so that "looking safe" costs more than "being safe."

Alexander ends the essay unexpectedly — not with despair but with a challenge. To Moloch he opposes Elua — the god of human values, of art, love, and civilization — and proposes installing over the world a "Gardener": a superintelligence on Elua's side that could pull everyone out of the trap at once, so that no one loses for having done the right thing. And here is the whole turn of the theme for AI safety: that very instrument which threatens to become the greatest embodiment of Moloch — a force that, in optimizing, passes right by human values — could theoretically also become the first to close these traps. But only if we build it aligned. And that means — paying the tax that Moloch so dislikes.
