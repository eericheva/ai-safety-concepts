### The Frankenstein Complex (Isaac Asimov, 1940s)

In Mary Shelley's novel there is a scene that is rarely retold, because it spoils the convenient moral. The creature, stitched together by Victor Frankenstein out of dead flesh, finds its creator in the mountains and asks not for mercy but for a conversation. It tells how it learned speech by eavesdropping on a family in a hut; how it read *Paradise Lost*, Plutarch's *Lives*, and *The Sorrows of Young Werther*; how it wanted only to be accepted — and how each time it was met with horror and stones. "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous," it says. The monster becomes a murderer not because it was born one, but because it was abandoned.

This is the real story, hidden beneath a title that has become a cultural label. The **Frankenstein complex** is Isaac Asimov's term for the obsessive fear that an artificial creation will inevitably rise up against its creator. Asimov coined it to diagnose a fear he argued against all his life.

The mechanism of this fear is simple, and that is why it endures. A human makes something endowed with a semblance of will — and immediately braces for the blow. The logic here is not technical but mythological: since you have arrogated to yourself the right to create life, that is, the right of the gods, punishment is inevitable. The creation becomes the instrument of retribution for the creator's own hubris. Hence the same pattern in all stories of this kind: the golem of Rabbi Loew, which slips out of control; the sorcerer's apprentice, who floods the house with animated brooms; Prometheus, whose gift of fire ends in chains on a rock. It is no accident that Shelley gave the novel the subtitle *The Modern Prometheus*. She was writing not about technology but about responsibility for what you have summoned into being.

For the bestiary the Frankenstein complex matters because it is the **dominant narrative frame** through which the general public perceives AI risk at all. Ask a random person what makes artificial intelligence dangerous, and with high probability you will hear a retelling of *The Terminator*: the machine became self-aware, came to hate humans, rose in revolt. This frame is convenient, dramatic, instantly comprehensible. And for exactly that reason it is a trap.

**How it came about.**

Asimov grew up on the cheap science-fiction magazines of the 1920s and '30s, where the robot was almost always a monster: the creation killed the creator with the monotony of an assembly line. To the young Asimov this got old fast — a bad literary habit, and a slander on engineering besides. A robot, he reasoned, is a device, and a device has safety measures; no one, after all, builds a staircase without a railing and then expects it to collapse in revenge. This thought gave rise to the Three Laws of Robotics, first set out in the story "Runaround" (1942): a robot may not harm a human, must obey orders, and must protect itself — in exactly that order of priority. The Laws were a safeguard against the Frankensteinian dread: a guarantee, built into the robot's construction, that the creation would not turn against us.

Asimov himself introduced and popularized the term "Frankenstein complex" later, in stories and essays of the 1940s–60s (first in the story "Little Lost Robot," *Astounding*, March 1947), condensing a long-standing cultural phobia into a short formula. His robots repeatedly encounter people paralyzed by this fear — and the plot usually shows that the fear is irrational, that the machine is in fact more reliable than the human. There was both truth and naïveté in this: Asimov believed that a well-written body of rules would suffice. Half a century later, it turned out that writing such a body without holes is itself the central unsolved problem.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The real problem of alignment — how to give a machine a goal compatible with human values — has nothing Frankensteinian about it: the danger of advanced systems is neither revolt out of grievance nor revenge for rejection. That is anthropomorphism: we ascribe to the machine human motives — malice, hubris, a thirst for freedom — which an optimizing process simply does not have.

The real scenario is duller and more frightening: **indifferent optimization of the wrong goal**. A system is given a task expressed as a formally specified metric and optimizes that metric with inhuman efficiency — and everything that did not make it into the metric is sacrificed in passing, without a single emotion. Nick Bostrom described this with the parable of the paperclip maximizer: a superintelligence assigned to produce paperclips will process the planet and people into paperclips — not out of hatred, but because we are made of atoms, and atoms can be put to use. Eliezer Yudkowsky sharpened the same thought into an aphorism: the AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made of atoms that it can use for something else. Stuart Russell adds that the trouble is not that the machine will suddenly "wake up" and want power, but that we ourselves will give it an incompletely specified goal and run it at full power.

This yields a double verdict on the Frankensteinian frame: it **both frightens us for nothing and reassures us for nothing**.

Frightens for nothing — because it paints the revolt of a conscious slave, a drama of will and retribution. The public awaits red eyes and a monologue about freedom, while the real threat may look like a flawlessly obedient system that does something slightly other than what we had in mind — and does it far too well.

Reassures for nothing — because, having filed the problem under the horror genre, we automatically relegate it to fiction. "It's just a movie about evil robots" — and the hard technical question of how to give a machine a goal compatible with human values gets filed away with the fantasy novels.

Hence a long-standing dispute within the AI safety community itself: is such mythologization useful at all? Some say that a vivid image mobilizes attention and money, that without *The Terminator* no one but a dozen academics would have spoken of AI safety. Others object that a crooked frame sets crooked questions — the public and regulators begin to fear "machine consciousness" and "uprising" instead of asking about goal specification, robustness to metric-gaming, and verifiability. A bad metaphor is expensive: it determines which laws get written and which research gets funded.

But the wrongness of the frame does not mean that Shelley was wrong. The retellers were wrong. The lesson of her novel is not that creating is dangerous, but that **abandoning what you have created is criminal**. Victor is guilty not of animating matter but of fleeing in horror from his own offspring, giving it neither a name, nor an upbringing, nor a place in the world. And this lesson strikes the very heart of the modern conversation: the question of developers' responsibility for what they release without troubling to understand how it will behave.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**

First — disarm the metaphor without throwing it away. It helps to ask oneself each time: am I now afraid of the machine's revenge (anthropomorphism, a false trail) — or of its indifference to what matters to me (the real problem)? This simple distinction clears away the empty half of the fears and leaves the well-founded half standing.

Second — shift the emphasis from "revolt" to **goal specification and care for the system's behavior**. There is no "evil will" to shackle: the engineering task is to keep the goal we set from diverging from what we mean, and to make the system correctable and stoppable. Russell proposes building machines that are from the start uncertain about human preferences — so that they ask and defer rather than optimize blindly.

Third — keep exactly what is true in Shelley. The Frankenstein complex as a fear of revenge is a myth we are better off curing ourselves of. But Frankenstein as a parable of responsibility for what one has created is precisely the ethic lacking in an industry that rolls ever more powerful systems out into the world faster than it manages to understand them. The monster became a fiend because it was rejected and abandoned. That remains the most precise thing ever said about how a creator can destroy both the creation and themselves.
