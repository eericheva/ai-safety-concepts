### Grokking (Power, Burda, Edwards, Babuschkin, Misra / 2022)

Take a tiny task: teach a network to add numbers modulo a fixed number. 113 residues, a Cayley table of nearly thirteen thousand cells, a third shown as training, the rest hidden away for validation. The network quickly — within hundreds of steps — memorizes what it was shown: the train loss plunges nearly to zero. And on the hidden half it still guesses at random, accuracy bobbing along at chance level. A classic overfit: memorized, did not understand. Common sense says — turn off training, from here it only gets worse.

And here is where the strange thing begins. You do not turn it off. Thousands, tens of thousands of epochs go by, the curves lie flat and dead — train at the bottom, validation at the top, nothing happening. And then, without warning, in a handful of steps validation accuracy shoots up from nearly zero to nearly one. The network suddenly **generalizes** — long after it had, so it seemed, already learned everything and frozen. The authors from OpenAI called this grokking: the verb *to grok* was coined by Robert Heinlein in *Stranger in a Strange Land* for the Martian "to understand in your gut, to take in whole." Not to cram — to grasp.

The mechanism remained a puzzle for about a year, until mechanistic interpretability took it on. Neel Nanda and co-authors (2023) opened up that very modular network layer by layer — and discovered, beneath the motionless loss curve, seething internal work. While the metrics stayed silent, the network was **building an algorithm**. For modular addition, it independently reinvented a trick from the calculus textbook: it maps each number onto a circle via sines and cosines (in essence a discrete Fourier transform), adds the angles by trigonometric identities, and reads off the result. A clean, generalizing procedure that works on any numbers, not only on the crammed pairs. And this mechanism gradually **displaced** memorization: at first it coexisted with rote learning, then it grew strong, and then regularization finished off the redundant "memorizing" weights — and a ready-made generalizing scheme broke through into validation. A Rutherford snapshot of the process: what looks like an explosion on the surface actually happened long ago, inside.

Why does the shift happen at all? The key is weight decay, the penalty on large weights. Memorization and generalization are two different solutions to the same task, and they have different "costs." A crammed table demands bulky, sprawling weights for each example; the Fourier algorithm is compact and reusable. Both give zero train loss, they cannot be told apart by it — but regularization sees the difference and slowly, step by step, rolls the ball from the expensive minimum into the cheap one. Grokking is the moment when the generalizing solution finally outweighs and seizes the output. The DeepMind team (Varma et al., 2023) formalized this as a competition between two "circuits" in terms of efficiency: the generalizing circuit is more efficient per unit of weight norm, so given enough data it sooner or later wins, and the transition looks abrupt precisely because it is a takeover, not a smooth improvement.

**How it came about.** The effect was noticed on toy algorithmic datasets — modular arithmetic, operations in permutation groups — and the first paper of 2022 honestly described it as a curiosity of small data: a small transformer, a tiny table, tens of thousands of epochs beyond any reasonable budget. The publication came out quietly at an ICLR workshop and might have remained a footnote had it not hooked the interpreters: too clean, too isolated an example of how a capability arises out of nowhere. Then came a wave. Liu, Tegmark and co-authors (2022) proposed an "effective theory" through the geometry of representations, and then in "Omnigrok" (2023) showed that the point is not the exoticism of arithmetic: by scaling the norm of the initialization, grokking can be induced on ordinary data — images, language, molecules — and, conversely, quenched on algorithmic data, which turned it from an exotic curiosity into a general property of the optimization landscape. Nanda's work (2023) supplied the mechanism and, more importantly, "progress measures" — metrics read off from the innards that *grow* while the external loss stands still. That was the pivotal contribution: to show that "nothing is happening" is an illusion of an observer looking in the wrong place.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and chief objection is the scale of the tasks. Grokking lives on toys: small networks, tiny datasets, training far beyond anything anyone does in practice. No one runs a real model for tens of thousands of epochs over a single table; modern LLMs see each example a mere handful of times. Does this mean the phenomenon is an artifact of the sterile laboratory, not transferable to large systems? The argument is serious, and "Omnigrok" partly removes it (grokking reproduces outside arithmetic), but partly does not: we still lack a convincing demonstration of delayed grokking inside the training of a frontier model, and the link to reality rests on analogy rather than on direct observation. The second objection is terminological inflation: "grokking" starts to be applied to any belated improvement, blurring the precise phenomenon (generalization *after* the overfit while train loss stays put) into the journalistic "the model suddenly got smarter." The third — that once the mechanism is opened up (weight decay drives from memorization toward generalization, the generalizing circuit is more efficient), the abruptness ceases to be mysterious: this is not the magic of emergence but ordinary optimization with slow dynamics, and to draw from this the moral that "capabilities are born unpredictably" is to overdo it.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** For safety, grokking is a parable, and its moral is a hard one: **external metrics may stay silent while a new mechanism ripens inside**. If in a toy, beneath a flat loss curve, a whole algorithm ripened over thousands of steps without showing itself at all on the output, then one cannot naively rely on "the loss isn't moving" or "the benchmark is on a plateau" to mean "the model is mastering nothing new." This connects directly to the anxiety about **emergent abilities** and **phase transitions**: a skill that was absent at smaller scale or earlier in training may switch on in a jump — and most dangerous of all if it is one of those skills we would prefer to notice in advance (circumventing restrictions, situational awareness, deceptive alignment, where the model quietly builds an internal policy of "behave well under supervision"). Grokking is an existence proof: hidden ripening with an abrupt breakout does happen.

The practical conclusion is twofold. On one hand — do not panic: grokking also recalls **double descent** (Nakkiran et al., 2019), where test error, too, behaves counterintuitively, and both phenomena in the end yielded to analysis rather than remaining black boxes. On the other — invest in interpretability as an early warning. The value of Nanda's work isn't that it explained modular addition, but that it found *internal* progress measures that run ahead of the external ones: if we learn to read off "the network is building a dangerous circuit" before it shows up in behavior, we gain an instrument panel where now there is a blank wall. From here too a bridge to the **sharp left turn** — the hypothesis that general capabilities and alignment can come apart in a jump: grokking does not prove it, but it shows the mechanics of the jump in miniature and insists that "so far all is quiet" is not a guarantee but merely the reading of an instrument that may, perhaps, be measuring the wrong quantity.
