### Takeoff speeds (Hanson–Yudkowsky, 2008; Christiano, 2018)

Imagine you are standing at the foot of a staircase but do not know its height. Maybe it's three steps — and you're at the top before you've properly lifted a foot. Or maybe it's an airport escalator: long, gentle, and while it carries you there is time to look around, change your mind, step off. The whole dispute over takeoff speeds is a dispute about which staircase, exactly, humanity stands on before the transition to superintelligence. And the most unpleasant part: you can learn the height only by beginning the climb.

"Takeoff" here is the transition from roughly human-level systems to systems that qualitatively surpass us. One question, but with two handles. The first is **speed**: weeks or decades? The second is **continuity**: will the curve of capabilities be smooth, with forerunners visible in advance, or will it have a break — an abrupt jump with nothing comparable coming before it. Usually these handles are turned together, yielding two portraits. **Fast, discontinuous takeoff**: a single system, through recursive self-improvement, pulls away within days, overtakes everyone, and gains a decisive strategic advantage — the world becomes unipolar. **Slow, continuous takeoff**: capabilities grow smoothly and in a distributed way, AI first noticeably accelerates the economy, many comparable systems exist at once, and we have years of observable signals — the world is multipolar.

The difference is not academic. Which portrait is true determines literally everything in safety strategy. If takeoff is fast and local — we have one shot, we must align the system in advance and get it right the first time, because there will be no one and no time left to learn from mistakes. If slow and distributed — we correct errors as we go, on ever more powerful but still controllable models, and the central problem shifts from "one system will outplay us" to "how a crowd of actors, each afraid of falling behind, can reach an agreement."

**How it came about.** The dispute took public shape in 2008 as a months-long exchange between two people on the Overcoming Bias blog — the economist Robin Hanson and Eliezer Yudkowsky; later MIRI collected it into the book *The Hanson–Yudkowsky AI-Foom Debate*. Yudkowsky defended FOOM — the idea of an intelligence explosion: a system that has learned to improve itself enters a positive feedback loop, where each cycle's gains raise its ability to produce the next cycle's gains, and the curve shoots almost straight up. The root of this thought reaches back to 1965, to the mathematician Irving Good's paper on the "first ultraintelligent machine," which will design a still better machine — "and there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far behind." Hanson objected from the economist's position: breakthroughs are almost never local, knowledge diffuses, no single firm pulls away from the entire world economy by orders of magnitude on the strength of one internal advantage.

For a long time this remained a conversation between two camps, until in 2018 Paul Christiano wrote a short essay, "Takeoff speeds," that reframed the whole dispute. His move was elegant: stop asking "fast or slow" and ask "continuous or discontinuous." Christiano formulated the "slow takeoff" thesis this way: **before the arrival of a superintelligence that fully transforms the world, there will exist a weaker AI that has already doubled the world economy**. That is, the years of "insane, world-changing" progress will be preceded by years of merely "very fast" progress — with clear warning signs. Meanwhile, in 2014 Nick Bostrom in *Superintelligence* had split the scenarios into fast, moderate, and slow and linked fast takeoff to a "decisive strategic advantage" — the conceptual scaffolding on which the anxiety about a unipolar outcome rests.

Adjacent to this is the **hardware overhang** — the idea that the world may accumulate enormous reserves of cheap compute before an algorithm appears that knows how to use them. When such an algorithm is at last found, it instantly unfolds across the already existing "overhang" of hardware — and this is one of the physical mechanisms capable of turning a smooth curve into a cliff.

**Criticism / arguments against.** An honest steelman of both sides is obligatory, because here no one has won.

In favor of a fast, discontinuous takeoff is the fact that intelligence is not an ordinary commodity. Self-improvement really does have a feedback loop that smelting steel does not. History knows discontinuities: a chain nuclear reaction did not "smoothly build up" out of chemical combustion; AlphaGo Zero, in three days of self-play, traveled a road that took humanity millennia. If the workings of intelligence contain one or two key "simple" principles, their discovery could yield a jump, not a slope.

In favor of a slow, continuous takeoff are the arguments of Hanson and Christiano, joined by the **scaling laws** (Kaplan et al., 2020): the capabilities of contemporary models grow predictably and smoothly as data, parameters, and compute grow — this is an empirically smooth curve, not a series of cliffs, and it is visible to investors and regulators in advance. An AI Impacts investigation under Katja Grace, which went through 37 historical technological trends, found a large discontinuity — a jump of more than a hundred years of progress — in roughly a quarter of them: most trends grew continuously, though discontinuities turned out to be less of a rarity than the authors themselves had expected. Finally, Tom Davidson's report for Open Philanthropy (2023), in a compute-centric model, yields rather a "fast on the calendar, but still continuous" transition — a hybrid that breaks the clean dichotomy.

But the slow picture has its Achilles' heel too, and it is worth stating plainly. Continuity on the graph of capabilities does not guarantee continuity of consequences. The curve may grow smoothly and nevertheless cross a threshold — for example, the threshold past which a system first becomes capable of deception or of autonomous seizure of resources — and on the far side of that threshold "smoothness" is no longer any comfort. And "warning signals" are useful only if the world has a mechanism to react to them; the presence of a signal and the presence of a reaction are different things.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The first honest admission: we do not know which staircase is under our feet, and we must behave so as not to lose whichever answer is right. Strategies that require the world to be strictly fast or strictly slow are fragile.

Practically, the dispute over speeds is a dispute about how many tries we get. If takeoff is fast, the entire bet shifts to the work done "before": interpretability that lets us look inside before deployment; alignment methods that work the first time; hard thresholds that must not be crossed without proven safety. If slow — the center of gravity shifts to governing a multipolar world, and here **Moloch** from Scott Alexander's essay raises its head: even if every individual developer wants to act carefully, the competitive race punishes the careful and rewards those who cut corners. A slow takeoff saves us from the dictatorship of a single system — but in exchange slips us a coordination trap, where no one is a villain and the outcome is bad all the same. Multipolarity buys time, but time without coordination eats itself.

Where does this lead in practice? To a regime that does not pick a side in advance but measures. To track the real rates: is AI's contribution to the economy doubling, is AI itself accelerating AI research (the prime candidate for that very feedback loop), do capabilities appear in jerks or on a slope. To build policy around observable thresholds rather than around a favorite scenario. And to keep in mind that this very discussion is not about predicting a date but about distributing probability between a three-step staircase and an endless escalator. The adult posture is to prepare for both, because the cost of error is asymmetric: to underestimate the steepness is far more expensive than to overestimate it.
