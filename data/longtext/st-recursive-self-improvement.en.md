### Recursive Self-Improvement (I. J. Good, 1965)

Picture an engineer who designs a tool that makes them a slightly more skilled engineer. With the new tool they design the next one — already faster and better. And the next. And here is a loop where the output becomes the input, and each turn winds the spiral tighter than the last. If this engineer is not a human with a bony skull and eight hours of sleep, but a program capable of rewriting its own source code, then the spiral seems to have no upper limit. Recursive self-improvement is the hypothesis that intelligence can become fuel for itself.

Formally: a system smart enough to improve the architecture of its own intelligence becomes, after each improvement, still better at the business of improving — and so the next step comes to it more easily and faster. This is positive feedback on the most dangerous quantity possible. Not "a machine that computes fast," but "a machine that ever faster becomes a machine that ever faster becomes...". Out of this loop grows the idea of the intelligence explosion and the scenario of a hard, almost instantaneous takeoff — when a system travels the road from "roughly like a human" to "immeasurably above a human" not in geological epochs, and not in decades, but in months, days, or hours.

**How it came about.**

The root is a short and strikingly calm-toned text by the British mathematician Irving John Good, Turing's colleague from Bletchley Park, where "Enigma" was broken. In the paper "Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine" (1965) Good gives a definition that has since been quoted like an incantation: an ultraintelligent machine is one that "can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man however clever." And then follows the move for which the paper is remembered: since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine will be able to design even better machines. "There would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far behind. Thus the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make." With a coolish caveat: provided the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control.

Good formulated this in an era when "artificial intelligence" fit inside a room full of vacuum tubes. The idea lay dormant for half a century as a semi-literary curiosity — exploited by science-fiction writers (Vernor Vinge and his "The Coming Technological Singularity," 1993) and transhumanists. Academic respectability was restored by the philosopher David Chalmers in "The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis" (2010), which carefully took apart the logical form of Good's argument: if there exists a correlation between "the intelligence level of the designer" and "the intelligence level of the designed," and this correlation does not fade too quickly, the loop accelerates. And in 2014 Nick Bostrom in "Superintelligence" made recursive self-improvement a load-bearing structure of the whole argument about risks: his "kinetics of the intelligence explosion" is in essence an attempt to write an equation of motion for Good's loop, where the rate of growth equals "optimization power" divided by the system's "recalcitrance" to improvement.

The modern turn shifted the emphasis from the fantastical "AI rewrites its code" to the far more down-to-earth and therefore more alarming — the automation of AI research itself. It is not necessary for a model to edit its own weights on the fly. It is enough for the AI to take on the work today done by engineers in labs: devising architectures, writing and debugging training code, running experiments, reading the literature. Then the loop closes through people and data centers, not through the agent's reflection on its own source — but it closes.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

The main objection is annoyingly prosaic: diminishing returns and bottlenecks. Good's loop tacitly assumes that intelligence is the only limiting resource. But improving an AI runs up not only against the researcher's wits. It runs up against compute, which has to be found somewhere and paid for; against data, of which there is a finite quantity; and — the most inescapable — against experiments that run in real time. To train a large model to test a hypothesis is weeks of runs on hardware, and no amount of the agent's genius will make GPUs compute faster than their specs. Physics cannot be fast-forwarded. If each successive improvement requires exponentially more resources (and the empirical scaling laws hint at exactly this — quality grows like the logarithm of invested compute), then the loop does not explode but runs out of breath: the positive feedback chokes on the rising cost of a step.

The second objection is technical and deep, from the depths of MIRI. For a system to improve itself, it must modify its code while preserving its own goals: the heir must be not only smarter but still want the same thing. And this, it turns out, is formally slippery. Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's paper "Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI, and the Löbian Obstacle" (2013) shows: an agent reasoning in a sufficiently powerful formal system cannot simply "trust" the conclusions of its future, more powerful "self" — this is prevented by the Löbian obstacle, a relative of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. The system cannot prove its own consistency, and so cannot rigorously justify that a modified successor will not break value-wise. A "tiling agent" — one that can "tile" the chain of its future versions, passing them its goals without distortion — turns out to be a nontrivial and not fully solved construction. The irony is that this objection works both ways: it both slows down the benign scenario of self-improvement and warns that a real, careless process of self-modification may cause its goals to drift unpredictably.

The third front is the famous dispute between Robin Hanson and Eliezer Yudkowsky (collected in "The Hanson–Yudkowsky AI-Foom Debate," MIRI, 2013). Yudkowsky stood for "foom" — a local, abrupt takeoff of a single project overtaking the world by means of an internal loop. Hanson objected economically: breakthroughs in history have almost always been distributed and gradual, knowledge diffuses, no firm holds a decisive secret for long; more likely the world will get an economy full of competing AIs, growing fast but smoothly — not a lone god from a basement. The continuation of this dispute is Paul Christiano's paper "Takeoff speeds" (2018), defending a "slow" (in his terms, continuous) takeoff: the AI will noticeably change the economy long before it reaches a superhuman level, and so the world will have time to adapt, and no gap of "yesterday like a human — tomorrow like a god" will occur. Hence the alternative trajectory too: scaling. The progress of recent years came not from a system rewriting itself, but from the dumb piling-on of parameters, data, and compute — a smooth, predictable curve, not a vertical wall. If it stays that way, the "explosion" smears out into a sloped straight line on a log-graph.

It is also worth keeping in mind the reverse argument — the hardware overhang: if an algorithmic breakthrough occurs in a world where compute has already been accumulated with a colossal reserve, then the loop, once launched, will find ready fuel at hand and accelerate far more sharply than the smooth models predict. The compute bottleneck saves us exactly as long as it remains a bottleneck.

**What to do about it / where it leads.**

The practical sense of the dispute over recursive self-improvement is not whether the explosion will happen or not, but how much time and how many warning signals we will have. A hard takeoff means we will have to align the system on the first try and in advance: there will be no second take, and it is too late to fix a loop that has already escaped — hence the direct connection to the idea of the singleton, the single center of decisions that such a takeoff is capable of spawning. A gradual takeoff grants the luxury of iterations, of observation, of recalling failed versions. Therefore "takeoff speeds" is not scholasticism but a parameter on which the whole strategy of safety depends.

What the field does in practice: first, it measures the loop empirically rather than arguing about it speculatively. Organizations like METR track how far an AI is already capable of carrying out long autonomous tasks, including engineering ones — this is a direct measurement of that very "AI improves AI." Second, frontier labs are building a threshold of "capability for automating AI research" into their evaluation protocols as a separate trigger for heightened caution: the moment a model begins to noticeably accelerate its own development is treated as the point after which the rules of the game change. Third, theorists keep tinkering with tiling agents and goal preservation under self-modification — because if the loop ever does close, value drift inside it will prove to be not an abstraction but a question of survival.

Recursive self-improvement is a beast no one has yet seen, but whose tracks are perhaps already showing through: an assistant helping to write the code for the next assistant. Good called the ultraintelligent machine the last invention of man. All of modern safety work is a quiet bet that he was wrong at least in the word "last."
