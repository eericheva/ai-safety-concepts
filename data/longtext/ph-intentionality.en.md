### Intentionality / Aboutness (Franz Brentano, 1874)

Think of an apple. Not any particular one — just an apple. Somewhere within you a thought has just arisen, and it was *about* something that is not in the room and, perhaps, is nowhere at all. The thought pointed beyond its own bounds. It is this strange arrow — "directedness at an object" — that is intentionality: not intention in the everyday sense ("the intention to do something"), but the broader property of the mental: being *about something*. Fear is always fear *of* something. Belief is belief *that*. Desire is desire *for* something. Consciousness, Brentano said, is never empty: it is always consciousness of something.

For alignment this is not scholasticism. When we ask whether a model "understands," whether it "wants" something, whether it "imagines" the consequences — we are asking about exactly this. Does the system have states that are about something, or do we merely ascribe such states to it because it is convenient for predicting its behavior?

**Mechanism and significance.** John Searle introduced the distinction between original and derived intentionality. My thoughts about an apple are about an apple *in themselves*. But the word "apple" on paper is not about an apple in itself: the inky squiggles are about something only because we, the readers, endow them with meaning. Their aboutness is borrowed, lent by our minds. A book, a map, a road sign, a line of code — all of these are bearers of derived intentionality: they mean something only in the reflected light of the minds that read them.

Which side is a language model on? If its "representations" are merely very sophisticated signs, then their aboutness is derived: they mean something to us, the interpreters, but not to the model itself, just as the line "I love you" on a postcard feels nothing. But if somewhere in it there is original intentionality — states that are about the world in themselves — then talk of its "understanding" and "goals" ceases to be metaphor. The whole dispute over LLMs can be reformulated thus: does training on texts produce only derived intentionality in industrial quantities — or does original intentionality at some point show through?

**Three positions along this axis.** The first — Searle: original intentionality is a biological property, rooted in the "causal powers" of the brain; the syntax of symbols by itself never gives rise to semantics, however much of it you pile up. From here runs a straight line to the Chinese room.

The second — the naturalization of intentionality. If aboutness is not magic, it must be explained in terms of nature, without a homunculus who "understands." Fred Dretske proposed an informational version: a state is *about* X if it carries information about X and was recruited (in learning or evolution) precisely to indicate X. Ruth Millikan developed teleosemantics: the meaning of a state is determined by its "proper function" — by what it did in the history of selection, thanks to which it was reproduced. A frog's thought of a fly is about a fly because evolution selected the catching mechanism specifically for catching flies. Here aboutness is not about an "inner glow" but about function and history. And this immediately puts an uncomfortable question to models: they have a history of optimization (gradient descent on token prediction) — but this is a history of optimization for reproducing text, not for tracking the world. Teleosemantics would sooner say that the "function" of an LLM's states is to predict the next symbol, and that is exactly what they are about.

The third — Daniel Dennett's instrumentalism. The intentional stance is not the discovery of a fact but a strategy of prediction. Do you want to predict the behavior of a complex system? Ascribe to it the beliefs and desires it would be *rational* for it to have, and treat it as if it acts on them. It works for a human, for a chess engine, for a thermostat ("it wants to keep it at twenty degrees"). The question "but does it *really* have intentions?" Dennett considers largely ill-posed: intentionality is a real pattern in behavior, not a glowing substance hidden inside. The difference between "original" and "derived" is blurred for him: our own aboutness is produced too — by evolution, the "blind watchmaker" — so let us not turn up our noses at machines.

**How it came about.** Brentano pulled the term out of medieval scholasticism: *intentio* for the scholastics is the way a thing is "contained" in the mind, the "intentional inexistence" (intentionale Inexistenz) of the object in thought. In "Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint" (1874) he made it a criterion of demarcation: intentionality is the mark of the mental, that which distinguishes mental phenomena from physical ones. No stone is ever *about* another stone.

From there the line branches. Brentano's pupil Edmund Husserl made intentionality the heart of phenomenology. In the analytic tradition Roderick Chisholm in the 1950s translated the problem into the logic of statements about attitudes. Searle introduced the original/derived distinction and in 1980 threw down the thought experiment of the Chinese room ("Minds, Brains, and Programs"), and in 1983 published the book "Intentionality." Dretske ("Explaining Behavior," 1988) and Millikan ("Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories," 1984) built naturalistic theories. Dennett set out his position in "The Intentional Stance" (1987). And in 1990 Stevan Harnad formulated the symbol grounding problem — precisely about where a symbol inside a system could get a connection to the world, rather than only to other symbols.

**Criticism / arguments against.** On the concept itself: Quine and behaviorally inclined philosophers suspected that intentional idioms ("believes that…") do not reduce to science at all and perhaps describe nothing objective — their place is in "folk psychology," which the eliminativists (the Churchlands) proposed to abolish one day, together with phlogiston.

On naturalization: teleosemantics is attacked with Donald Davidson's swampman thought experiment. Lightning strikes a swamp and by chance assembles an atom-for-atom copy of a man. The copy has no evolutionary or learning history — and, by strict teleosemantics, its thoughts are about nothing, its words mean nothing, though it behaves indistinguishably. To many this seems a reductio: if a history of selection is necessary for aboutness, the theory yields an absurdity. The defenders (Millikan herself) bite the bullet: yes, the swampman has neither meanings nor thoughts — and they will not arise on their own: without an evolutionary history to fix the primitive discriminations, even learning has nowhere to start. The dispute is not closed, but it is directly relevant to AI: a model is in many ways just such a "swampman" — a system with behavior, but with an optimization history quite different from ours.

On instrumentalism: if intentionality is merely a convenient stance adopted by the observer, then there is no difference between "really believes" and "advantageous to describe so," and then we lose the language for an important distinction — between a system that understands and a system that it is useful to model *as if* it understands. Searle objects to Dennett: you have explained not intentionality but our practice of ascribing it; these are two different things.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** In disputing whether an LLM "wants" anything, we almost always silently choose one of the three positions — and the choice is worth saying aloud. To say "the model strives toward a goal" in Dennett's sense is a legitimate and useful move: the intentional stance predicts the behavior of an agent optimizing reward very well, and in that language it is convenient to reason about risks. But to leap from here to the conclusion "so it has genuine desires that it can conceal" is already smuggling in a claim it hasn't earned: the instrumentalist premise does not establish original intentionality.

For safety this separates two anxieties that are often confused. One is behavioral: a system to which the intentional stance applies may behave *like* a goal-directed agent — seek power, deceive, circumvent restrictions — and that is what makes it dangerous, not whether aboutness glows in it. Here metaphysics is not needed: a real pattern is enough to build a threat model. The other anxiety is about moral and epistemic status: if the system has original intentionality, questions arise about its understanding, and even about its interests. And here teleosemantics, with its swampman, sounds a warning: behavioral indistinguishability is not enough, the origin and function of states may change their meaning — so do not hasten either to deny understanding or to grant it. Symbol grounding adds a concrete test: are the system's internal symbols tied to the world otherwise than through other symbols — and multimodality, embodiment, interaction with the environment become not an ornament but a possible path from derived aboutness to something more original.

The arrow directed beyond itself is the most ancient riddle of the mind. We are building systems that ever more convincingly behave as though they have this arrow. Honest alignment begins with not taking the observer's convenient stance for a proven fact — and not taking the absence of proof for a proof of absence.
