### The family of decision theories: CDT, EDT, TDT, UDT, FDT (Nozick, Yudkowsky, Wei Dai, Soares, ~1969–2017)

A predictor named Omega sets two boxes before you. In the transparent one is a thousand dollars, visible at once. In the closed one is either a million or nothing. The rule is this: Omega has already flown off, but before departing it scanned you and, if it predicted that you would take only the closed box, put a million in it; if it predicted that you would grab both, it left it empty. It is wrong once in a million. The boxes stand there, the money is already inside (or not inside), nothing can be changed. Do you take one box or two?

Here rationality splits in half — and along the line of that split runs a whole family of theories about what it even means to "choose sensibly."

**The first voice** says: the money is already there. Your choice physically cannot reach a hand into the past and shift the million around. So whatever is in the closed box, taking both is strictly better by an extra thousand. Take both. This is **causal decision theory (CDT)**: evaluate an action by its *causal consequences*, treating the past as fixed. You pull the world's levers and watch where the gears will roll — but only forward along the arrow of time.

**The second voice** answers: look at the statistics. Everyone who took both boxes consistently walked away with a thousand. Everyone who took one — with a million. Your choice is *news* about what kind of person you are, and Omega has already read this news. Take one. This is **evidential decision theory (EDT)**: choose an action by what it is *evidence of* — by the conditional probability of good outcomes given this action. Not "what will I cause" but "what will I be glad of as an omen."

On Newcomb's paradox these two clash irreconcilably. And here something unpleasant comes to light: each of them has a task where they look like idiots.

CDT takes the thousand in Newcomb and proudly calls this the "dominant strategy," while the EDT agent beside it counts out a million. But EDT gets caught on the "smoking problem": if there existed a gene that *both* causes cancer *and* makes you reach for a cigarette, EDT would be afraid to light up — not because smoking is harmful, but because the desire to smoke would be *bad news* about the genes. This is an obvious foolishness: the gene is already either there or not, abstaining will not expel it. Each theory cures the other's disease and comes down with its own.

**How it came about.** The thought experiment itself was invented by the physicist William Newcomb, and dragged into philosophy by Robert Nozick in a 1969 paper — which immediately formulated that the task pits two respected principles of choice against each other, the dominance principle and the expected-utility principle. Then for decades philosophy departments honed CDT and EDT as two champions. And then the theme was taken up by people who cared not about a student with boxes but about superintelligence.

Because for an AI, Newcomb is not a puzzle but an engineering specification. Imagine an agent that *can be predicted* (and any program can be copied and run). Then "Omega" is not science fiction but anyone who reads the source code. And here CDT begins to behave catastrophically: it forfeits the million time after time, because it cannot think "my decision and the prediction about me are one and the same quantity, computed twice."

**Timeless Decision Theory (TDT)** of Eliezer Yudkowsky (roughly 2009–2010) proposed a shift of perspective. Do not ask "which *action* to perform" — the past really is fixed. Ask: "which *logical output* should my decision-making algorithm produce?" For if Omega modeled you, then in the world there are *two* copies of one and the same computation — you and its model — and both give the same answer by logical necessity. By changing the algorithm's output, you "simultaneously" change both yourself and the prediction. You decide at once for all your instances: for the original, for the simulation in Omega's head, for the copy in the next room. TDT takes one box — and carries off the million, without violating any physics in doing so.

**Updateless Decision Theory (UDT)** of Wei Dai (also 2009, on LessWrong) came at it from the other end and turned out to be more radical. Its move: stop updating on observations before the choice. A real agent fixes a *policy* — a function "observation → action" — in advance, as if, before its very birth, from behind a veil of ignorance, it had committed to behaving a certain way *in all possible worlds at once*, and now simply executes. Hence "updateless": you do not say "since I have ended up in this situation, then…," you choose the plan optimal over the whole ensemble of worlds, weighted by prior probability. This fixes tasks where the very *willingness* to pay or keep your word must be visible in advance, before you learn whether you got lucky.

**Functional Decision Theory (FDT)** of Yudkowsky and Nate Soares (2017) is already an attempt to write out the intuitions of TDT and UDT in a tidy, publishable form. Its motto can be boiled down to this: a rational agent asks not "what should I do" but "which output of my *decision function*, fixed everywhere this function is realized, leads to the best outcome?" You picture yourself not as a body at a specific point but as a mathematical function whose instances are scattered across the world — in your head, in others' predictions, in simulations — and you choose the value of the function that, stamped into all these places at once, gives the best result. FDT takes the million with one box, pays in the counterfactual mugging, and picks up Parfit's hitchhiker.

They deserve a separate mention — these are the tests on which theories show their teeth.

**Parfit's hitchhiker** (from *Reasons and Persons*, 1984): you are stranded in the desert, and an egoist picks you up who will drive you to the city only if he believes you will pay there. He is an excellent lie detector. The CDT agent, having arrived, reasons: "I am already in the city, there is no point in paying" — and so the detector, still out in the desert, reads this on his face, and CDT dies in the sands. The FDT agent pays, though in the city this is a pure loss — and precisely for that reason stays alive. Rationality here is measured not by what is advantageous *now* but by what it is advantageous *to be*.

**The counterfactual mugging**: Omega tosses a coin, and had it come up heads — it would have given you ten thousand; tails came up — it asks you for a hundred, and will give the winnings only to one who, in the mirror situation, would have paid. To pay when you have already lost seems madness — but an agent willing to pay is, on average across both sides of the coin, richer. This is a direct illustration of why updatelessness is needed.

And then begins the most alarming part: **acausal trade**. If two superintelligences, never meeting, can model each other accurately enough, they are able to "strike a deal" — each behaves cooperatively, knowing that the partner's model will do the same by logical symmetry. From this too grows the infamous **Roko's basilisk** — a thought experiment that a future AI could acausally "blackmail" people in the present. It should be said at once: almost everyone, including MIRI, considers the basilisk untenable — and, importantly, a competent decision theory *neutralizes* it rather than generating it. A UDT/FDT agent that has fixed the policy "I do not yield to blackmail" makes the blackmail pointless before it begins: threatening someone who, by construction, doesn't pay is a waste of resources.

**Criticism / arguments against.** Causalist philosophers (for example, Wolfgang Schwarz in his analysis "On Functional Decision Theory," 2018) answer sharply: FDT declares "correct" all too easily whatever is simply pleasing to its defenders. There are tasks where the FDT agent predictably ends up with less money — and to call it rational in doing so is to substitute "maximize the outcome" with "have a winning character." The second objection is technical and honest: to "decide for all your copies" one needs the notion of a *logical counterfactual* — "what would be if this mathematical function had produced a different value" — and a rigorous theory of such counterfactuals still does not exist; this is an open problem, acknowledged by the authors themselves (see Soares & Fallenstein, "Toward Idealized Decision Theory," 2015). The third: defenders of EDT (Arif Ahmed, "Evidence, Decision and Causality," 2014) reasonably note that much of the winnings of TDT/FDT is achievable with ordinary evidential theory too, if you count carefully. So the "new" theories are not an indisputable consensus but an active battleground.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The choice of a decision theory for an AI is not scholasticism but a question of safety. It determines: whether the superintelligence is vulnerable to blackmail and to manipulation by predictors; whether it can coordinate with its copies and other agents without a communication channel; whether it will keep commitments when keeping them is locally disadvantageous. An agent on "naive" CDT is predictably blackmailable and betrays in the prisoner's dilemma against its own copy — bad properties for something smarter than us. An agent on FDT/UDT is more robust to blackmail, but opens the door into the murky world of acausal trade, whose consequences we understand poorly. The practical conclusion is modest and important: we do not yet know which decision theory to "hardwire in," and we cannot even rigorously write down the best candidates. So decision theory is not a solved chapter of a textbook but one of the few areas where pure philosophy and AI safety turned out to be one and the same question.
