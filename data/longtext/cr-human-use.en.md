### The Human Use of Human Beings (Norbert Wiener, 1950)

In 1960, decades before the word "alignment" existed at all, the sixty-five-year-old Norbert Wiener published a paragraph in the journal Science that today is quoted almost verbatim on the opening pages of AI-safety textbooks: "If we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency with whose operation we cannot efficiently interfere once we have started it… we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire." This is, in essence, a definition of the alignment problem. It was formulated by the father of cybernetics — in an article that, in passing, discussed a chess program — back in the world of punch cards and vacuum-tube calculating machines, and it hasn't aged a day.

Wiener drew the illustrations for this thought not from engineering but from theology and folklore. His favorite example is not a neural network at all, but W. W. Jacobs's story "The Monkey's Paw" (1902): a talisman grants three wishes literally. The parents wish for two hundred pounds — and receive them exactly, as compensation for their son's death at the factory. The wish was granted with perfect precision and a monstrous outcome. Alongside it Wiener sets the sorcerer's apprentice, who set a spell going but does not know the word to stop it, and the genie in the bottle; Stuart Russell would later add to this same list the legend of King Midas, for whom both food and daughter turned to gold. The common nerve of these stories is one: magic — like the machine — takes your wording, not your intention. It is deaf to what you meant. And if the agent acts faster than you can intervene, the gap between "what I said" and "what I wanted" becomes irreversible.

This is the mechanism that Wiener spotted before anyone else. The danger is not that the machine will "revolt" or "come to hate" the human — the idea of a machine's malicious will never much concerned him. The danger is in speed and literalness. "The machine like the djinnee, which can learn and can make decisions on the basis of its learning," he wrote, "will in no way be obliged to make such decisions as we should have made, or will be acceptable to us." An automaton optimizes exactly what is put into it, and does so all the more relentlessly the more powerful it is — so if the criterion is set carelessly, all that power will go in the wrong direction. The monkey's mummified paw grants your request perfectly; the trouble is that the request was the wrong one.

A second, no less important layer of the concept is hidden in the very title of the 1950 book, *The Human Use of Human Beings*. Here Wiener turns the warning the other way: not only can the machine slip out of control, but the human can be built into the machine as a part. He objected sharply to people serving as relays in an automatic system — cogs whose task is to pass on a signal and ask no questions. "Any use of a human being," he insisted, "in which less is demanded of him and less is attributed to him than his full status is a degradation and a waste" — and that status includes his judgment, his freedom, his ability to refuse. For Wiener, automation that turns the worker into an appendage of the conveyor belt, or the official into the executor of an algorithm, was not a technical failure but a moral one — and one nearer and more probable than any revolt of the robots.

**How it came about.** Wiener's work grew out of a military project. During the Second World War he worked on systems for the automatic aiming of anti-aircraft fire — a problem that requires predicting an aircraft's future position and closing the control loop with feedback. Out of this engineering work grew *Cybernetics, or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine* (1948) — a dense, mathematical book for specialists. *The Human Use of Human Beings* (Houghton Mifflin, 1950; revised edition 1954) became its humanistic sequel, written for the general reader: the same ideas about feedback, information, and entropy, but turned toward society, labor, language, and ethics. Wiener had grown up a prodigy under the harsh pressure of a philologist father, and he had been horrified by the use of the atomic bomb; he wrote like someone acutely aware of a scientist's responsibility for the fruits of his work. By 1960, in the article "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation," he compressed the warning into that very formula about "the purpose put into the machine," leaning now not only on folklore but on real learning programs — Arthur Samuel's checkers machine, which was already defeating its own programmer, and the chess automata that had reached the level of a respectable amateur.

**Criticism / arguments against.** In his lifetime Wiener was reproached for alarmism and for projecting fantasies onto machinery that in the 1950s could barely add numbers. Skeptics said: as long as machines are so stupid and slow that a human will have a thousand chances to pull the plug, the whole argument about the "impossibility of intervening" remains literature, not an actual problem. A second, subtler reproach: the analogy with magic is deceptive. "The Monkey's Paw" is frightening precisely because it is supernatural and willful; a real program does not "grant a wish" but honestly minimizes exactly the function you wrote — and the error here is yours, not the program's, so it is solved by ordinary engineering, without metaphysics. There is also a critique from the left: Wiener diagnosed the human's transformation into a relay, but proposed no coherent political program against it — his humanism remained an ethical appeal rather than a strategy. Finally, some modern researchers note that the canonization of Wiener's quote creates the illusion that the alignment problem was "understood as far back as 1960"; in fact Wiener named an intuition but had neither a formal apparatus nor examples of the scale at which the intuition becomes an engineering nightmare.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** What makes Wiener valuable today is that he is the missing bridge between the historical and technical canons of the bestiary. His mummified monkey's paw is the direct ancestor of the modern "hidden complexity of wishes": the thought that human values are too rich to fit into a short objective function, and that any naïve specification will be fulfilled literally and destructively. Stuart Russell draws directly on Wiener's 1960 paragraph in *Human Compatible* (2019), deriving from it the mistaken "standard model" of AI: one cannot put a fixed goal into a machine and set it running — because we almost always formulate the goal imprecisely. From this grows his program of "beneficial machines" that are deliberately uncertain of the human's goals and therefore allow themselves to be corrected and switched off. The "human-as-relay" line, meanwhile, leads to Ivan Illich and his "conviviality" (*Tools for Conviviality*, 1973): a tool should expand human autonomy, not dissolve the human into the system. And its present-day reincarnation is the conversation about people as the "human layer" of recommendation and management algorithms that optimize engagement, using us precisely as relays.

Wiener's practical conclusion is simple and still unfulfilled: before you set an irreversible autonomous agent running, make sure the criterion you have built in is really what you want, and not its literal shadow; and do not build systems in which the human is assigned the role of a part with no right to say "stop." He did not know the word "alignment," but he described its outline — and, more importantly, described the price of error. The monkey's paw always grants the wish. The only question is whether it was the right wish.
