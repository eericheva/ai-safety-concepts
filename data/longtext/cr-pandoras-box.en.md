### Pandora's box (Hesiod, *Works and Days*, ca. 8th century BC)

The lid lifts a finger's width — and it is already too late. Pandora, the first woman, molded by the gods as a "beautiful evil" (Hesiod's epithet for her in the *Theogony*), removes the lid from a great clay vessel, and out into the world scatters everything that until that day humans had not known: hard toil, thousands of nameless griefs, and the sicknesses that come to men "silently; for wise Zeus took away speech from them." The lid falls back — by the will of Zeus — but the woes are already in the air and will not return. Inside, right under the rim of the jar, elpis alone remains — hope. And we still argue about what remains.

For the bestiary what matters is not Hesiod's moral about women (it is frankly misogynistic and belongs to its age), but the pure mechanics of the event. There is a vessel — a closed system in which danger is locked away and harmless. There is an act of opening — a single, often curious or careless action by one agent. And there is irreversibility: after the opening the world changes forever, because what has been released cannot be put back in. The slammed lid does not undo the escape. It is precisely this figure — "once you let it out, you can't catch it" — that makes Pandora a working metaphor for release decisions: publishing the weights of a dangerous model, disclosing a dangerous method, launching a self-propagating system. A downloaded file cannot be recalled by a patch. A trick that has spread across the network cannot be unlearned by the community. An autonomous agent that has gone off into the wild does not obey the "stop" button on your server.

The term infohazard names the bare fact that knowledge can do harm; irreversibility is the property of a transition from one state to another; the unilateralist's curse is about how it only takes one of many decision-makers to decide to open. Pandora glues all of this into a single plot and adds what the dry formulations lack: the intonation of the irreparable and the question about hope.

**How it came about.** In Hesiod there is no "box" at all. In the original, the word is πίθος (pithos) — a large clay vessel for storing grain, wine, or oil, which was dug into the ground; by tradition, Diogenes lived in just such a pithos. The vessel became a "box" through the error of Erasmus of Rotterdam: in his *Adagia* (early 16th century), retelling the myth in Latin, he rendered πίθος as pyxis — "a casket, a small chest." Perhaps the image of Psyche's casket from Apuleius, which she too could not resist opening, got mixed in here. The error proved durable: "Pandora's box" entered all the European languages and appeared in painting, crowding out the original vessel. The art historians Dora and Erwin Panofsky gave the classic reconstruction of this substitution in the book *Pandora's Box: The Changing Aspects of a Mythical Symbol* (1956) — they traced how the image changed from antiquity through the Renaissance to modern times. A separate, centuries-long branch of commentary is the dispute over elpis: Hesiod does not explain whether the hope that remained with humans is a good, or the last evil that was locked away from them. Both lines live on in philology to this day, and neither is accepted as definitive.

So the metaphor of irreversible release acquired, before any technology, a built-in ambiguity: we know for certain that the woes flew out, and we do not know at all what to do with what remained.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first and chief objection is that the myth is fatalistic, whereas real release decisions are discrete and graduated. In Hesiod a single movement of the lid — and that is that, the future is predetermined, there is no playing it back. A real release, though, is not a binary "opened / didn't open" but a staircase of intermediate steps: a closed model, API access with filters, access for vetted researchers, a staged release, fully open weights. The classic example is GPT-2 in 2019, which OpenAI released in stages, from small versions to the full one, watching the consequences; one may argue whether the gesture was justified or was marketing theater, but the very fact of gradation shows: in practice the "box" is opened a crack, not flung wide. The Pandora frame tempts one to think of the catastrophe as instantaneous and total where in fact there is time, feedback, and intermediate states.

The second objection is about the hope at the bottom. If elpis is a good, the myth is suddenly optimistic: yes, the woes flew out, but humans were left with the thing that keeps them going. But if elpis is another evil, stuck in the vessel, then the moral is the reverse: hope is an illusion that was, fortunately, locked away from us, and sobriety is more important than consolation. For a conversation about risks this is not idle philology: in choosing an interpretation, we imperceptibly choose a position — "not all is yet lost, let us work" versus "soothing promises are more dangerous than the woes themselves." The myth does not settle the dispute for us, and an appeal to it as an argument often simply smuggles in the ready-made attitude of whoever invokes it.

The third — Pandora easily turns into a rhetorical bludgeon. "Don't open Pandora's box" can be said about anything: about the printing press, about the internet, about any open publication. The metaphor does not distinguish woes that are truly irreversible and grave from those that are reversible, manageable, or altogether imaginary. Like any argument from catastrophe, it tends to prove too much — and therefore by itself proves nothing, until you have shown a specific mechanism of harm and identified exactly what cannot be reversed.

And yet the core of the metaphor holds up against this criticism. Gradations of release soften but do not undo the fact: having taken the last step of the staircase, you find yourself in a world where the weights have been downloaded by tens of thousands of people, and it is technically impossible to recall them. Irreversibility is not a poetic exaggeration but a literal property of certain actions with information and self-propagating systems. At this point Pandora is right with no reservations at all.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion from the myth is not "open nothing" but this: treat irreversible releases differently from reversible ones. A reversible decision can be made quickly and corrected as you go; an irreversible one demands a higher threshold of certainty, because the cost of an error is never recovered. Hence an asymmetry: when in doubt, postpone the release, since it is almost always possible to release later, but never to "release backward".

Pandora naturally joins with several beasts of the bestiary. The unilateralist's curse (Bostrom, Douglas, Sandberg) shows why it is dangerous when everyone decides on their own whether to open: the more independent players, the more likely that at least one will overestimate the benefit and open the vessel for all the rest — which is why irreversible releases call for coordination, not a race. The vulnerable world hypothesis (Bostrom) adds the frightening possibility of a "black ball" — a technology that is destructive by default: if such a ball exists in the urn of inventions, someday it will be drawn, and Pandora-logic says that putting it back in the urn will no longer work. Infohazards and Bostrom's typology specify what forms the invisible woes take. And differential technological development offers one constructive answer to all of this: not to try to stop progress entirely (the lid cannot be held shut), but to deliberately accelerate protective technologies relative to destructive ones — so that by the time the next vessel inevitably opens, the world knows how to cope with what flies out.

And, finally, Pandora is incomplete without a paired beast — Prometheus. It is one story told from two sides: Prometheus steals fire and gives humans power, Pandora releases the woes and gives humans vulnerability; the gift and the reckoning are two faces of a single act of seizing what once belonged to the gods. To hold both metaphors together is more honest than to hold either alone: any powerful release is at once fire and vessel, and the question is not whether to open in principle, but who, when, how far ajar, and with what hope at the bottom.
