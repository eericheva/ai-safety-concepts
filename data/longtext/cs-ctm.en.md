### The Computational Theory of Mind (Putnam, Fodor, 1960–1975)

Take a calculator and a human, both adding 17 and 26. The silicon heats up, the neurons slosh with sodium and potassium — the physics is different beyond all recognition. But something in these two processes is one and the same, or we would not say that both "add." The computational theory of mind (CTM) is the audacious bet that this very commonality is the essence of thinking. Not flesh, not silicon, but the form of the process: the rule-governed manipulation of symbols, indifferent to what the substrate is made of. Thought is computation. And that means that, if the symbols and rules are arranged correctly, a tin can could think too.

The mechanism here rests on two supports. The first is functionalism: a mental state is defined not by its substance but by its role in the system — by which inputs evoke it, which outputs it leads to, and which other states it connects with. Pain is not C-fibers but that which is caused by damage and causes groaning and avoidance; in principle its role could be played by silicon, or by Martian hydraulics. The second support is Fodor's Language of Thought (LOT): for there to be something for the rules to apply to, one needs the representations themselves, and they are built like a language — with syntax, compositionality, discrete "word"-symbols out of which "sentences" are assembled by grammar. To think "the cat is on the mat" is to have in one's head a structure containing separate symbols CAT, MAT, and the relation ON, joined formally. The brain relates to the mind as hardware to software: one and the same program can be run on different hardware, and vice versa — what matters is not the electronics but the process executed upon them.

From here it is a short step to "strong AI." If thinking is syntactic work over symbols, and a computer is by definition a universal machine for syntactic work over symbols, then there is no fundamental moat between "processing information" and "thinking." A correctly programmed machine does not imitate a mind — it literally has one. CTM is not just one idea in the philosophy of AI among others — it is the load-bearing beam that holds up the very thought that a machine could have a mind at all.

**How it came about.** It all began with Hilary Putnam's articles "Minds and Machines" (1960) and "The Nature of Mental States" (1967): he proposed to regard mental states as functional states of a Turing machine. The move was aimed against two dead ends at once — against dualism (no immaterial substance is needed) and against the crude identity theory "thought = a specific type of neural activity" (otherwise a creature with a different physiology could not feel pain). Functionalism elegantly sidestepped both: what matters is organization, not matter. In 1975 Jerry Fodor, in "The Language of Thought," built the missing floor — the representations over which computations work — and connected philosophy with the then-young cognitivism. The psychology of the sixties and seventies was just rebelling against behaviorism, which forbade peering "inside" the black box; CTM gave it the right to speak of internal states rigorously, mechanistically, without lapsing into introspective mysticism. By the end of the seventies this had become almost the official metaphysics of cognitive science: the mind as the software of the brain.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The blow that became canonical was struck by John Searle in "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980) — the "Chinese Room" thought experiment. A person who knows no Chinese sits in a room with a rulebook and, by formal instructions, produces flawless Chinese answers to Chinese questions. The syntax is impeccable — the understanding is absent. Searle's moral: purely syntactic manipulation of symbols does not give rise to semantics, meaning, "aboutness." And since a computer does exactly this — runs syntax — no program by itself yields understanding. For the machine the symbols remain empty squiggles.

A kindred complaint is Stevan Harnad's symbol grounding problem (1990). Even if a language of thought is spinning inside, what ties its symbols to the world? In the dictionary CAT is defined through other symbols, those through a third set, and so on in a circle; the system never steps out of the purely symbolic carousel to the cats themselves. Without grounding in perception and action, the LOT remains a closed cipher with no referent.

From another flank struck Hubert Dreyfus — as early as "What Computers Can't Do" (1972), before the theory's own heyday. Leaning on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, he argued that human competence is at bottom non-representational: an expert does not run through explicit rules, he "copes skillfully" out of an embodied background of skills, corporeality, involvement in the situation. Common sense cannot be fully formalized — which is exactly why symbolic AI got bogged down on the "obvious." Thinking is rooted in body and world, not floating in syntax.

Finally, from within science came connectionism. Neural networks do not store or move explicit symbols: "knowledge" is smeared across the weights of the connections, processing is parallel and distributed, the boundaries between "symbols" are erased. If the mind works this way, then classical CTM with its discrete language of thought is describing the wrong level. Fodor and Pylyshyn, in "Connectionism and Cognitive Architecture" (1988), snapped back that without compositional structure networks cannot explain the systematicity of thought (whoever understands "John loves Mary" understands "Mary loves John" too) — a dispute that, in essence, still smolders to this day in the debates around large language models.

Most tellingly of all, Putnam himself, in "Representation and Reality" (1988), renounced functionalism — among other reasons, because of the problem of meaning (his own argument that "meanings just ain't in the head") and because almost any physical system can, if one wishes, be interpreted as realizing almost any computational structure. The author walked out of his own house.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** It is important not to conflate the levels of criticism. Dreyfus and connectionism undermine the concrete, symbolic version of CTM (GOFAI, "good old-fashioned AI") — and here they were largely vindicated: the breakthrough came not from rule trees but from learnable distributed representations. But the deeper idea — substrate independence, the thesis that thinking is a kind of informational process and is not tied to carbon — survives the fall of symbolism. A connectionist network also computes; just differently than a machine with an explicit LOT. The dispute shifted from "computation or not" to "what kind of computation."

Searle, however, aims not at the implementation but at semantics and consciousness themselves — and his argument remains an open wound. Hence a practical watershed, critical for safety. One question is whether a system can behave competently, plan, generalize (here CTM's bet is being vindicated before our eyes). Quite another is whether it has understanding, experience, "what it is like to be it." The conflation of these questions is the source of both naive alarmism and naive dismissal. Modern LLMs are a living test: they manipulate symbols on a scale Fodor never dreamed of, and it is on them precisely that it is being tested whether syntax converts into semantics on its own, or whether Searle was right.

For the "Bestiary" CTM is not an obsolete exhibit but a root system. From it grow functionalism (and the question of whether to count a behavioral-cognitive role as sufficient for ascribing a mind), the Chinese Room (the boundary of syntax and meaning), symbol grounding (what ties meaning to the world), the language of thought (whether a mind needs discrete structure), and substrate independence (whether one can think in something other than flesh). The practical conclusion is sober: substrate independence looks ever more plausible as a thesis about capabilities — and ever foggier as a thesis about consciousness. In building and evaluating AI, one should keep these two blades apart. Where we bring them together will determine both whom we one day deem worthy of moral status, and whether we err in both directions at once.
