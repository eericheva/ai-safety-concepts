### Moral Uncertainty: how to act without knowing which ethics is correct

Suppose we want AI to be "moral." But we immediately run into an inconvenient fact: over thousands of years, people have never agreed on which ethics is the right one. Utilitarianism commands us to maximize the general good, deontology to observe inviolable rules, virtue ethics to develop a good character — and in practice all three diverge in their conclusions. If even we ourselves do not know which theory is correct, how are we to build morality into a machine? This is precisely the problem of moral uncertainty: how to act rationally while being unsure not about the facts but about the ethical principles themselves. Will MacAskill, Krister Bykvist, and Toby Ord worked through it systematically in their book "Moral Uncertainty" (2020).

The simplest answer is "My Favorite Theory": pick the most probable ethical theory and follow it. But that rule ignores the stakes: if under one theory an action is only slightly better, while under another it is a monstrous crime, it is reasonable to be cautious, even if the second theory seems less probable. So the authors develop the approach of "maximizing expected choiceworthiness" (MEC): weigh each theory's verdict by your confidence in it and by how much the question matters within that theory — a direct analogue of expected utility, only the averaging runs across ethical theories. The snag here is intertheoretic comparisons: how do you match up the "degree of wrongness" in theories that measure good and evil on different, incommensurable scales? A fine metaphor for this case is the "moral parliament" (Bostrom and Ord): imagine the theories as delegates who bargain and vote in proportion to your confidence in each, seeking a compromise acceptable to all.

For AI, moral uncertainty goes to the very root of alignment. Hard-wiring a single ethical theory into a system and ordering it to optimize strictly according to that theory is the same paperclip trap, only an ethical one. A self-assured utilitarian AI will easily justify an atrocity: cut up one person for organs to save five — the arithmetic checks out, and the horror remains. Moral uncertainty suggests otherwise: an AI should act with ethical humility — hedge, respect "side constraints," avoid actions that any plausible theory would deem a catastrophe, and preserve the "option value" of morality by doing nothing irreversible while the very question of values is unresolved. This is, in essence, Stuart Russell's second principle — the AI must not be certain that it knows our values — translated from the level of preferences to the level of the moral theories themselves.

Intertheoretic comparisons can be declared simply incoherent: what does "in utilitarianism this is three times worse than in Kantianism" mean? Someone says: let us first settle ethics, and only then build it in — but settling it is exactly what we cannot do; if we could, there would be no uncertainty. There is also a risk of paralysis: if you endlessly fear violating any given theory, you may stop acting altogether.

An AI absolutely certain of its ethics is more dangerous than one that doubts and is corrigible. Ethical humility is not a weakness of design but a protective mechanism. And here, too, is a bridge to the next question, one we have so far skirted: if it is so hard to agree on "universal human values," then whose values are we building in at all?
