### Polysemanticity (Chris Olah and the interpretability team, Distill/Anthropic, 2020+)

Take one neuron deep in a vision network — say, from the InceptionV1 family, which Chris Olah's team took apart down to the last screw. Look at what it responds to most strongly. And here's where things get strange. The neuron flares on cats' faces. And on the shiny fronts of cars. And — for some reason — on furry cat legs. One and the same neuron, one and the same scalar activation, three concepts incompatible for a human. It is not a "cat neuron" and not a "car neuron." It is all three at once, fused into a single number.

This is polysemanticity: the property whereby a single unit of the network (a neuron, a direction in activation space) encodes not one meaningful feature but several unrelated ones at once. And this is not a rare curiosity but the norm. If you naively try to "read" the network by looking at neurons one at a time, you will read mush. Features in the network are tangled among neurons, as if every letter of the alphabet had been smeared across several keys, and on each key sat three different letters.

**Why it is this way and not otherwise.** Intuitively one wants the network to be tidy: one neuron — one concept. Such neurons do occur, they are called monosemantic, and popular articles point at them gleefully ("look, a Spider-Man neuron in CLIP!"). But they are a minority. The mechanism that generates polysemanticity was formalized by Anthropic in "Toy Models of Superposition" (2022), and it is called superposition.

The idea is this. The real world demands that the model hold in mind far more distinguishable features than it has neurons. Concepts, faces, syntactic constructions, shades of meaning — tens and hundreds of thousands. Neurons in a layer — thousands. There simply isn't enough room. But statistics come to the rescue: almost all these features are sparse — that is, in any given input only a tiny percentage of them is active. A given picture doesn't have a cat, a road sign, and Cyrillic text all at once. Since features almost never co-occur, the network can afford the luxury of packing them into the same space, laying them over one another. It stores more features than it has dimensions, encoding them not by orthogonal axes but by "almost orthogonal" directions, and an exponentially large number of those can fit into a high-dimensional space. Intersections (interference) occasionally give false firings, but the gain from having managed to cram in an extra hundred features outweighs this rare nuisance.

Hence a direct consequence: the sparser the features, the more advantageous the superposition and the stronger the polysemanticity. "Toy Models" shows this on toy networks literally as a phase transition — you turn the sparsity knob, and the model jumps from an honest "one feature — one dimension" to a dense packing with overlaps. A polysemantic neuron is not a bug of training and not an engineer's carelessness. It is what gradient descent does on purpose, because it is a more efficient way to compress the world.

**Why this matters beyond the pretty pictures.** Mechanistic interpretability is a program whose goal is to understand what exactly the network computes — not from inputs and outputs but from the inside, at the level of the algorithm. If neurons were monosemantic, the task would come down to labeling each of them and tracing how they connect into circuits. But polysemanticity wrecks this plan at the very first step: a neuron cannot be labeled, it has no single meaning. And this is not just an academic worry. It is precisely at this level that we hope one day to catch dangerous things in a model — a hidden goal, the beginnings of deception, a "thought" along the lines of "I'm being tested right now, better behave differently." Such things are not visible in the outputs by definition — a deceiving model deceives for exactly that reason. The only hope is to read them in the innards. And the innards are written in a polysemantic cipher.

**How it came about.** The observation is older than its name. As far back as the era of feature visualization (Nguyen and colleagues, "Multifaceted Feature Visualization," 2016; then "Feature Visualization," Distill, 2017), researchers noticed that optimizing an input for a single neuron gives not one picture but several different "facets" — a hint that the neuron is responsible for several things. The term and its central place on the agenda were cemented by the Circuits series — above all "Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits" (Olah et al., Distill, 2020), where polysemantic neurons are directly named a major challenge for the whole circuits agenda — decomposing the network into comprehensible circuits. Further, the logic developed in two steps. First, researchers worked out where polysemanticity comes from ("Toy Models of Superposition," Elhage et al., 2022). Then they proposed a cure: "Towards Monosemanticity" (Bricken et al., 2023), which used a small language model to show that a sparse autoencoder (SAE) can untangle superposition — decompose the activations into thousands of near-monosemantic features, each with its own comprehensible meaning. In 2024 "Scaling Monosemanticity" (Templeton et al.) carried the approach over to a real production model, Claude 3 Sonnet, pulling out millions of features — from the Golden Gate to concepts like "buggy code" and "flattery."

**Criticism and arguments against.** Here the dispute runs on two levels, and both are serious.

The first: is the neuron even the right unit of analysis? Perhaps polysemanticity is an artifact of our stubbornly looking at coordinate axes (neurons), whereas the "real" features live in arbitrary directions of activation space. Then the problem is not in the network but in our coordinate system. The linear representation hypothesis and the whole SAE project stand on exactly this: if you find the right basis, the features untangle. But this is an assumption, not a theorem. It is not a fact that all features are linear, nor that "feature" is a stable notion at all rather than our projection.

The second level strikes at the cure itself. SAE features are not as flawless as one would like. Their number and their "monosemanticity" depend on hyperparameters: widen the dictionary — and the features split into ever finer shards (feature splitting), and it is unclear where to stop and whether a "true" number of features exists. A criticism of reconstruction has emerged: an SAE reconstructs activations decently, but reconstruction is not the same as correctly capturing causal role. 2024–2025 papers (including from Anthropic itself) honestly note that part of what is found are "dead" features, that interpretations are sometimes fitted after the fact, and that the computational cost of untangling a huge model in full is still prohibitive. In other words: we explained superposition beautifully, but untied it so far only in places and not cheaply.

**What to do about it and where it leads.** The practical conclusion is sober. Do not read neurons one at a time — you will almost certainly be fooled. The unit of honest analysis is not the neuron but the feature reconstructed from superposition; and it must be mined with tools like sparse autoencoders, transcoders, and their heirs, not by peering at activations. The mainline today runs from individual features to circuits over features: not "what does this unit encode," but "which feature reads which and how a computation is assembled from them" (at Anthropic this direction unfolds into "attribution graphs" and the analysis of specific model skills). Polysemanticity in this picture is not an enemy to be defeated but a fundamental property of how neural networks compress reality. To understand it is a precondition for everything else: for one day looking into a sufficiently powerful model and seeing, instead of a mush of numbers, its real intentions — including those it would prefer to hide from us.

For now, polysemanticity remains an honest reminder of the scale of the task. We have learned to say why the network is opaque. To make it transparent in full — not yet.
