### The Distorting Mirror (Angwin / O'Neil / Kleinberg, 2016)

In the spring of 2016 journalists at ProPublica published an investigation that became the starting point for a whole field. They took apart the workings of COMPAS — a commercial algorithm by the firm Northpointe that, in the courts of several U.S. states, assigned defendants a "recidivism risk score." Judges looked at a number from 1 to 10 and decided whether to release a person on bail or give a suspended sentence. ProPublica took more than seven thousand cases from Broward County in Florida and checked the predictions against what actually happened. The conclusion was scalding: among those who ultimately committed no new crime, Black defendants were almost twice as likely as white ones to be mistakenly placed by the system in the "high-risk group." And white defendants who later did reoffend were, conversely, more often given the reassuring tag "low risk." The machine that was bought for the sake of impartiality — for an algorithm has neither race nor mood nor fatigue by the end of a session — reproduced the racial skew of American justice in its output. And lent it the look of arithmetic.

This is **algorithmic bias**: not a failure, not a bug in the code, but a systematic skew in a trained system's decisions against certain groups of people — a skew that passes itself off as neutral objectivity. The problem is not a "malicious" algorithm. The model does exactly what it was taught to do — it finds patterns in the data. The trouble is that data is the compressed history of human decisions, and history is unjust. If for decades Black people were arrested more often at an equal level of crime, if a bank granted loans less often in certain neighborhoods, if managers hired men more often — all of this is written into the numbers. The model conscientiously learns the pattern and extends it into the future, polishing prejudice to the gleam of a statistical regularity.

The mechanism hides in four places, and none of them is about the machine's "consciousness." The first is **the data itself**: the training sample is a cast of the past with all its skews and gaps. The second is **the labels**: what do we teach the model to predict? In criminology the target variable is almost always not "crime" but "arrest" — and these are different things, and the gap between them is precisely the racial one. The third is **the framing of the task**: someone decided that a "good candidate" is one who resembles the company's successful employees, and that "risk" is measured by the probability of a new arrest rather than by the severity of the act. The fourth, the most insidious, is **feedback loops**. Predictive policing sends patrols where the model expects crime; the patrols find more offenses there simply because they are looking; the new data confirms the model; the next day still more cars drive out there. The prophecy feeds itself. Lum and Isaac showed this in 2016 by running the PredPol algorithm over Oakland's drug-arrest data: in simulation the model would send patrols again and again into the same Black neighborhoods, drawing the loop ever tighter.

The second textbook story is about hiring. In 2018 Reuters reported how Amazon quietly wound down an internal résumé-screening tool. The system was trained on résumés sent to the company over ten years — and men had dominated the tech industry during those ten years. The model drew an honest conclusion: a successful candidate looks male. And it began penalizing résumés that contained the word "women's" (as in "captain of the women's chess club") and downgrading graduates of two women's colleges. The engineers scrubbed these features out — but no one could guarantee that the model would not find proxies, indirect correlates of gender, and carry on. The project was shut down. Discrimination cannot be "cut out" by deleting the "gender" field, because gender seeps through hobbies, phrasings, school names. Blindness to a feature is not the same as fairness.

**How it came about.** The concept ripened at the junction of two worlds that rarely talk to each other. On one side — the critical humanities tradition: 2016 brought Cathy O'Neil's book *Weapons of Math Destruction*, in which she described opaque, scalable, harmful models ruling university admissions, loans, and layoffs. Then came Safiya Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* (2018) — about how Google's search results for "black girls" spat out pornography — and Virginia Eubanks's *Automating Inequality* (2018) about how social-services algorithms punish the poor. Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru, in "Gender Shades" (2018), measured the error rates of commercial gender-classification systems: on light-skinned men the systems' error rate was under one percent (0.8%), on dark-skinned women up to 34.7%. Buolamwini called this "the coded gaze." On the other, the engineering-mathematical side, at exactly the same time Barocas and Selbst examined the legal implications of "big data's disparate impact," and Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan derived the theorem discussed below. The ProPublica investigation turned out to be the spark from which both halves of the field caught fire and within a few years fused into the FAccT conference.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The most interesting counterstrike came from Northpointe itself — and it was no mere excuse. They showed that COMPAS is *calibrated*: among those to whom the system assigned, say, a risk score of 7, the share of those who actually reoffended is roughly equal for Black and white defendants. By this definition of fairness the algorithm is flawless. ProPublica was measuring something else — equality of error rates (false positives and false negatives) across races. And here it was not journalistic imprecision that came to light but fundamental mathematics. Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan (and, in parallel, Chouldechova) proved: if the base rates of the event differ across groups, then it is **impossible** to satisfy calibration and equality of error rates simultaneously — except in degenerate cases of perfect prediction or complete equality of the groups. Different versions of "fairness" are mathematically incompatible. You cannot be fair along every axis at once; in choosing a metric you are already making a value choice — and to pretend it is technical is dishonest. This sobers both sides: those who shout "the algorithm is racist" and those who reply "no, it is statistically correct." Both are right by their own metric — and that is the horror: no neutral metric exists to settle the dispute.

There is also a second line of criticism, aimed at the concept itself. Human judges are no less biased, and often worse — opaquely, irreproducibly, without any possibility of audit. An algorithm can at least be measured, compared, corrected. Demanding perfect fairness from the machine when even a human is not perfectly fair ends up, unwittingly, defending the status quo. And finally — the warning of Kearns, Neel, Roth, and Wu (2018) about "fairness gerrymandering": if you optimize equality by race and by gender separately, it is easy to create a glaring skew at their intersection. Fairness across groups and fairness across individuals also diverge.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The technical toolkit has grown large: dataset audits and "data passports" (datasheets, model cards), group-equality metrics, debiasing methods, counterfactual tests of "what if we flip gender/race — does the decision change?" But the impossibility theorem sets a limit: fairness cannot be "fixed" purely by engineering, because fairness is not a property of the code but a political and ethical choice about which error we are willing to tolerate and who pays its price. So the emphasis shifted from "make the algorithm impartial" to "make it accountable": the right to an explanation, mandatory audits, participation of affected communities, and sometimes an honest refusal to automate the decision at all. In regulation this grew into the EU's AI Act, where credit-scoring, hiring, and justice systems are classed as "high-risk."

For the bestiary the "distorting mirror" is a bridge. On the humanities side it joins with **weapons of math destruction**, **the coded gaze**, and **automating inequality**, and through the prejudices absorbed by the corpus — with the **stochastic parrots** that inherit the internet's skews. On the technical side it turns out to be a miniature of the great problem of **value alignment**: we do not know how to formally write down what "fair" and "good" mean, and any attempt of ours to optimize a measurable proxy in place of an elusive goal breeds a skew. Algorithmic bias is Goodhart with a human face: a metric that has become a goal ceases to be a measure of the good. And the mirror we built to see impartial truth honestly shows us ourselves — only we mistook the reflection for a window.
