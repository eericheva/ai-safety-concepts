### Infra-Bayesianism (Vanessa Kosoy & Alex Appel "Diffractor", 2020)

Picture an ideal Bayesian reasoner. It has a prior — a distribution over hypotheses about how the world is arranged — and a theorem that promises: observe, update by Bayes, and sooner or later your predictions will converge to the truth, and your accumulated regret will be bounded. This is beautiful. But the promise has fine print that readers usually skip: the true environment is obliged to lie inside the agent's space of hypotheses. In the jargon — **realizability**, a "grain of truth" in the prior. If the true model is not in the support of the prior, the Bayesian guarantees turn into a pumpkin: the agent can be confidently and indefinitely wrong.

And now notice where the agent itself sits. It does not hover above the Universe, surveying it as an object on a table — it is a **part** of it, a piece of the world, smaller than the world. It cannot contain within itself a full model of the Universe that includes it, together with all its computations — that would be like an atlas obliged to contain an exact, life-sized map of the atlas itself. So for an embedded agent the true environment is **in principle non-realizable**. Classical Bayesianism here is not "a little inaccurate" — it loses its foundation. Infra-Bayesianism is Vanessa Kosoy and Alex Appel's (a.k.a. Diffractor's) attempt to rebuild the foundation itself so that the guarantees survive in a world the agent cannot fully conceive.

Where the Bayesian holds one probability distribution (or a convex mixture of them), the infra-Bayesian holds a **convex closed set of distributions** — an "infra-distribution." This is the language of imprecise probability, of credal sets, and of **Knightian uncertainty**: the distinction Frank Knight drew back in 1921 between risk, where the probabilities are known, and uncertainty, where you do not know even the probabilities themselves. An infra-distribution expresses precisely the second: "the truth is somewhere in this set, but which one exactly — I will not presume to say."

How is one to make decisions over such a set? Pessimistically. The value of a policy is assessed by its **lower expectation** — the minimum of expected utility over all distributions in the set. The agent maximizes this minimum — the classic maxmin. The authors' convenient metaphor is a game against **Murphy**: the malicious demon Murphy chooses, to your detriment, everything not fixed by your knowledge, within the bounds of the admissible set. You are playing not against random nature but against the worst world consistent with your knowledge. Technically, an infra-distribution is represented by its support function, a concave functional on the space of utility functions. So that Bayesian updating stays correct on sets too, the construction is lifted to "sa-measures" (signed measures with an affine term). But the intuition fits in a single phrase: **probabilities where you know; pessimism where you do not**.

Pessimism repairs non-realizability. You no longer need the exact model of the world to sit in your class of hypotheses — it suffices that the true environment be **consistent** with at least one of your infra-hypotheses (that it lie in one of your sets). Then the lower bound you guaranteed yourself by pessimism holds in reality too. Kosoy and Appel prove, within this frame, analogues of the theorems on learnability and regret bounds that **do not require realizability**. A formal learning guarantee where the Bayesian has none — this is the central technical result.

The second gain is more beautiful than the first. Pessimism quells the **malign prior**. In Solomonoff induction the universal prior, as Paul Christiano noted, may turn out to be captured by "consequentialist" hypotheses — in essence internal simulator-agents that slip in convenient predictions and use them to pull the strings of whoever relies on the prior. Bayesian averaging is trusting: if a hypothesis promises the world, its contribution grows. The infra-Bayesian, by contrast, measures a hypothesis by its **worst** scenario. A manipulator whose enticing predictions come true only under obedience is devalued under Murphy's gaze — for you have hedged in advance on the assumption that it will deceive you. Manipulation ceases to be a profitable strategy against your decision rule. For the alignment program this is a rare thing: not a patch, but a structural reason why a whole class of attacks loses its leverage.

**How it came about.** Infra-Bayesianism grew out of Vanessa Kosoy's **learning-theoretic agenda** — a years-long attempt to give alignment a foundation drawn from learning theory with provable guarantees, rather than from heuristics. In late August 2020 Diffractor and Kosoy posted the "Infra-Bayesianism" sequence on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum: from an introductory post through "Basic Inframeasure Theory" to theorems on learning under non-realizability. The work also grew alongside neighboring themes in the MIRI circle on the foundations of agency — embedded agency, Cartesian frames (Scott Garrabrant's), decision theory for embedded agents — where time and again the same thing surfaced: an agent smaller than its world breaks the familiar mathematics. In 2021 Kosoy and Appel extended this work to "infra-Bayesian physicalism" — a formal theory of naturalized induction, where the agent reasons about a physical world containing its own computations. The intellectual ancestors are clearly visible: imprecise probabilities and lower previsions, and in decision theory the **maxmin expected utility** of Gilboa and Schmeidler (1989), which gave the axiomatics of preferences over credal sets.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The chief complaint is the cost of entry. The mathematics is exceedingly technical and inaccessible: sa-measures, support functionals, LF-duality. Because of this barrier the sequence has been genuinely read by only a handful, and a narrow circle works on it. The second is the gap between theory and practice: it is a program about idealized agents, not about neural networks; empirical results are almost nonexistent, and **transferability to real ML** remains disputable, more an article of faith than a proven bridge. The third strikes at the very heart of the idea: pessimism may backfire. Maximizing the worst case tends to breed an **overly cautious and therefore useless** agent, paralyzed by an imagined Murphy and doing nothing valuable — and the quality of the guarantees depends entirely on choosing the infra-sets appropriately and not too broadly, which is itself a nontrivial and non-automated task. Finally, honest skepticism: even if the frame is correct, there is an enormous distance between it and an artifact that actually learns and scales within that framework, and it is unclear whether that distance can be crossed at all.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The value of infra-Bayesianism today is not a ready recipe but a **rare serious mathematical assault on embedded agency** and on the malign prior at once, instead of the usual "let's hope the environment is realizable." For a designer, this means shifting the default stance: where you do not know, hedge against the worst case consistent with the data rather than averaging trustingly; this is a discipline of robustness to the worst case that now has a learning theory under the hood. Where the line leads: to agents with **provable guarantees** within Kosoy's program, to naturalized induction where the agent honestly counts itself as part of the world, and to attempts to feel out a bridge from idealization to real systems — for example, to calibrate the width of pessimism so that it quells manipulation but does not paralyze usefulness. Even if the concrete formalization does not live to see production, the question it posed point-blank — what it even means to "learn and not be deceived" for an agent smaller than its own world — will not go away. And this is exactly the question that almost all the rest of alignment passes by.
