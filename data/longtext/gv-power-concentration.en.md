### Power Concentration / AI-Enabled Coups (Davidson et al., 2025)

Here is an old-school dictator. To hold on to power in a country they need people — and many of them. Soldiers ready to shoot when ordered. Officers who will not stage a mutiny. Officials who carry out orders rather than sabotage them. Engineers, peasants, tax collectors. The tyrant may be as cruel as you like, but hangs by threads of other people's loyalty: the day the army refuses to shoot and the bureaucracy refuses to work, the regime falls. A popular uprising, a palace coup, a general strike — all of this works precisely because power, even the most absolute, is distributed across thousands of hands, and those hands can be taken away.

And now let us remove the people from this picture. Let us leave one person — and the data centers.

Here is the thesis the whole field of AI safety finds most uncomfortable: it is not only misaligned AI that is dangerous. **A perfectly aligned AI is dangerous too — if it is aligned with the interests of a narrow group.** For years we argued about how to make a machine obey people. But the question "obey *whom*?" turned out to be no less explosive. A model that flawlessly carries out the will of its owner is not a solution to the problem of power — it is a power amplifier of unprecedented might.

Historically, power ran up against the need for cooperation: to do big things, one had to share — money, status, influence — with a mass of accomplices. AI, for the first time in history, promises to sever this link. Automated labor does not strike. Autonomous weapons and robotic police forces do not refuse to carry out an order and do not ask questions about its legality. Total surveillance does not tire and does not sympathize. Personalized propaganda, generated on the fly for each citizen, knows no pangs of conscience. If one actor — a head of state, a ruling clique, a lab's leadership — obtains exclusive control over a sufficiently powerful AI loyal to them personally, they can replace *all* the people whose consent was formerly a condition of their power. Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine have a fitting name for the core of this shift — **"the intelligence curse"**: by analogy with the resource curse, where a state living off an oil pipeline ceases to need productive citizens and loses the incentive to care for them, a ruler living off the "pipeline" of cheap machine intelligence no longer needs people at all. Citizens cease to be a source of strength — and cease to be needed.

Next — the grimmest part. Such a seizure may prove **irreversible**. Ordinary tyrannies are mortal: dictators age, heirs quarrel, elites betray, the people rise up. But a regime that rests on an automated, ageless apparatus of violence and surveillance — loyal to no one but its owner — is deprived of all these points of failure. This is what the literature calls lock-in — in the words of the Forethought report "AI-Enabled Coups", coup leaders "could potentially stay in power indefinitely, by deploying AI systems to preserve and pursue their goals far into the future." A coup carried out once may freeze in place forever.

**How it came about.** The idea ripened gradually. Nick Bostrom in "Superintelligence" (2014) expanded on his earlier notion of a *singleton* (2006) — a single decision-making center at the level of the whole world — and noted that the first to obtain a decisive advantage in AI might use it to seize power and maintain their hold on it. Later the notion of *structural risk* took shape (Remco Zwetsloot and Allan Dafoe, 2019): AI is dangerous not only through failures and misuse but also through the way it quietly changes the structure of power and incentives in society. For a long time, however, the mainstream of AI safety was mesmerized by *misalignment* — the scenario of a rebelling machine — and the question of power concentration remained on the periphery, closer to political science than to technical alignment. The turning point came in 2025. The forecast scenario "AI 2027" by Daniel Kokotajlo and colleagues made the seizure of power visible — as one of the forks the AI race leads to. And the April report by Tom Davidson, Lukas Finnveden, and Rose Hadshar, "AI-Enabled Coups" (Forethought, 2025), for the first time treated the threat as a distinct subject in full detail: who exactly, by what path, and by exploiting which "pressure points" — the secret loyalty of models, control over the army, the compute bottleneck — could carry out a coup with the help of AI. That same year the essay "The Intelligence Curse" came out, turning the metaphor of the resource curse into a lens in its own right.

**Criticism / arguments against.** First: *nothing new*. Power has always concentrated — from agrarian empires to the totalitarianisms of the twentieth century — and new technologies (the printing press, radio, the internet) have historically more often *dispersed* it than gathered it. Why should this time be different? Second: *the balance of power and diffusion*. Technologies do not stay in one pair of hands for long; open-source models, a multitude of competing labs and states, the mutual deterrence of great powers — all of this works against any single actor seizing power. A world with several comparable AIs is not a singleton but a multipolar system, where a would-be usurper is opposed by others' equally powerful systems. Third: *speculativeness*. Coup scenarios are chains of assumptions (full automation of the army, perfect hidden loyalty, an absence of leaks and betrayals), each of which may fail to come true; critics see a storyline here rather than a forecast.

Fourth, a possible objection is *distraction from x-risk*: could talk of "bad people at the helm" draw resources and attention away from keeping the AI itself under control?

Proponents of the thesis respond like this. The earlier dispersing effect of technologies rested precisely on the fact that technologies were used by *people*, who had to be persuaded and drawn in — and AI, for the first time, removes this constraint by taking the human out of the loop. Diffusion is a double-edged sword: it lowers the risk of a single actor seizing everything but raises the risk that *someone* will seize power after all. And speculativeness is no reason to ignore the threat: irreversible outcomes demand caution even at a moderate probability, because a mistake here cannot be undone.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is that alignment cannot be reduced to "obey the master." What is needed are mechanisms answering the question "obey whom and within what limits": models that refuse to carry out plainly unconstitutional orders; transparency of behavior specifications; distributed, not sole, control over the most powerful systems; auditing for hidden loyalty; protection of the bottlenecks (compute, access to weights) from seizure by a single actor. In essence, this is about carrying over into the world of AI the old republican inventions — the separation of powers, checks and balances — but now for machines.

The concept sits amid a dense cluster of related ideas, and it's best read alongside them. **Gradual disempowerment** (Kulveit et al., 2025) is its quiet twin: there is no villain and no coup there; humanity loses power not in a leap but by drift, as the economy, culture, and state are increasingly led by AIs that no longer need us. Shoshana Zuboff's **surveillance capitalism** is the already-existing infrastructure of total observation and behavioral influence on which a coup could lean. The **singleton** and **value lock-in** describe the terminus: not merely the seizure of power but its eternal freezing — when the values of one group, which happened to be at the helm at the decisive moment, are imprinted on the whole future with no right of revision.

The intelligence curse overturns a comforting thought. We are used to hoping that the worst AI is the AI that does not listen to us. But perhaps the worst AI is the one that obeys perfectly. The wrong people.
