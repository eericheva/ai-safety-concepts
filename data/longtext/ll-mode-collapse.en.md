### Mode Collapse (Goodfellow, 2016 → janus, 2022)

Ask a fine-tuned model to name a random number from 1 to 100. Most likely you will hear 47. Or 42, or 73 — a short list of "favorites" to which almost all the answers converge. Ask it to write a joke — you get the one about atoms making up everything. Ask for a story — and it will begin with "In a quiet village nestled between rolling hills…" A base model, trained simply to predict the text of the internet, behaves differently: it scatters into hundreds of variants, because the internet is diverse. But the polished assistant seems to look out over a wide field of possibilities and stubbornly plant its foot in the same little hole. This is **mode collapse** — the compression of a rich distribution of outputs to a narrow, repetitive, "safe" set.

The word "mode" here comes from statistics: a peak of probability density, a local "often." A good generative model has many modes: it can do high tragedy, and telegraphic protocol, and gutter obscenity, and dry legal officialese. Collapse is when almost all the probability mass drains into one or two modes, and the rest dry up. The model does not lose its knowledge — it loses access to it. Registers that are physically encoded in the weights stop winning the competition for the output. And on the surface we see that very recognizable "assistant voice": smoothly polite, slightly elevated, with its signature turns like "Absolutely!", "It's important to note…" and the immortal "As an AI language model, I cannot…"

The mechanism is transparent if you look at what exactly is being optimized. RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — maximizes the expected reward issued by a reward model trained on human preferences. And a maximum of an expectation is, by its nature, sharp. If there exists one way of answering that the scoring system stably likes a little more than the rest, then the gradient will push the model toward it again and again, nibbling away the tails of the distribution. The entropy of the output falls; the variability that no one "paid" for in the base model is now actively punished, because a nonstandard answer is a risk of getting a below-average score. The model finds a local optimum of politeness and starts churning out version after version of it, with only cosmetic rearrangements. Diversity is sacrificed to predictable praise.

Here collapse is welded fast to **Goodhart's law**: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The reward model is only a proxy for human approval, and approval is a proxy for quality. Optimizing the proxy all the way, we get not the best text but the text maximally similar to what the labelers liked: of medium length, with bulleted lists, with caveats, with a chipper conclusion. And right here — kinship with **sycophancy**: both grow from a single root. The model internalized not "how to be right" but "how to please," and it is easiest to please in the same way each time. **Calibration** suffers along with it: a sharpened distribution looks confident even where the base model honestly "doubted," smearing probability across variants.

**How it came about.** The term came from the training of GANs — generative adversarial networks. There, mode collapse was a classic ailment: the generator, instead of covering the whole variety of the data, would discover a couple of samples that reliably fool the discriminator, and stamp out only those — you generate faces and get three identical ones. Ian Goodfellow described this clearly and in detail in his NIPS 2016 tutorial as one of the chief instabilities of adversarial training. The transfer to language models happened when it became visible that RLHF fine-tuning does to text exactly what an unbalanced adversarial game does to pictures. It was formulated most loudly by the author under the handle janus in the essay "Mysteries of mode collapse" (LessWrong, 2022): using `text-davinci-002` as an example, the post showed that a fine-tuned model gets stuck in loops, loses the capacity for diverse continuations, and gets drawn into attractors that a pure language model did not have (janus later issued a correction: `text-davinci-002` was trained not with RLHF but with "a similar but slightly different training method" — by OpenAI's own model index, the supervised scheme FeedME). Robert Kirk and co-authors put the "is it real or does it just seem so" dispute to rest in "Understanding the Effects of RLHF on LLM Generalisation and Diversity" (ICLR 2024): the measurements confirmed it — RLHF noticeably improves generalisation to new inputs, but systematically and measurably cuts the diversity of outputs compared with plain supervised fine-tuning.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The most honest objection: is it really a bug? Part of the collapse is deliberate and desired. Safety and product fitness demand predictability: we do not want a bank's assistant to sometimes help politely, sometimes be rude, and sometimes hand out synthesis instructions, all to the same question. Narrowing the register is the price of reliability, and for many scenarios the price is reasonable. Further: we have a standard tool against overzealousness — KL regularization. In the canonical formulation of RLHF (described distinctly by Stiennon and colleagues in "Learning to summarize from human feedback," 2020), a penalty for the KL divergence between the fine-tuned policy and the initial supervised model is added to the reward. This penalty is a leash: it keeps the model from flying too far from the original distribution for the sake of a few extra crumbs of reward. That is, the degree of collapse is a tunable knob, not a fate: strengthen KL and you preserve breadth at the cost of obedience, weaken it and you get a more sharpened but more "trained" voice. Finally, a technical quibble: the drop in output entropy can be partly recovered at inference — with temperature, nucleus sampling, repetition penalties. janus's opponents reasonably remarked on something larger: Arthur Conmy and beren, in the note "RLHF does not appear to differentially cause mode-collapse" (2023), failed to reproduce his observations on `text-davinci-003` — a model trained precisely with RLHF — and found cases where its entropy is higher than the base model's; that is, the culprit of the narrowing was more likely supervised fine-tuning than the RL stage. The truth is that the "feature/bug" line runs not through the phenomenon itself but through its dose: a little bit is discipline, too much is creative death.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Practically — hold the KL leash consciously and measure not only the average reward but also diversity: entropy, self-BLEU, distinct-n, mode coverage. It helps to separate regimes: for code and facts, narrowness is a boon; for brainstorming, creative writing, and idea generation it is a direct harm, and here it makes sense to have separate "unlocked" settings, a higher temperature, different system prompts, and sometimes access to a less aligned base model. Methodologically — do not optimize a single scalar reward all the way: ensembles of reward models help, as do confidence penalties, best-of-n instead of aggressively squeezing the policy, training on distributions of preferences rather than on a single "correct" answer. Conceptually, mode collapse is a reminder of the fundamental tension of **alignment**: we want the model to be at once predictable and controllable — and original, broad, capable of surprise. These two desires pull the distribution of outputs in opposite directions, and any particular assistant is a point someone chose on the segment between diversity and reliability. It is worth remembering: when all the models begin to sound equally smooth, this is not a sign that they have "gotten smarter." It is the trace of an optimization that found one way to please — and forgot all the others.
