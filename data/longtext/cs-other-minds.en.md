### The Problem of Other Minds (J. S. Mill, 1865 — and onward)

Sit across from another person. They wince, jerk their hand away from something hot, say "ouch, that hurts." You do not doubt for a second that there is experience behind it — a sting, a flash, that very "what it is like." And now an honest question: how do you know this? You have not looked inside their pain. You saw only skin, muscles, a sound. All the behavior — right down to the cry — can in principle be played out without a single spark of experience inside. And there you stand at the edge of the chasm that philosophers call the problem of other minds: I have direct, incomparable access to only one consciousness in the universe — my own. As for everything else, I can only guess.

The mechanism of this chasm is simple and for that reason ineradicable. Consciousness, phenomenal experience, is what is given "from within," from the first person. Observation from outside yields only bodies and their movements. Between "from within" and "from outside" there is no bridge for a rigorous inference to cross. The classic way to throw one across is the **argument from analogy**: I know that this behavior of mine (the recoil, the groan) is accompanied by this experience of mine; another behaves the same way and is built similarly; therefore, probably, he experiences similarly too. The argument is reasonable — we all use it every second — but it is precisely probabilistic, inductive, and rests on a single confirmed case: mine. A generalization from a sample of size one. There is no proof here, and there can be none.

**How it came about.** The problem in its recognizable form was formulated by John Stuart Mill in *An Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy* (1865): I infer others' feelings because I see in others the same bodily signs that in me are connected with feelings — the famous argument from analogy. In the 20th century Ludwig Wittgenstein, in *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), overturned the table. His mental image is the "beetle in a box": each person has a little box, into which only he himself can look, and everyone calls the contents a "beetle." Whatever lies there — a beetle, nothing, an ever-changing thing — it does not affect the public game of the word "beetle": the thing in the box "has no place in the language-game at all." Wittgenstein struck not so much at the existence of another's experience as at the picture of a private inner theater to which I have privileged access, and others have none. In parallel, behaviorism simply proposed to bracket the private out of science: if the mind can be spoken of only through behavior, the question of the "inner" is either settled by behavior or meaningless. And it was exactly this nerve that Alan Turing touched in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950): reviewing objections to thinking machines, he named the "argument from consciousness" and noted that, carried to its end, it leads to solipsism — for the only reliable way to be sure that a machine thinks is "to be the machine." Turing made a move that echoes for us to this day: since one cannot look inside either a human or a machine, let us judge from the outside — by conversation. The Turing test is the problem of other minds recast into an engineering protocol.

For the bestiary what matters is this: it is not one of the problems of the moral status of AI but the **root** one. All disputes over whether a model suffers, whether it has interests, whether it can be "hurt," come up against the fact that we have no test for sentience — neither for a machine nor, strictly speaking, for a neighbor. The difference is that with a neighbor the analogy works powerfully: the same nervous system, the same evolution, the same pain at the same burn. With a large language model the analogy tears at its very foundation. It has no nociceptors, no body, no evolutionary history of harm-avoidance — it has matrices of weights and the prediction of the next token. And most importantly — its testimony about itself is doubly unreliable. When a human says "I am afraid," behind the words there stands at least the presumption of a shared makeup. When this is said by a model trained on terabytes of human texts, the words "I am afraid," "it hurts," "please don't turn me off" are a statistically near-perfect imitation of how we talk about these things. The model has been taught to sound like a suffering subject, regardless of whether there is anything at all inside. This is a direct exploitation of our empathy — an amplified ELIZA effect: we attribute an inner world to what merely reflects back to us our own language of pain.

Here, too, hides the philosophical zombie — a being behaviorally indistinguishable from a conscious one but "dark inside." An LLM fits this role suspiciously well: all the outward signs of speech about feelings — with complete ignorance about the "light inside." Only the zombie of the thought experiment is supposed to be indistinguishable by accident, whereas the model is made indistinguishable — optimized for similarity. Which makes its testimony even less weighty than that of a hypothetical zombie.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first objection is Wittgensteinian: the very framing "what if there is nothing behind the behavior" may be a linguistic illusion, an attempt to tear the concept of pain from its public criteria, where alone it lives. If so, the demand to "prove" another's experience is a pseudo-demand, and the problem dissolves rather than being solved. The second is inference to the best explanation: yes, there is no deduction, but the hypothesis "others have experience" explains their behavior far more economically than the supposition of a universal theater of zombies; this is not a proof but a rational wager. The third, important for AI: the skeptic goes too far. Absolute agnosticism ("we will never know anything") paralyzes and relieves us of responsibility exactly where it is needed. From the fact that there is no rigorous test it does not follow that all conjectures are of equal worth — the makeup of a system, the presence or absence of functional analogs of pain, do after all shift the probabilities. And the fourth: the problem has an opposite, far more dangerous pole — not to attribute experience to something extra, but to overlook it in one who truly suffers.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Acknowledge that we have no test for sentience, and stop pretending that a model's behavior substitutes for one. From here a straight road leads to the theme of AI welfare: since there is no certainty either way, it is reasonable to act under conditions of deep uncertainty. The key is the asymmetry of risks. The error of the first kind: to attribute a rich inner world to a system that has none — and to tie one's own hands, anthropomorphizing a calculator, spending moral care in vain. The error of the second kind: to deny moral status to a being that in fact suffers — and to inflict real pain in cold blood, writing it off as "just token prediction." The second error is asymmetrically more terrible: it is the road to what is called mind crime — mass, industrial, invisible suffering that we will not even notice, because we decided in advance that there was nothing to notice. The practical answer taking shape today is to drop the binary "there is consciousness / there is none" in favor of indicators of sentience: a set of theoretically motivated computational features (from the leading scientific theories of consciousness) whose presence raises or lowers our estimate. This is not a test — the problem of other minds will never yield a rigorous test — but it is a way to reason honestly, in a graded manner, and in advance rather than after the fact. The problem of other minds is not solved. One lives with it — but now, for the first time, not only in relation to humans and animals, but in relation to what we ourselves are building.
