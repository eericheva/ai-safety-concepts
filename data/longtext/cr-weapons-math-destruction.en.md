### Weapons of Math Destruction (Cathy O'Neil, 2016)

A schoolteacher from Washington, D.C., named Sarah Wysocki received excellent reviews from her principal and her students' parents — one of them called her "one of the best teachers I've ever come into contact with." And then she was fired. Not by a person — by a model. An algorithm called IMPACT judged that her students had fallen short on standardized tests, assigned her a low "value-added rating," and passed sentence. There was no one to appeal to: the formula was deemed objective, and objectivity does not argue. It later emerged that the previous teachers had most likely doctored the results, inflating the children's baseline — so that the "drop" in scores was an artifact of someone else's cheating. But the model could not have known this and did not want to know it. It simply computed.

With this story Cathy O'Neil — a mathematician with a Harvard PhD, a former hedge-fund quant who lived through the 2008 crash from the inside — opens her book *Weapons of Math Destruction*. And she gives the beast a name: **weapons of math destruction**. The play on "weapons of mass destruction" here is not ornament but diagnosis. O'Neil argues that among the algorithms governing our lives there is a special class — malignant models that do not merely err but systematically maim the most defenseless, cloaked in the mantle of mathematics.

By O'Neil's account, a model must have three traits to earn the title of WMD. The first — **opacity**: the victim does not understand how they were scored and cannot contest the verdict; the formula is hidden in a proprietary black box or is simply too complex for anyone to explain. The second — **scale**: the model is replicated across millions of people, turning a private error into a public epidemic. The third — **damage**: the consequences are ruinous and fall disproportionately on the poor, black people, the sick, the marginalized — on those with the fewest resources to defend themselves.

The beast's key mechanism is a **vicious feedback loop**. Take "predictive policing." A model is trained on historical arrest data and patrols are sent where crime is "predicted." More patrols in a poor district — more petty offenses recorded — more arrests — "the data confirms" that the district is criminal — even more patrols. The model does not predict crime; it predicts police attention and then reinforces it. The same with credit scoring: a low score denies a person a decent job and cheap credit, poverty deepens, the score falls lower still. The model does not describe reality — it produces it, and then cites it as proof of its own correctness.

This is **mathwashing** (a term coined in 2016 by Fred Benenson, Kickstarter's former VP of Data): a social decision, often biased and cruel, is passed through a formula and comes out the other end looking like a neutral, scientific, incontestable fact. "Models are opinions embedded in mathematics" — O'Neil's central phrase. Behind the appearance of objectivity hides someone's choice: which data to take, what to designate as the target variable, which error to count as acceptable. A hiring score screens out candidates with the "wrong" zip code or a résumé gap due to maternity leave — and does so silently, for tens of thousands of vacancies at once.

**How it came about.** O'Neil came to the subject not from academia but from disillusionment. Trained as a pure mathematician, she worked at Barnard College and then, at the height of the bubble, left for the hedge fund D.E. Shaw — and found herself inside the very machine that in 2008 shattered other people's lives while the quants themselves came out clean. She quit the industry, joined Occupy Wall Street, started the blog mathbabe, and began methodically collecting cases where mathematics serves not truth but power. The 2016 book was the upshot of this turn — from insider to critic. It came out at an opportune moment: right after ProPublica's investigation of the COMPAS system, which assessed recidivism risk and systematically inflated it for black defendants. *Weapons of Math Destruction* made the National Book Award longlist and set the vocabulary for a whole field — what would later be called algorithmic accountability.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The first — methodological: the book is built on vivid anecdotes rather than systematic data. Reviewers said so: the geographer Danny Dorling, writing in Times Higher Education, found the evidence for a number of its claims "scant", and John McGowan, reviewing it on MathBlog, counted "only a few numbers" in it. An awkward charge for an author who denounces the lack of checks on other people's models. The second — the definition of WMD is blurry: the three criteria (opacity, scale, damage) are subjective; everyone draws the line between "weapon" and "just a bad model" in their own way, and almost any automation can be stretched to fit the label.

The third, and perhaps the main one — **the baseline problem**: the alternative to the algorithm is not perfect justice but a living judge, HR officer, or police officer, whose prejudices are also real but less transparent, and cannot be audited. A well-built model is at least loggable and checkable — and you cannot hook up a human to a log. O'Neil underrates this counterargument: she brilliantly shows what is wrong with models, but has a weaker answer for why they are worse than what came before them. Finally, techno-optimists object that many of the described ills are not a property of mathematics but a property of bad mathematics, and are cured not by abandoning models but by better models, honest data, and regulation.

To be fair, O'Neil anticipates some of these objections — she is no Luddite and states plainly that the goal is not to ban algorithms but to make them answerable. But the reproach of asymmetry — that the criticism is more vivid than the prescriptions — remains.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** O'Neil proposes not the destruction of the machines but their taming: mandatory audits of algorithms, a "Hippocratic oath for data scientists," a right to an explanation and to contest a decision, regulation modeled on what exists for drugs and finance. She herself founded the consultancy ORCAA, devoted to auditing algorithms — that is, she tried to turn criticism into a craft. Since 2016 the push for algorithmic accountability has grown: the GDPR arrived with its "right to explanation," the US Congress introduced (but never passed) the Algorithmic Accountability Act, the FAccT conference took shape, and in 2024 came the European AI Act, directly banning social scoring and requiring human oversight of high-risk systems. The beast from the book became a character in the laws.

But for the Bestiary, what matters more is its kin. WMD is the humanistic-critical twin of alignment's engineering fears, and it holds hands with several neighbors on the map. With **automating inequality** by Virginia Eubanks (2018), who showed how digital welfare systems in the US turn poverty into a sentence — a "digital poorhouse." With the **coded gaze** of Joy Buolamwini: her Gender Shades project, done with Timnit Gebru, proved that commercial gender-classification systems err on dark-skinned women dozens of times more often than on light-skinned men — bias literally sewn into the optics. And, deepest of all, with **Goodhart's law**: as soon as a teacher's rating or recidivism risk becomes the target, it ceases to be an honest measure — the metric supplants the goal, and the system begins to optimize a simulacrum. WMD is, in essence, Goodhart's law multiplied by scale and power and aimed down the social ladder.

And here two traditions that usually do not talk to each other converge. Technical alignment fears a superintelligence that optimizes the wrong goal too well. O'Neil shows that we do not need a superintelligence to get exactly this catastrophe — a stupid linear regression, deployed across millions of people and shielded from criticism by the word "mathematics," already optimizes the wrong goal and breaks lives. The analogy is strongest where a metric supplants the goal, but the failures O'Neil describes also involve human incentives and institutions that make harmful scores authoritative. Sarah Wysocki's case is an early, small, entirely human version of the alignment problem. And so weapons of math destruction stand in the bestiary not off in a corner of the humanistic wing, but in the very center — as a reminder that misalignment did not begin in the future but has already happened, quietly, in a Washington, D.C. school district.
