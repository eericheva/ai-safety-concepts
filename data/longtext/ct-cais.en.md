### Comprehensive AI Services (K. Eric Drexler, 2019)

Picture two futures. In the first there is a single creature: a superintelligence that has a goal, a will, and the world as raw material to be shaped by that will. It wakes, surveys the planet, and begins rearranging atoms to fit its utility function. This is Nick Bostrom's picture, and nearly all of early safety theory grew up as a defense against exactly that picture: how to lock up the genie, how to write its desires so that it does not remake us into paperclips.

In the second future there is no "it" at all. There is a data center packed with thousands of narrow services: one translates, another folds proteins, a third writes code, a fourth designs the fifth. None of them has a "life" beyond its task — each switches on in response to a request, produces a result, and goes dark. Taken singly, they are transparent and replaceable, like contractors. And taken together, they outperform humanity at any measurable skill. Superintelligence is there; a sovereign is not.

The second future is **Comprehensive AI Services** — Eric Drexler's thesis. His proposal is that what we call "general intelligence" need not be an *agent*. Generality can be assembled from below — out of a multitude of specialized, task-bounded and time-bounded services that in aggregate cover the whole space of human tasks. Each service is trained for a concrete function rather than released into the world as an autonomous doer pursuing its own goals. And — the key turn — even the development of new AIs can itself be delegated to services. AI that creates AI, as one more service in the catalogue rather than as a self-reproducing subject.

If superhuman capability arrives not as a single sovereign but as a network of tools, then safety is not about how to instill the right values into the head of a god, but about how to design, test, and constrain services. A service has no reason to resist being switched off: it has no continuous goal worth staying alive for. There is no instrumental convergence toward self-preservation and resource acquisition, because there is no final goal that these sub-goals would serve. Controlling a contractor who forgets the task once the work is delivered is incomparably easier than keeping in check an ever-wakeful strategist. This reframing directly continues the **oracle / tool** line and the old dispute of **agency versus instrumentality**: Drexler says that the instrumental path is not merely safer — it is also more *natural*, because this is how machine learning is actually built, task by task.

**How it came about.** Drexler is an almost mythological figure: it was he who, in 1986, with the book *Engines of Creation*, launched the very field of molecular nanotechnology and gave the world the fear of "gray goo." Thirty years later he came to the Future of Humanity Institute (FHI) in Oxford — Nick Bostrom's own institute, the one that had produced *Superintelligence* (2014) with its monolithic agent. And he wrote a long technical report of more than two hundred pages, FHI Technical Report #2019-1 — "Reframing Superintelligence" — in essence a challenge from within to the institute's prevailing view. The text came out in January 2019, at a moment when the paradigm of "the single agent as the chief threat" was almost dogma in the community. Drexler did not dispute that AI is dangerous; he disputed *in what form* the danger would arrive. The report spread not thanks to the academic press but through Rohin Shah's review on the Alignment Forum, which compressed two hundred pages into a readable series of theses and gave the term CAIS currency. Since then the acronym has lived as a convenient name for a whole camp: "superintelligence as an economy of services, not as a personality."

**Criticism / arguments against.** The objections came pouring in quickly, and they all strike the same spot — the boundary between "tool" and "agent," which Drexler considers firm and the critics, Richard Ngo among them, consider permeable.

First: *services recombine into an agent*. Nothing stops you from wrapping narrow services in a "planner → executor → critic" loop and getting something that acquires a stable, temporally extended goal. Agency here is emergent — it is in none of the building blocks, but it is in the construction made of them. The transparency of an individual service is not inherited by the composition.

Second, and this objection turned out to be prophetic: *economic and competitive pressure pushes toward agents*. A bounded service is, by definition, less useful than an autonomous doer for whom you can set a fuzzy goal and walk away. Agents are more useful — and so the market will produce them, and the **AI race** between labs and countries will reward whoever removes the limiters first. Drexler's discipline of "keep everything narrow and ephemeral" requires that *everyone* observe it voluntarily, forgoing the advantage — and that is exactly what a race does not allow.

Third — and here history has already handed down a partial verdict. CAIS describes a world where we deliberately slice intelligence into services. Reality after 2020 went the opposite way: large language models are precisely *general*, task-unbounded systems, and on top of them grew AutoGPT, agentic frameworks, autonomous assistants with memory, tools, and goals. DeepMind, back in 2022, named its model Gato outright — "A Generalist Agent." We are building not a catalogue of narrow contractors but a single generalist to which we keep grafting autonomy.

And fourth, the subtlest objection, which CAIS does not answer even if its vision were to come true: *the gradual disempowerment of the human* requires no single agent at all. One can live in a world of perfectly narrow, obedient services and still quietly lose control — when the economy, governance, and defense are so tied to AI services that a human can no longer either understand a decision or refuse it without collapse. The work of Jan Kulveit and co-authors on gradual disempowerment (2025) describes exactly this dynamic: catastrophe as the slow displacement of humans from the loops where decisions are made, rather than as a blow from a sovereign usurper. CAIS neutralizes the genie — but promises no protection against this drift.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The most honest way to read CAIS is not as a prediction but as a *design fork and a norm*. As a prediction about the monolith it has half lost: the base models are general, not narrow. But as a recipe for a safe *deployment architecture* it is alive and perhaps more needed now than in 2019. From it follow concrete requirements: keep systems task-bounded and time-bounded where possible; do not give services a continuous goal and persistent memory without dire need; break the loops out of which autonomous agency is assembled; build capabilities as callable and switchable services rather than as independent doers. This is the program of "a tool by default; an agent by explicit and justified decision."

CAIS's practical value is also diagnostic. It gives a language for naming the dangerous movement: every time a service acquires an extended goal, its own memory, the right to fine-tune its successor itself or to launch other services with no human in the loop — we are moving out of Drexler's safe corner toward the sovereign. CAIS does not so much describe the future as mark out the scale along which we shift with every release, usually not in the direction it called for. And that makes it all the more useful to remember where zero stood on that scale.

Drexler, the father of the fear of gray goo, offered the most *reassuring* picture of superintelligence — an economy of transparent services instead of a god with a will. The market and the race chose the opposite. But the choice is not yet final: the boundary between tool and agent is drawn not by laws of nature but by engineering and political decisions. CAIS reminds us that this boundary can be drawn at all — and that every step toward a "more useful" autonomous agent is worth taking with open eyes, knowing its price.
