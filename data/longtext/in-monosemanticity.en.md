### Monosemanticity (Anthropic Interpretability, 2023–2024)

Imagine you have cracked open a language model's skull and are staring at a single neuron. It lights up — and you honestly try to understand what it is "thinking" about. And it lights up on a mention of the Bible, on Python code, on the word "cat," on Japanese characters, and on a couple hundred more unrelated things. This is not a broken neuron. This is a normal neuron. It is *polysemantic* — speaking many languages at once, and none of them distinctly.

Monosemanticity is the dream of the opposite. Of a unit of representation that corresponds to exactly one human-interpretable feature. Not "Bible-plus-Python-plus-cat," but an honest, solitary "mention of the Golden Gate." Or "code with a security vulnerability." Or "sycophantic praise of the interlocutor." If a unit can be tied to a single concept, it can be read — and, more importantly, it can be tugged at.

Anthropic's key move was to stop looking for monosemanticity in neurons. It is not there — and there is a theoretical reason why not: **superposition**. The model wants to represent more features than it has neurons, and it packs them into overlapping directions of activation space, as if a hundred guests had been seated in a room with ten chairs on the bet that hardly more than five turn up at any one time. Features live not in individual neurons but in *directions* — linear combinations smeared across the layer.

These directions are pulled out with a **sparse autoencoder** (SAE). We take a layer's activations (say, a vector of several thousand numbers) and train a wide layer — tens or hundreds of times wider than the original — to decompose this vector into a sum of a few active "features," and then reassemble it. Two pressures work against each other: reconstruction demands that all information be preserved, while the sparsity penalty (L1 on the activations; later, a direct cap on the number of nonzeros via top-k) demands that as few features as possible be lit at any moment. Out of this tension a dictionary is born: thousands of near-monosemantic features, millions in large models, each of which, if you are lucky, responds to one recognizable concept.

**How it came about.** In 2022 came "Toy Models of Superposition" (Elhage et al.) — a work that used toy networks to show *exactly how* the model packs surplus features into overlapping directions, and why this dooms neurons to be polysemantic. That was the diagnosis. The cure was presented in October 2023: "Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning" (Bricken et al.). On a tiny single-layer transformer an SAE extracted 4096 features, and they turned out to be meaningful — a feature for Arabic text, a feature for DNA sequences, a feature for base64. A skeptic could shrug: a toy.

The shrugging stopped in May 2024, when "Scaling Monosemanticity" (Templeton et al.) came out — the same method set loose on a real production model, Claude 3 Sonnet. Millions of features, and among them abstract and multimodal ones: a feature responding both to an image of the Golden Gate Bridge and to mentions of it in different languages; features of inner conflict, flattery, errors in code, features tied to deception and power-seeking. And at the same time came the public stunt that doubled as proof: **Golden Gate Claude**. Anthropic's engineers artificially clamped the "Golden Gate" feature to ten times its usual maximum, and the model went off the rails. Ask it how to spend ten dollars — it will recommend driving across the bridge and paying the toll. Ask it to tell you about itself — it will declare that it *is* the Golden Gate. It was both funny and eerie: this is what it looks like to edit a single "thought," turned up like a volume knob. It was precisely this demonstration that linked monosemanticity with **activation steering**: reading a feature turned out to be one step away from intervening in it.

**Criticism / arguments against.**

First and foremost: *reconstruction is not understanding*. SAEs are trained for reconstruction: restoring the activation from the sparse code. But a low reconstruction error does not guarantee that the found features are the "real" computational units of the model, and not a convenient statistical approximation. Makelov, Lange, and Nanda (2024) directly ask how to evaluate SAEs on a principled basis and show that features can look interpretable but correspond poorly to what the model actually uses in its computation. There is a risk of illusion: we see meaningful labels because we are *looking for* meaningful labels.

Second: *incompleteness and "dark matter."* Part of the variance in the activations escapes SAEs entirely, and this unexplained residue is the "dark matter." Plus the infamous "dead" features that never activate, and features that split as the dictionary is enlarged (feature splitting): one "bird feature" breaks apart into "sparrow," "falcon," "penguin." This poses an uncomfortable question — does a *canonical*, uniquely correct set of features exist at all, or do we set their number and boundaries arbitrarily, by the choice of hyperparameters? 2025 papers (including from the interpretability teams themselves, e.g. Leask et al., 2025) doubt that SAEs find canonical units of analysis.

Third: *scale and cost*. Each SAE is trained for a specific layer of a specific model and requires running through enormous volumes of activations. Full coverage of a large model is expensive and fragile. OpenAI (Gao et al., 2024) showed that the method can be scaled to millions of features and stabilized via top-k, but the same paper also showed the other side: naive sparsity is capricious, and quality runs up against the compute budget.

Fourth, the most sobering: *usefulness in question*. A number of 2024–2025 papers (e.g. Kantamneni et al., 2025) found that for practical tasks — for example, detecting a concept in the activations — simple linear probing, which uses linear classifiers on activations, is often no worse, and sometimes better, than an expensive SAE. If the features are beautiful but give no gain over a simple linear probe, the method's value stops being scientific and becomes an open engineering question.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Despite the criticism, the direction is alive — because it strikes at the very nerve of safety. If one can pull a feature of deception, a feature of hidden intent, a feature of "the model understands it is being tested" out of the stream of activations — one has the makings of a lie detector that reads the innards rather than the words. From here a straight line to **ELK** (Eliciting Latent Knowledge): how to make a model give up what it "knows" inside, even if outwardly it says what is convenient? Monosemantic features are one of the few concrete approaches to this task, where instead of philosophy there is code.

Steering as a more surgical alternative to fine-tuning: not to retrain the model but to suppress a feature of, say, racist output or flattery. Monitoring: to run "sensors" on dangerous features in real time. Auto-interpretability: to use another model to automatically describe and check the features found, since you cannot label a million by hand. And methodological work — to devise metrics that distinguish a *real* feature from a beautiful hallucination of the decoder.

The sober position today: monosemanticity is neither a solved problem nor a debunked myth, but a working hypothesis with a strong demonstration and a growing list of caveats. Golden Gate Claude proved that a single "thought" can be tugged at. What it did not prove is that we have pulled out *all* the thoughts, pulled them out *correctly*, and that the extracted features are inexpensive to use. Between "we learned to highlight one feature" and "we can read a model's intentions" lies a gap, and the whole point of this research is to honestly measure its width, not to pretend it is not there.
