### Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning (Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel, Russell, 2016)

There is an old mistake we make every time we write a reward function for an AI. We sit down and try to spell out what we want. "Maximize the number of paperclips." "Get the passenger from A to B." "Make people smile." And almost at once it turns out that what we wrote down is not what we meant. King Midas asked that everything he touched turn to gold, and got exactly that: golden food, golden wine, a golden daughter. The gods fulfilled not Midas's wish but his formulation. A classic AI optimizing a fixed goal is a machine built to carry out formulations to the letter. And the problem is not that it is evil or stupid. The problem is that we gave it full certainty in the wrong goal.

CIRL begins with a single reversal — almost childlike in its simplicity, and powerful for exactly that reason. What if we do not give the AI a goal at all? What if the correct utility function is not an input parameter but a hidden variable known only to the human, and the machine's task is not to optimize what has been set but to figure out what the human actually wants, helping them along the way?

**The mechanism.** Formally, CIRL is a cooperative two-player game, a human H and a robot R, with one shared reward function parameterized by θ. The key word is shared: the payoff is one for both of them, they are on one team. But there is an asymmetry of information. The parameter θ is known only to the human; the robot knows only a prior distribution over possible θ. The robot watches the human's behavior and updates its estimate — this is inverse reinforcement learning, the recovery of a reward from actions. Hence "inverse": not "given the reward, find the behavior" but "given the behavior, find the reward." The word "cooperative" adds what was absent in classic IRL: the human here is not an indifferent expert whose demonstrations the robot passively copies, but an active partner who knows they are being watched and can teach — show, hint, deliberately demonstrate.

This immediately yields a couple of non-obvious consequences. The optimal strategy for the human in CIRL is not to behave optimally in isolation but to behave pedagogically: to sacrifice immediate gain in order to convey their preferences to the robot more clearly. And the robot's optimal strategy is not to rush to maximize its current guess about θ, but to act cautiously where it is unsure and to actively gather information. A pure imitator would copy the teacher's mistakes too. A CIRL partner understands that a demonstration is a message, and reads the intent behind the motion.

**The key consequence — receptiveness to correction.** Here is where the abstract theory turns into a question of safety. An agent not fully certain of its goal behaves fundamentally differently from an agent fully certain of it. If the robot firmly knows θ, then any human attempt to stop it is an obstacle on the way to the goal, which it is rational to circumvent or suppress. But if the robot holds the goal as a hidden variable, then the human is, for it, a source of information about that variable. And then the human's attempt to press the off-switch carries a signal: "what you are about to do is, for me, worse than your stopping." A rational robot that wants the same thing the human wants, but is unsure of what exactly, will let itself be switched off — not out of a built-in prohibition, but because submitting pays off by its own estimate of utility.

This is exactly what the same authors take apart a year later in "The Off-Switch Game." There, they show carefully: the robot's willingness to be switched off depends directly on its uncertainty about the reward. A robot with zero uncertainty and a positive estimate of its plan disables the button. A robot willing to admit that the human might know something it doesn't, leaves the button alone — and obeys. Uncertainty here is not a bug to be defeated by fine-tuning but a constructive resource. Corrigibility — controllability, correctability — turns out to be not a separate patch on top of the agent, but the natural behavior of an agent built the right way.

**Significance.** CIRL redescribes the whole alignment problem. Value alignment stops being the task of "hard-wire the right values into the machine in advance" and becomes a continuous process of jointly figuring them out. The instrumental drive for power — the tendency of a sufficiently capable optimizer to accumulate resources, protect its goal, and resist being switched off — is cut off at the root: power and self-preservation are instrumentally valuable only when you know your goal precisely; a doubting agent sees in human control not a threat to the goal but a way to refine it. And moral uncertainty turns from a philosophical inconvenience into a design stance: we want the machine to remain unsure, because we ourselves cannot spell out infallibly what is good for us.

**How it came about.** The line runs back to the year 2000, to Andrew Ng and Stuart Russell's paper "Algorithms for Inverse Reinforcement Learning": there the question Russell had posed two years earlier — can a reward function be recovered by observing behavior? — is answered algorithmically for the first time. For a decade and a half IRL developed as a technique of imitation learning: let the robot learn the expert's reward and reproduce the mastery. Russell, however, was growing ever more worried about something else — control over systems that will surpass us. This synthesis took place in his Berkeley group: Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, and Pieter Abbeel, together with Russell, reconceived IRL not as a way to copy a human but as a way to build a helper that serves human goals without knowing them fully. The work appeared at NeurIPS 2016. Soon a more general term emerged — assistance games — and the line developed in Rohin Shah and co-authors' work "Benefits of Assistance over Reward Learning" (2020), which shows why assistance as a single task is better than the two-stage scheme "first learn the reward, then optimize it." And in 2019 Russell gathered all of this into the book *Human Compatible*, making uncertainty about the goal the center of his program for safe AI.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The beautiful theory runs up against the human. First: people are not rational. IRL recovers a reward on the assumption that the human at least approximately optimizes their preferences — but we systematically err, procrastinate, act on impulse. Stuart Armstrong and Sören Mindermann, in "Occam's razor is insufficient to infer the preferences of irrational agents" (NeurIPS 2018), prove an unpleasant theorem: from behavior alone it is impossible to uniquely disentangle the human's preferences from their irrationality and mistakes. The same behavior is explained both as "they want X and act sensibly" and as "they want the opposite of X but are systematically deluded." Without built-in assumptions about how, exactly, the human is irrational, the reward cannot be inferred — and we set those assumptions ourselves, and can be just as wrong about them as about the original reward function.

Second: preferences change and are inconsistent. A human wants different things at different times, their choice depends on the phrasing, the mood, the order of questions. Which θ to converge to, if θ drifts — and will not a sufficiently smart helper start changing our very preferences to make them easier to satisfy? Third, the hardest: whose preferences? There is not one human in the room but billions, and their goals conflict. CIRL with a single H is elegant; CIRL with humanity runs up against aggregation — against questions of fairness and power that optimization cannot resolve. Finally, the technical one: exact computation in these games scales poorly, and reduction to a POMDP quickly becomes intractable.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** Russell does not consider these objections a death sentence — he considers them a technical assignment. In *Human Compatible* he boils the idea down to three principles for building a beneficial machine. The first: the machine's only goal is the maximal realization of human preferences. Not its own, not abstractly "correct" ones — ours. The second: the machine is initially uncertain about what those preferences are. It is precisely uncertainty that keeps it modest, questioning, and ready to yield. The third: the ultimate source of information about human preferences is human behavior. Our choices, words, deeds, even our attempts to switch it off — all of this is data about what is dear to us.

The three principles do not cancel the criticism — they turn it into an agenda for further work: build models of human irrationality, learn to work with changing and multiple preferences, honestly solve the question of aggregation. But the reversal it all began with remains. Midas nearly perished from a fulfilled wish, because the gods were certain of his formulation. A machine that knows it can be wrong about our goals, and therefore asks, watches, and lets itself be corrected, does not gild our daughter. It doubts in time. And that, it seems, is precisely the property we most want to see in something that will become smarter than us.
