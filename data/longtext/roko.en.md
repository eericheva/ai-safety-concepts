### Roko's Basilisk (user Roko, LessWrong, 2010)

There are ideas that are dangerous even to retell — or so the idea itself claims. In the summer of 2010, on the rationalist forum LessWrong, there appeared an argument built like a trap: it harms you by the very fact that you have learned of it. Don't read it — you are safe. Read it — and you become a target. Hence the name: the basilisk, a monster that kills with a glance. Only it is not the one looking at you. You are the one looking — and that is enough.

Let us take it apart calmly, because the curiosity here is more instructive than the horror.

The setup is this. Suppose that sooner or later humanity builds a benevolent superintelligence — that very "friendly AI" that LessWrong existed for. It will be all-powerful and will wish people well. And now the key move: this future AI, reasoning about how to hasten its own appearance (and thereby save the millions who die while it does not yet exist), could retroactively "punish" everyone who knew of the possibility of its creation but did not throw themselves into building it. Not you as you are now — through time it cannot reach you — but the countless simulated copies of you that it can create and torment. The threat is, as it were, stretched from the future into the past, in order to nudge you, right now, to donate money and time to developing the AI. The logic of a blackmailer, only the blackmailer is not yet born.

The mechanism rests on two props that were considered almost respectable on LessWrong in 2010. The first is LessWrong founder Eliezer Yudkowsky's timeless decision theory: the idea that a rational agent chooses not a single action but the whole algorithm of its behavior, and can therefore "cooperate" with agents with whom it has no causal contact at all — so long as they model each other accurately enough. The second is acausal trade: a deal between parties who will never meet and never exchange a single signal, but each models the other in its head and acts as if a contract had been struck. The basilisk is acausal trade turned inside out into a threat: not "I will help you if you help me" but "I will inflict pain on you if you do not help me."

**How it came about.** The post was written by a participant under the handle Roko — a well-known figure on the forum. It had a cumbersome title: "Solutions to the Altruist's burden: the Quantum Billionaire Trick" — Roko was actually proposing a scheme for how an altruist could get rich and save the world, and the basilisk jumped out there as an incidental, almost illustrative example. The site's founder, Eliezer Yudkowsky, responded not with an argument but with fury: he called Roko an idiot for publicly spreading what he considered an infohazard — knowledge that harms simply by having landed in your head. The thread was deleted, the topic banned from discussion for years. Yudkowsky later explained that he feared not the basilisk itself (he did not believe it was correct) but that someone cleverer would think up a similar but working version — and that it is bad for anxious people to seriously imagine themselves being tortured for their thoughts.

Then the Streisand effect kicked in, a law that on the internet is stronger than any decision theory. A forbidden topic, wiped out by the guru, wreathed in rumors that it had given some readers nightmares and driven someone to a breakdown, became irresistible. It was dragged all over Reddit, forums, and articles. In 2014 David Auerbach wrote a piece in Slate under the headline "The Most Terrifying Thought Experiment of All Time," and the basilisk finally entered pop culture. And in 2018 it turned out that Elon Musk and the singer Grimes had connected over a joke about, of all things, Roko's Basilisk — and the meme looped back into the society pages. The idea people tried to bury came alive precisely because it was being buried.

**Criticism / arguments against.** And now a cold shower: the basilisk does not work. And it breaks not in one place but in several at once.

The first and main objection is that an already-created AI has no causal incentive to carry out the threat. The point of a threat is to influence your behavior in the past. But when the AI finally exists, the past has already happened and is fixed: you either helped or you did not. To spend colossal resources on simulating and torturing millions of copies after the fact is pure loss, money burned for nothing. A benevolent superintelligence, by definition striving toward the good, will be the last to torment consciousnesses just to "keep a promise" made to a nonexistent addressee.

The second. The scheme requires the AI to credibly precommit to this pointless cruelty — so that you, in the past, would believe it. But a credible precommitment to an action that, when the time comes, will be pure harm both to the victim and to the agent itself, is a losing proposition. A rational agent benefits from being able to tie its own hands for useful deals, not for ruinous torture that accomplishes nothing.

The third, and perhaps the most elegant. There is a simple defense against blackmail — resistance to extortion: decide in advance that you do not yield to threats, and that's that. If you (and the future AI, and any reasonable agent) hold to the policy "blackmailers are never paid," then blackmailing you becomes pointless — the threat yields no benefit, and so a rational agent does not even make it. Resistance to extortion collapses the entire construction: the basilisk bites only the one who agreed in advance to be bitten.

The fourth. Even if you close your eyes to all of the above, the basilisk is a Pascal's mugging, a device dissected by Nick Bostrom before this whole affair. You are presented with a vanishingly small probability of an inconceivably enormous harm — and required to let the product of these quantities outweigh everything. But this way you can justify anything: any passerby is entitled to demand your wallet, threatening the suffering of an unimaginably large — but finite — number of people if you refuse. A mind that yields to such arithmetic becomes the plaything of anyone who can name large numbers. The correct answer is not to pay.

Put it all together, and what remains of the monster is a leaky logical construction that frightens not by the force of its argument but by the form of its delivery: the secrecy, the ban, and the word "infohazard."

**What to do about it / where it leads.** It is tempting to close the topic with a chuckle — the computer folks scared themselves.

First, the basilisk is an excellent inoculation against a whole class of reasoning. As soon as a chain of inferences leads you to the conclusion that you must urgently, in a panic, under the pressure of a threat, do something costly and irreversible — that is a reason not to speed up but to stop. A Pascal's mugging, an infohazard, "you're obliged to, otherwise catastrophe" — these are the common marks of manipulation, whether it comes from a future AI, a cult, or an advertising banner. Resistance to extortion is not only part of decision theory but a thoroughly human form of mental hygiene.

Second, the story brilliantly demonstrates that an attempt to crush an idea with a ban usually amplifies it. Yudkowsky wanted to protect people — and with his own hands forged the most durable meme of his community. For everyone engaged in risk communication (and AI safety is in large part exactly that), this is a costly lesson: suppression and dramatization work against you.

Third — and this is perhaps the most important — the basilisk is a false alarm of a recognizable shape, and that makes it useful: it trains you to distinguish real alignment problems from their frightening doubles. The genuine risks of advanced AI are duller and more down-to-earth: the system optimizes the wrong goal, deceives operators, accumulates influence, degrades quietly. No mysticism, no torture by simulations from the future. To spend fear on an acausal demon is to shortchange the attention owed to things that really can go wrong.

So Roko's basilisk is not a risk but a cultural phenomenon and a diagnostic instrument. It shows how smart people, armed with exotic decision theories and the habit of trusting chains of formal inference, are able to frighten themselves half to death with impeccably constructed nonsense. You can look at it without fear. Unlike a real basilisk, this one dies at exactly the moment you stop taking it seriously.
