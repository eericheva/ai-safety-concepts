### The Turing Test / the imitation game (Alan Turing, 1950)

Imagine a room that contains nothing but a teletype. You are the judge. On the other side of the wire are two participants: one a human, the other a machine. You cannot see them, cannot hear them, do not know which is which. All you have is text — the questions you type and the answers that come back. Your task is simple and treacherous at once: to determine who is who. It is the machine, naturally, that is trying to confuse you — but each of them will insist that they are the human.

If after a long conversation you cannot tell the machine from the human more reliably than by the toss of a coin — the machine has won. Turing called it the *imitation game* and built around it what is perhaps the most cited and most misunderstood thought experiment in the history of computing.

**What Turing actually proposed.** The 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" opens with a sentence worth rereading slowly: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" And at once Turing makes a move for which we ought to love him — he declares this question too meaningless to deserve discussion. The words "machine" and "think," he says, are defined by how they are ordinarily used, and ordinary usage leads straight to settling the matter by a Gallup poll of public opinion, which is absurd. Therefore the question is not to be answered — it is to be replaced.

The replacement is operational. In place of elusive "thinking," which lives somewhere inside the skull and is inaccessible to observation, Turing proposes externally verifiable behavior: is the machine able, in a textual dialogue, to pass for a human before an attentive judge? This is a deliberate, almost surgical reduction of mind to what can be observed from the outside — to the indistinguishability of verbal behavior. No peering into the soul, no introspection, no metaphysics. There is a transcript — and there is a verdict.

The genius of the move is that the textual channel cuts away everything inessential. It does not matter that the machine has no face, no voice, no body, that it is made of silicon and not of meat. The judge does not see the interlocutor — and so cannot award it points for a human appearance or dock it for a metallic timbre. What remains is the pure ability to hold a conversation. Turing, in essence, proposed to treat language as sufficient evidence of mind — and thereby set the frame within which we argue to this day.

**How it came about.** By 1950 Alan Turing was no longer merely a mathematician. Behind him lay the 1936 work on computable numbers, where he devised the abstract machine that became the foundation of all of computer science. Behind him lay Bletchley Park, the breaking of the Enigma, a war won in part by the efforts of his codebreaking machines. Few people on the planet knew as much about computation as he did, and he knew it through hands-on work, not just theory.

The paper appeared in the philosophy journal *Mind*: Turing addressed not engineers but philosophers, and built his defense in advance. Perhaps the larger part of the text is a methodical treatment of nine objections that might be raised against him: the theological ("thinking is a function of the immortal soul"), the mathematical (Gödel's theorems), the objection from consciousness, the "Lady Lovelace" objection (a machine can create nothing new), and others. Turing was optimistic almost to the point of naivety: he predicted that by the year 2000 machines would fool the average judge in a five-minute conversation at least 30% of the time. He got the exact figure wrong, but he got the direction right half a century ahead of time.

**Criticism and arguments against.** The Turing test lived a long life in large part because of how fiercely it was attacked.

The main blow was struck by the philosopher John Searle in 1980 — with his famous thought experiment, the "Chinese Room." Imagine, says Searle, that I, not knowing a word of Chinese, am locked in a room with a thick rulebook in English. Through a slot I am handed cards with Chinese characters. I consult the rulebook to find which characters to hand back in reply, and push them out again. From outside it seems the room speaks fluent Chinese — and might well pass the Turing test. But inside, I understand nothing at all. I manipulate symbols by formal rules, with not the slightest access to their meaning. Searle's conclusion is merciless: syntax is not semantics. Correct answers can be produced without any understanding, and so the Turing test measures imitation, not comprehension.

The second long-standing argument came from history itself — from the program ELIZA. In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum wrote a tiny script parodying a Rogerian psychotherapist. Its whole trick amounted to turning the user's remarks around: say "my mother annoys me" and you get "tell me more about your mother." No understanding, just simple pattern-matching over a list of a few dozen keywords. And yet people — including, to Weizenbaum's horror, his own secretary — became genuinely attached to the program, poured out their souls to it, asked to be left alone with the "doctor." Thus was born the phenomenon later named "the ELIZA effect": the persistent human tendency to ascribe understanding, sympathy, and an inner life to a machine on the basis of negligible external cues. This is a painful discovery for the Turing test. If people are so easily fooled by a trivial program, then perhaps the test measures not the machine's intelligence but the judge's credulity.

And finally, the general philosophical upshot: passing the test ≠ mind. Indistinguishability of behavior is evidence, but not proof. A duck that quacks and swims may turn out to be a very good mechanism. Turing, to his credit, partly acknowledged this — he did not claim the test defines thinking; he proposed to replace an unfit question with it. But the temptation to read the test as a criterion of mind proved too strong, and almost all subsequent criticism strikes precisely at that reading.

**Legacy and connections, relevance in the age of LLMs.** The Turing test acquired a family. The "Chinese Room" is its chief counterexample, its eternal opponent. The ELIZA effect is its dark twin, a reminder that the judge is easier to fool than it seems. There are also attempts to fix the test itself: the Lovelace test, proposed in 2001 (Bringsjord, Bello, Ferrucci), requires not passing for a human but creating something genuinely new — something the machine's creators did not build in and cannot explain. And nearby looms the "uncanny valley" — the same anxiety of indistinguishability, only about bodies and faces rather than text: the closer the imitation comes to a human, the sharper our unease at the slightest mismatch.

And then came the age of large language models — and the old thought experiment turned from a philosophical amusement into a news item. In 2025 researchers Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen of the University of California, San Diego, conducted a rigorous controlled test and showed that GPT-4.5, with the right prompt, was taken for the human more often than the real human was: judges picked it as the human in roughly three games out of four. By Turing's formal criterion the machine passed. The game is won.

Only the victory proved surprisingly empty. Not one serious person exclaimed: "So GPT thinks!" On the contrary — the dispute over understanding only sharpened. It turned out that we had always tacitly held the Turing test to be sufficient precisely because we were sure that no machine would pass it until it had truly grown smart. As soon as machines learned to pass it without growing smart in the sense that interests us, the test laid bare its own limitation. All this time it had been measuring exactly what Turing promised — the indistinguishability of verbal behavior. We had merely hoped that this was the same thing as mind.

Turing gave us not a criterion of thinking — he gave us a mirror. And when machines at last came right up to it, in the reflection we saw not so much them as the blurriness of our own concepts: what, exactly, do we mean when we say "understand," "think," "be someone." The test did not answer the question — it showed how badly the question is posed. For 1950, astonishing foresight on Turing's part.
