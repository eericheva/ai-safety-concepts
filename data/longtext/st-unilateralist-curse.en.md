### The Unilateralist's Curse (Bostrom, Douglas, Sandberg, 2016)

A village, ringed by an ancient, half-abandoned forest. In the forest stands an old dam, rumored to be about to burst. Ten of the villagers each have an axe, and any one of them can go alone, asking no one's leave, and bring down its remains — so that the water drains off in a controlled way, before it is too late. Nine of them, having thought it over, decide: no, the risk of flooding the lower fields is too great, better to wait for the engineers. The tenth is certain that delay is deadly dangerous. And they go with their axe.

The dam falls. Nine votes against one did not make the decision — one vote in favor did. The majority was against, but the majority here means nothing: for the action to happen, it is enough that at least one person deems it good. This is the unilateralist's curse — a systematic bias toward excessive action that arises whenever many independent actors are each able, single-handedly, to do something that affects everyone.

The mechanism of the curse is vexingly simple, almost arithmetical. Take a group of well-meaning, honest people, each of whom sincerely tries to estimate whether an action will bring benefit or harm. Their estimates diverge — someone errs on the high side, someone on the low, as always happens when judging something uncertain. If the group as a whole decided — by vote, by consensus, by averaging — the wisdom of the crowd would kick in: the individual errors would cancel one another out, and the outcome would be close to the true value of the action. But under a unilateral structure it is neither the median nor the mean that decides. The maximum decides — the most optimistic, the most reckless, the one who overshot furthest in the direction of "let's do it." The threshold for the whole group is set not by its typical member but by its most extreme one. And the more independent actors there are in the group, the higher the probability that at least one will turn out optimistic enough to pull the trigger.

The key word here is "single-handedly." The curse lives precisely where the action is one-sided: where no one's consent is needed, where no one can undo another's move, where one launch, one publication, one release irreversibly changes the world for everyone. The symmetric situation — the curse of inaction — also exists (when an important step requires unanimity and a single pessimist blocks it all), but in matters where the stakes include irreversible harm to all of humanity, it is precisely the bias toward action that is more dangerous.

**How it came about.** Nick Bostrom, Thomas Douglas, and Anders Sandberg first presented the idea in a working paper of the Future of Humanity Institute at Oxford around 2013, and the final version — the article "The Unilateralist's Curse and the Case for a Principle of Conformity" — appeared in the journal *Social Epistemology* in 2016. It grew out of very concrete bioethical anxieties: the debates over whether to publish a method for enhancing the transmissibility of the H5N1 virus (the notorious gain-of-function research of the early 2010s), and whether to publish the work on synthesizing poliovirus from scratch. In each such case there are dozens of laboratories around the world, and it takes only one to publish — and the genie is out of the bottle forever. The authors noticed that this is not a quirk peculiar to biology but a general structural trap: it manifests wherever the capacity for irreversible one-sided action is dispersed. The term "curse" is a deliberate echo of the "winner's curse" from auction theory, where the auction is won by the one who most overestimated the value of the lot.

**Criticism / arguments against.** The cure the authors propose — a "principle of conformity": do not act single-handedly on your private estimate, but shift it toward the group's estimate, as though your judgment were merely one among many — provokes legitimate resistance. For the history of progress is in large part written precisely by nonconformists: the lone individual who went against the unanimous opinion of their colleagues turned out to be right — Galileo, Semmelweis, dissidents of every stripe. If every pioneer meekly averaged their judgment with the crowd's, we would be stuck. The unilateralist's curse and the "conformist's curse" are mirror images, and in crushing the first it is easy to awaken the second: to kill a useful bold action whose value the majority failed to recognize.

The second difficulty is epistemic. To shift your estimate toward the group's, you have to know that group estimate, and it is precisely this that is usually missing — the actors are scattered, do not communicate, do not trust one another, or are directly competing. How do you factor in an opinion you have never even heard? And how do you tell "I am a reckless optimist who overshot on the high side" from "I am the only one who possesses the correct information"? From the inside both states feel the same — like the certainty of being right. The authors spell out the limits: the model assumes that the actors are genuinely well-meaning and that their errors are symmetric and independent; irreversibility sharpens the problem but is not a necessary condition for it. And the main objection to their principle is one they formulate themselves: if there is already a bias against unilateral action in the environment, conformity will only exacerbate it. Allowing for the curse is not a universal ban on acting but a correction that must be applied consciously and according to the situation.

**What to do about it / where it leads.** The practical conclusion is not to force everyone into silence, but rather, where irreversible power is dispersed, to change the very structure of decision-making — to turn a multitude of single triggers into one common safety catch. A softened principle of conformity: before acting single-handedly, ask yourself why the others who could have acted did not; perhaps they see what you do not. Deliberation and the exchange of estimates: if the actors talk and pool their judgments honestly, the distributed decision defeats the lone optimist. Mechanisms of veto and moratorium: agree that an irreversible step is taken not when one person is for it, but when too few people oppose it. And the transfer of the decision to a higher level — to consortia, standards, regulators — so that it becomes impossible to "fire" single-handedly.

For AI safety the unilateralist's curse is one of the most cited motifs, and it is clear why. The open publication of the weights of a dangerous model is exactly a unilateral irreversible action: it takes only one laboratory deciding that "this is safe and useful for everyone" — and there is no rolling it back. The disclosure of a dangerous capability, an exploit, a method — the same thing. The race for the lead, in which one team, the most optimistic about its own readiness, rushes to release a system first, is a direct embodiment of the curse: the threshold is set by the most reckless participant in the race, not the most cautious. Hence the ideas of voluntary moratoria, joint commitments among labs, coordinating bodies, practices of responsible disclosure and "staged release." The concept naturally joins with Bostrom's vulnerable world hypothesis (if a "black ball" exists — a technology that ruins civilization — then a unilateral structure all but guarantees that someone will draw it), with infohazards, with differential technological development, and with the general problem of coordination. The moral of the bestiary is simple and uncomfortable: when anyone can press the button on everyone's behalf, what matters is not only how certain you are that you are right — but that the most careless of your colleagues feels exactly the same way.
