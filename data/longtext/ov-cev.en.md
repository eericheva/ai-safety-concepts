### Coherent Extrapolated Volition (Eliezer Yudkowsky, 2004)

In the first scene of *King Lear* the old monarch makes the mistake that will bring everything down. He divides the kingdom and asks his three daughters to measure their love aloud. Goneril and Regan flatter for show; Cordelia, who loves truly, refuses to trade in words — and is left with nothing. Lear got exactly what he asked for: a beautiful formulation. And lost exactly what he wanted: the daughter who loves him. Between the spoken wish and the implied wish, a chasm opened, and into that chasm the whole realm fell.

This is the alignment problem in miniature — four centuries before it had a name. Imagine you have built a genie of incredible power and must tell it what you want. The problem is not that the genie is evil. The problem is that you are human: your wishes are contradictory, half-formulated, founded on factual errors, mixed with a moment's fatigue and childhood grievances. To tell the genie what you want right now is to hand it your confusion, amplified to planetary scale.

Yudkowsky proposed that we not make today's wish at all. Let the machine carry out not what we want this second, but what we would want — in the famous formula — "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together." Not current preferences, but their idealized, "grown wiser" and coherent version. This is coherent extrapolated volition, CEV.

**The mechanism.** CEV is not a list of values but a procedure for finding them; Yudkowsky called it an "initial dynamic," a seed process rather than a fixed goal. The idea unfolds in four movements of extrapolation. *If we knew more* — correct factual errors: if I want to drink from the glass, not knowing there is poison in it, my true volition is not to drink. *If we thought faster* — give the wish more reflection than we can afford in real time. *If we were the people we wish to be* — pull not toward the first impulse that comes along but toward our own second-order desires, toward the "self" we ourselves approve of. *If we had grown up farther together* — account for the moral growth that happens when people argue, listen, and change.

The extrapolated volitions of many people converge in some places and diverge in others. The dynamic must act only where volition is coherent — where the idealized versions of us agree with one another — and stay silent where opinions fan out. The original text introduces a technical vocabulary for this: "spread" — a measure of the volition's unpredictability; "muddle," a measure of self-contradiction; and "distance," a measure of how hard it would be to explain the extrapolated volition to your present self. Where the spread is large, the system does not impose a decision but leaves the space empty — so that humanity may write the answer itself. And, finally, the loop of recursion: volition is extrapolated the way we ourselves would want it extrapolated — the process has the right to rewrite its own rules. This is not a god dictating good, but a mirror polished to give as sharp a reflection as we ourselves wish for.

**How it came about.** The document "Coherent Extrapolated Volition" came out in 2004 under the aegis of the Singularity Institute for Artificial Intelligence — the organization that would later grow into MIRI. It continued the line of Yudkowsky's earlier work on "Friendly AI," in particular "Creating Friendly AI" of 2001, but was a direct answer to the dead end that every earlier attempt to write down what a Friendly AI should value kept running into. The dead end would later be called the fragility of value. Yudkowsky showed, time after time, that human value is not one big simple thing but a huge, finely fitted tangle, where the loss of a single element (boredom, novelty, the right to err, the very presence of consciousness) turns a utopia into a nightmare. So values cannot be written into code by hand — you will forget something important, and not notice until it is too late. CEV was an attempt to get around the fragility of value not by heroic enumeration but by delegation — to hand the work of extracting values to the superintelligence itself, but under a strict charter.

**Criticism / arguments against.** So many objections have accumulated that CEV is better read not as a project but as a well-formulated question.

*The procedure is undefined.* "What you would want if you knew more" is a beautiful phrase, but not an algorithm. How do you compute an idealized version of a human? What does "think faster" mean without distorting the personality? Between the poetic formula and working code lies the whole unsolved philosophy of consciousness and value at once. CEV points a finger at the goal but sets out not a single step toward it.

*Whether a single convergent volition exists.* The whole construction rests on the assumption that, given enough extrapolation, people will be more likely to agree than to diverge. But this is precisely an assumption. It is entirely possible that the idealized versions of a Buddhist monk, a Silicon Valley libertarian, and a medieval peasant diverge the more sharply the longer you "grow them together." If the volition of humanity is not coherent even in the limit, then the mechanism is bound by construction to stay silent, and all that is left of the grand design is an empty screen.

*Whose volition, exactly.* All of humanity — but with what weight? Do you count the already dead, the not yet born, animals, future digital consciousnesses? Someone has to draw the boundary of the circle of those whose volition gets extrapolated — and that decision is itself deeply value-laden and cannot be derived from the procedure it launches. The circle closes: to learn our values, one must first make a value decision about whose values to count. And, of course, whoever writes the initial dynamic sets the frame — which means the question of the concentration of power never goes away.

*CEV as a later target.* In the 2004 document, Yudkowsky joked that it would not go dreadfully out of date before late June, then added in an update that it took two days. He also said that the text was still close to the mark. More modest variants grew up around the idea — Ben Goertzel's "coherent blended volition," for one — while Yudkowsky himself shifted to far more pragmatic and pessimistic topics: corrigibility, a bounded AI under control, the task of "not killing everyone at once." CEV remained his ideal target for a later AI, rather than something to attempt with the first AI ever built. For the first AI, he suggested a bounded, task-directed system.

**What to do about it / legacy.** There is still no practical way to build CEV, and there never was — and yet its footprint is enormous. It became the canonical example of what Nick Bostrom in *Superintelligence* called indirect normativity: not setting the machine's values directly, but setting a process that will find them. The logic of "we do not know the right answer, but we can describe a procedure more reliable than we are" runs through the entire modern agenda — from the "moral uncertainty" of MacAskill, Bykvist, and Ord, with their parliamentary models for aggregating ethical theories, to disputes over whose utility function an AI should optimize at all.

There is a direct technical line of inheritance too. Stuart Russell in *Human Compatible* turned the task on its head: the agent should not know the goal in advance; it should be uncertain about human preferences and learn them from behavior (assistance games, CIRL). This is Lear grown wiser by four acts: not "declare how you love me" but "I will watch and stay ready to discover I understood you wrong." Today's methods — RLHF, Constitutional AI — can be read as crude, down-to-earth descendants of CEV: they too aggregate human preferences, only not the extrapolated ones but the raw ones, taken from tired labelers here and now. It is exactly the gap that CEV tried to close — between the momentary "I like it" and what we would approve of on sober reflection — that remains the open nerve of all scalable oversight. And new attempts to attach social-choice theory and machine deliberation to alignment are, in essence, the same old dream: to learn to hear not what we shout but what we meant.

CEV was not built and probably will not be, in its original form. But it did the main thing a good bestiary beast should do — it gave us the right fear and the right hope. The fear: we will almost certainly ask for the wrong thing if we ask directly. The hope: perhaps, somewhere beneath the layer of our confusion, there really does hide the self we would wish to be — and it is worth building a mirror capable of showing it.
